# Family, culture and the translation of care: cross-cultural psychology of parent–child and intergenerational care

Conventions used throughout. Grades: **[E]** Established (replicated across meta-analyses or several strong designs); **[M]** Moderate or mixed; **[P]** Preliminary (single or small study, cross-sectional, or unreplicated); **[T]** Theoretical proposition; **[SPEC]** speculative extrapolation by me, not tested. "WEIRD-only" means every sample came from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic societies. "Causal ID" rates identification: none (cross-sectional correlation), weak (prospective observational), moderate (cross-lagged panel, fixed effects, quasi-experiment with caveats), strong (randomized trial or clean natural experiment). Every DOI below was checked against Crossref, OpenAlex and/or doi.org in November 2026 unless marked UNVERIFIED. Q15 lists the corrections to the candidate leads. Korea appears only where it is part of a cross-national study or general theory; Korea-specific detail is deferred to the Korea researcher. Couple-level processes, informant discrepancies, attachment foundations, coercion/psychological control and formal modelling are other researchers' scope and are touched only where needed.

## Q1. Parental acceptance–rejection theory (PARTheory/IPARTheory): is perceived acceptance pan-culturally linked to adjustment, and what do children appear to need in order to feel accepted?

### Takeaway
Children's *perceived* parental acceptance correlates with better self-reported adjustment in every country studied. The correlations are large in the theory group's meta-analyses (r = .51 for children, r = .46 for adults in 2002) and small but nearly universal in prospective nine-country data. But the pan-cultural evidence is mostly cross-sectional, self-reported, single-informant and mono-method (PARQ plus PAQ). Much of it comes from the theory's own research group, and longitudinal cross-national studies find child-to-parent effects at least as often as parent-to-child effects. What is universal is the link between *perceived* acceptance and adjustment, the same four perceived behaviour classes everywhere (warmth, hostility, indifference/neglect, undifferentiated rejection). This fits the premise that "love perceived/received" is what counts. It does not show that parents' intended love is often misperceived.

### Cited Findings
**Meta-analyses (all from the IPARTheory group; all use PARQ + PAQ)**
- Khaleque & Rohner (2002), "Perceived parental acceptance-rejection and psychological adjustment: A meta-analysis of cross-cultural and intracultural studies," *Journal of Marriage and Family* 64(1):54–64.
  - Design: 43 studies worldwide, using child and adult PARQ and PAQ. Mean weighted r = .51 for children and r = .46 for adults; the predicted association appeared in every included study.
  - Fail-safe N was 3,433 (children) and 941 (adults); no modern publication-bias correction.
  - Measured: the correlation between two questionnaires completed by the same respondent. Authors inferred a universal effect of acceptance on adjustment.
  - Causal ID: none. Grade: [E] for the association; [P] for any causal reading.
  - [doi:10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x)
- Khaleque & Rohner (2012; online 2011), *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology* 43(5):784–800.
  - Design: 66 studies, 19,511 respondents, 22 countries on five continents. Maternal and paternal acceptance correlated significantly with adjustment in all cultures.
  - The paternal-acceptance correlation was significantly stronger than the maternal one for children (not for adults). Effect sizes are not in the abstract.
  - Grade: [E] association. [doi:10.1177/0022022111406120](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022111406120)
- Ali, Khaleque & Rohner (2015), *JCCP* 46(8):1059–1080.
  - Design: 220 studies, 33,081 respondents, 23 nations.
  - Among children there were no gender differences.
  - Remembered maternal acceptance related more strongly to adult sons' adjustment than to adult daughters'. Adult daughters' remembered *paternal* acceptance related more strongly to their adjustment than their remembered maternal acceptance did. These are retrospective adult reports.
  - Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1177/0022022115597754](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022115597754)
- Khaleque & Ali (2017), *Journal of Family Theory & Review* 9(4):441–458. Overview of 12 IPARTheory meta-analyses: 551 studies (48% unpublished), 149,440 respondents, 31 countries, 1975–2016. It concludes the associations are pan-cultural and the measures reliable; note that authorship overlaps across the primary studies, the meta-analyses and the review. [doi:10.1111/jftr.12228](https://doi.org/10.1111/jftr.12228)
- Khaleque (2013; online 2012), meta-analysis of perceived parental warmth with adjustment and personality dispositions, *Journal of Child and Family Studies* 22(2):297–306. Title verified; effect sizes not retrieved. [doi:10.1007/s10826-012-9579-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-012-9579-z)
- Khaleque & Rohner (2002b), reliability meta-analysis of 51 studies, *JCCP* 33(1):87–99.
  - The PARQ is reliable among US ethnic groups and internationally. The PAQ is reliable among US ethnic groups, but evidence for its cross-cultural reliability is insufficient.
  - [doi:10.1177/0022022102033001006](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022102033001006)

**Theory statements**
- Rohner, Khaleque & Cournoyer (2005), *Ethos* 33(3):299–334.
  - Claims nearly 2,000 studies show children everywhere need acceptance, and that rejected children worldwide self-report a specific form of maladjustment.
  - Children and adults organize their perceptions around four classes of behaviour: warmth/affection (or coldness), hostility/aggression, indifference/neglect, undifferentiated rejection.
  - Grade: [T], with correlational support. [doi:10.1525/eth.2005.33.3.299](https://doi.org/10.1525/eth.2005.33.3.299)
- Rohner & Lansford (2017), *JFTR* 9(4):426–440. Restatement as interpersonal acceptance–rejection theory: acceptance and rejection predict adjustment across cultures and sociodemographic groups. [doi:10.1111/jftr.12219](https://doi.org/10.1111/jftr.12219)

**Prospective cross-national tests (Parenting Across Cultures project; multi-informant; partly independent of the PARQ-only design)**
- Putnick et al. (2015; online 2014), *Journal of Child Psychology and Psychiatry* 56(8):923–932.
  - Design: 1,247 families in China, Colombia, Italy, Jordan, Kenya, the Philippines, Sweden, Thailand and the US.
  - Children's perceived rejection predicted *increases* in internalizing and externalizing problems and *decreases* in school performance and prosocial behaviour over 3 years. Controls: within-wave relations, stability, parental age and education, and social desirability.
  - Patterns were similar for mothers and fathers and, with few exceptions, across all nine countries. The authors describe the effects as small but nearly universal.
  - Causal ID: weak to moderate. Grade: [E/M]. [doi:10.1111/jcpp.12366](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12366)
- Putnick et al. (2018), *Developmental Psychology* 54(10):1881–1890.
  - Design: 1,178 families, 9 countries, children aged 9–12.
  - Acceptance predicted later increases in prosocial behaviour; prosociality at age 9 also predicted increased acceptance from 9 to 10.
  - Paths were invariant across countries, mothers and fathers, and girls and boys. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1037/dev0000565](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000565)
- Rothenberg et al. (2020; online 2019), *Development and Psychopathology* 32(3):1113–1137.
  - Design: N = 1,315, 12 cultural groups in 9 countries, ages 8–13.
  - Child-driven effects of externalizing and internalizing on parental warmth and control were pervasive. Parent effects were rarer; father-based parent effects were more frequent than mother effects.
  - Most effects held across cultures. [doi:10.1017/s0954579419001214](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0954579419001214)
- Lansford et al. (2018), *Journal of Research on Adolescence* 28(3):571–590. N = 1,298. Child effects described the links between warmth or control and child behaviour better than parent effects did; parent effects were more typical of childhood than of early adolescence. [doi:10.1111/jora.12381](https://doi.org/10.1111/jora.12381)
- Deater-Deckard et al. (2011), *Journal of Family Psychology* 25(5):790–794. 1,421 children aged 7–10 in 13 cultural groups across 9 countries. More warmth went with more control overall, but the strength of this association differed greatly between cultural groups. [doi:10.1037/a0025120](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025120)
- Pinquart (2017), *Developmental Psychology* 53(5):873–932. Meta-analysis of 1,435 studies: warmth had very small to small negative associations with externalizing, and the warmth–externalizing association was bidirectional over time. [doi:10.1037/dev0000295](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000295)

**Measurement invariance of the PARQ**
- Gomez & Rohner (2011), *Cross-Cultural Research* 45(3):267–285.
  - The Adult PARQ (60 items, four scales) four-factor model was supported.
  - All mother-version items and all but six father-version items were invariant across 314 Australian and 509 US adults. Both groups are WEIRD.
  - Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1177/1069397111403111](https://doi.org/10.1177/1069397111403111)
- Senese et al. (2016), *Parenting* 16(4):219–236. Italian vs American Adult PARQ short forms; title verified, results not retrieved. [doi:10.1080/15295192.2016.1180943](https://doi.org/10.1080/15295192.2016.1180943)

**Needs beyond acceptance (brief; foundations are covered by another researcher)**
- Fischer & Schwartz (2011) found that values tied to autonomy, relatedness and competence are rated as highly important, with high agreement, in all countries studied. [doi:10.1177/0022022110381429](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022110381429)
- Chen et al. (2015; online 2014), *Motivation and Emotion* 39(2):216–236: basic psychological need satisfaction and frustration across four cultures. Title verified; results not retrieved here. [doi:10.1007/s11031-014-9450-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-014-9450-1)

### Inferences
- The defensible pan-cultural claim is narrow. Across roughly 30 countries, children who perceive warmth, and who do not perceive hostility, neglect or undifferentiated rejection, report better adjustment. That is what children across cultures appear to need in order to feel accepted.
  - The predictor is perception, not parental intent. PARTheory is therefore compatible with the translation premise that perceived/received love is what predicts outcomes.
  - It is equally compatible with a simpler "amount and quality of warmth" account.
- Correlations near r = .5 between two self-reports from the same person are probably inflated by shared-method variance, mood and response style. The prospective "small" PAC effects are a better guide to magnitude.
- Child effects are a serious alternative explanation (Rothenberg 2020; Lansford 2018; Pinquart 2017). A distressed child may both elicit and perceive less warmth, so "low perceived love" can be a consequence of problems rather than a cause.
- Ali et al. (2015) bears directly on the father–daughter example: adult daughters' recalled paternal acceptance is a comparatively strong correlate of their adjustment. But it rests on retrospective self-report and cannot separate recall bias from effects. [P]

### Gaps
- No independent meta-analysis (outside the Rohner group) of PARQ studies was found. Publication bias was assessed only with fail-safe N, an outdated method, in the 2002 meta-analysis.
- Effect sizes for Khaleque & Rohner (2012) and Ali et al. (2015) were not retrieved; their abstracts omit them.
- PARQ invariance between Western and East Asian samples, including the Korean-language PARQ, was not found. Gomez & Rohner tested only the US against Australia.
- No cross-cultural study was found that simultaneously measures the parent's intended acceptance, observed parental behaviour and the child's perceived acceptance. That is the core test the translation hypothesis needs.

## Q2. Does financial or instrumental provision matter causally for children, even when it is not perceived as love?

### Takeaway
Yes, but conditionally. Natural experiments and a systematic review of causal designs show that household income improves children's behavioural, educational and health outcomes, most clearly at the bottom of the income distribution, partly through parenting and family relationships.

The strongest disconfirming evidence for a simple "provision is love whether noticed or not" claim:
- The largest US randomized trial (Baby's First Years: \$333 vs \$20 a month from birth) found null effects on child development at ages 1–4, and no improvement in maternal mental health or family processes.
- Nonresident-father meta-analyses disagree about child support: positive in 1999, null in 2013. Relational involvement and closeness predict child well-being in both.
- In a four-country adolescent survey, perceived financial support was unrelated to distress, while perceived emotional support predicted lower distress in every country.

Taken together, provision is real care with real effects in poverty, but those effects are modest, depend on baseline need and dose, and are less tied to children's emotional well-being.

### Cited Findings
**Nonresident fathers (observational meta-analyses)**
- Amato & Gilbreth (1999), *Journal of Marriage and the Family* 61(3):557–573 (some sources give 557–575).
  - Design: 63 studies. Fathers' payment of child support was positively associated with children's well-being; frequency of contact was generally unrelated to child outcomes.
  - Feelings of closeness and authoritative parenting were positively associated with academic success and negatively with externalizing and internalizing problems.
  - Effect sizes not retrieved. Causal ID: none to weak (fathers who pay may differ). Grade: [M]. [doi:10.2307/353560](https://doi.org/10.2307/353560)
- Adamsons & Johnson (2013), *Journal of Family Psychology* 27(4):589–599.
  - Design: 52 studies. Positive forms of involvement were associated with child well-being, with a small but statistically significant effect.
  - Neither the amount of father–child contact nor financial provision was associated with child well-being.
  - The strongest correlates were involvement in child-related activities, positive father–child relationships and engaging in multiple forms of involvement.
  - Grade: [M]; it contradicts the 1999 child-support finding. [doi:10.1037/a0033786](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0033786)
- Kane, Nelson & Edin (2015), *Journal of Marriage and Family* 77(3):591–611.
  - Design: repeated semistructured interviews with nearly 400 low-income noncustodial US fathers.
  - In-kind (non-cash) support made up about a quarter of total support, averaging about \$60 a month in goods when given.
  - Qualitative analysis: fathers described their reasons for giving in-kind support mainly in relational terms. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1111/jomf.12188](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12188)

**Family stress model**
- Conger, Conger & Martin (2010), *JMF* 72(3):685–704. Review: SES relates to couple stability, parent–child relationship quality and child outcomes. The evidence supports an interactionist model that combines social causation and social selection. Grade: [M/T]. [doi:10.1111/j.1741-3737.2010.00725.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2010.00725.x)
- Masarik & Conger (2017; online 2016), "Stress and child development: a review of the Family Stress Model," *Current Opinion in Psychology* 13:85–90. Title verified. [doi:10.1016/j.copsyc.2016.05.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2016.05.008)

**Quasi-experiments**
- Costello, Compton, Keeler & Angold (2003), *JAMA* 290(15):2023–2029.
  - Design: 1,420 rural children aged 9–13, assessed annually from 1993 to 2000; one quarter were American Indian. A casino income supplement to every American Indian moved 14% of study families out of poverty.
  - Before the casino, persistently poor and ex-poor children had more psychiatric symptoms (4.38 and 4.28) than never-poor children (2.75). Afterwards, ex-poor children's symptoms fell to never-poor levels while persistently poor children's stayed high (vs never poor: persistently poor OR 1.50, 95% CI 1.08–2.09; ex-poor OR 0.91, 0.77–1.07).
  - The effect was specific to conduct and oppositional symptoms; anxiety and depression were unaffected.
  - Causal ID: moderate to strong (natural experiment, single region). Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1001/jama.290.15.2023](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.290.15.2023)
- Akee, Copeland, Keeler, Angold & Costello (2010), *AEJ: Applied Economics* 2(1):86–115.
  - An extra roughly \$4,000 a year for the poorest households raised schooling by one year at age 21 and reduced the probability of ever having committed a minor crime at ages 16–17 by 22%.
  - The mechanism is the authors' inference: they suggest improved parenting quality is the likely pathway. [doi:10.1257/app.2.1.86](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.2.1.86)
- Akee, Copeland, Costello & Simeonova (2018), *American Economic Review* 108(3):775–827.
  - Large beneficial effects on adolescents' emotional and behavioural health and personality traits, largest for children with the lowest initial endowments.
  - The income intervention also improved parental relationships, which the authors interpret as a potential mechanism. [doi:10.1257/aer.20160133](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.20160133)
- Cooper & Stewart (2021; online 2020), *Child Indicators Research* 14(3):981–1005.
  - A systematic review restricted to RCTs, quasi-experiments and fixed-effects studies. It found strong support for the hypothesis that household income positively affects children's cognitive, social-behavioural and health outcomes, especially in low-income households.
  - Income also affects intermediate outcomes: maternal mental health, parenting and the home environment.
  - The evidence base is mostly from the US (per the 2017 update). Grade: [E/M]. [doi:10.1007/s12187-020-09782-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-020-09782-0)

**Randomized evidence that disconfirms or bounds the income → parenting → child pathway**
Baby's First Years randomized about 1,000 US low-income mothers of newborns to \$333 or \$20 a month. Causal ID: strong.
- Noble, Magnuson, Duncan et al. (2025), NBER Working Paper 33844 (not peer reviewed).
  - At age 4 (N = 891), there were no significant effects on four preregistered primary outcomes (language, executive function, social-emotional problems, high-frequency EEG) or on three secondary outcomes.
  - The CBCL behaviour-problem index effect size was 0.011 (p = .872, N = 882). The design could detect effects of about 0.20 SD.
  - [NBER w33844](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33844)
- Duncan, Magnuson et al. (2025), *JAMA Pediatrics* 179(8):867–875. No improvement in maternal anxiety (ES 0.12, 95% CI −0.02 to 0.25), maternal depression (0.04, −0.08 to 0.17) or child physical health at age 4. [doi:10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.1612](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.1612)
- Hart et al. (2024), *Developmental Psychology* 60(12):2290–2305 (registered report). No statistically detectable effects on mothers' reports of language and socioemotional development at ages 1–3. [doi:10.1037/dev0001824](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0001824)
- Magnuson et al. (2025), *Nature Communications* 16:7517.
  - Household income rose moderately and poverty fell, but subjective economic hardship and quality of play with infants did not improve.
  - Parental psychological distress rose slightly and mothers' relationship quality fell slightly; most of these changes were not statistically significant.
  - High-cash mothers engaged more often in enriching child activities. [doi:10.1038/s41467-025-62438-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-025-62438-x)
- Raffington et al. (2026), *Nature Human Behaviour*. On a preregistered secondary outcome, high cash caused a −0.17 SD (95% CI −0.32 to −0.01) difference in children's DunedinPACE (epigenetic pace of ageing); there was no effect on Epigenetic-g. This is a caution against reading BFY as "money does nothing." [doi:10.1038/s41562-026-02568-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02568-4)

Other randomized and causal evidence:
- Baird, de Hoop & Özler (2013), *Journal of Human Resources* 48(2):370–403. A cash-transfer RCT in Malawi.
  - Among girls in school at baseline, psychological distress was 38% lower with unconditional transfers and 17% lower with conditional transfers than in controls.
  - There was no effect for girls who had already dropped out, and the benefits faded quickly once the program ended.
  - Non-WEIRD sample. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1353/jhr.2013.0014](https://doi.org/10.1353/jhr.2013.0014)
- Zimmerman et al. (2021), *BMJ Global Health* 6(4):e004661. Cash transfers and youth mental health in low- and middle-income countries.
  - 12 studies (13 interventions) were reviewed and 7 meta-analysed for depressive symptoms. Heterogeneity was very high (I² = 95.2%).
  - 11 interventions (85%) showed a significant positive impact on at least one mental-health outcome. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1136/bmjgh-2020-004661](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjgh-2020-004661)

**Is perceived financial support tied to adolescents' well-being across cultures?**
- Chentsova Dutton, Choi & Choi (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:353.
  - Design: school surveys in 2010; N = 7,279 high-school students (US 1,002; China 1,172; South Korea 3,993; Japan 1,112). Cross-sectional, with 3-item scales each for emotional, informational and financial/tangible parental support.
  - US adolescents perceived more emotional and informational support; East Asian adolescents, especially in China, perceived more financial support.
  - Distress (per the full text): emotional support β = −.19 (p < .001), informational −.05, financial −.01 (ns).
  - Positive self-beliefs: emotional β = .18, informational .12, financial .10. No individual culture × support interaction was significant.
  - The financial-support scale had low reliability (α = .52–.72), and the distress scale's invariance fit fell below the authors' own cutoffs.
  - Causal ID: none. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)

**Theory**
- Christiansen & Palkovitz (2001), *Journal of Family Issues* 22(1):84–106. Providing is a neglected form of paternal involvement, overlooked because it is assumed rather than noticed, goes unseen by family members, carries negative connotations and has been poorly conceptualized. Grade: [T]. [doi:10.1177/019251301022001004](https://doi.org/10.1177/019251301022001004)

### Inferences
- For the worked example: if the family was poor, the father's provision plausibly helped his daughter causally whether or not she read it as love. The evidence is strongest at low incomes and for behaviour and education, not for anxiety or depression. In a non-poor family, extra provision has weaker support as a cause of child benefit. [M]
- Baby's First Years and Adamsons & Johnson (2013) are the strongest evidence against "provision is love that children receive whether or not they notice." They do not show provision is irrelevant. They show modest, context-dependent effects, and that money does not automatically improve parents' mood or relationship quality.
- Chentsova Dutton et al. (2020) points the same way. Even where adolescents perceived high financial support, it was not associated with lower distress, while emotional support was, similarly in all four countries. That argues against a strong cultural-relativity version of translation, in which East Asian adolescents "receive" financial support as emotional care. It is cross-sectional, with weak measurement of financial support. [P]
- Financial and emotional care are not cleanly separable. Income partly affects children through parental mental health and relationships (Akee 2018; Cooper & Stewart 2021), that is, partly through emotional channels.

### Gaps
- No study was found that varies or measures whether a child *interprets* provision as love and tests whether that interpretation moderates provision's effect. That is the direct test of "received ≠ perceived."
- Amato & Gilbreth's effect sizes were not retrieved. Why the 1999 and 2013 meta-analyses disagree about child support (samples, measures, or the rise of nonmarital fathering) is not established.
- There is little causal evidence on provision in middle-class or affluent families, or for adult children.
- Zimmerman et al.'s pooled estimate was not retrieved because the abstract was truncated.

## Q3. Father involvement and how fathers express affection

### Takeaway
In longitudinal observational research, father engagement predicts better child outcomes. Children's perceived paternal acceptance is at least as strongly linked to adjustment as maternal acceptance. Yet a 2021 meta-analysis found paternal sensitivity unrelated to overall socioemotional functioning.

In the US father–son literature:
- Men express affection more through supportive or instrumental acts than through verbal or nonverbal affection.
- Fathers consistently report giving more affection than sons report receiving.
- Men who received little affection from their own fathers partly compensate with their sons (a curvilinear modelling–compensation pattern).

All of the affection studies are US-only, sons-only and self-report. The "provider father" is a historically situated script, not a fixed trait.

### Cited Findings
- Sarkadi et al. (2008; online 2007), *Acta Paediatrica* 97(2):153–158.
  - Systematic review of 24 longitudinal publications; 22 described positive effects of father involvement and 16 of these controlled for SES.
  - Active, regular engagement predicts a range of positive outcomes, but no particular form of engagement has been shown to outperform another. Engagement reduced behaviour problems in boys and psychological problems in young women.
  - Causal ID: weak. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1111/j.1651-2227.2007.00572.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1651-2227.2007.00572.x)
- Cabrera, Tamis-LeMonda, Bradley, Hofferth & Lamb (2000), *Child Development* 71(1):127–136. Essay on four US trends that changed father involvement: women's employment, nonresident fathers, greater involvement of resident fathers and cultural diversity. Grade: [T]. [doi:10.1111/1467-8624.00126](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00126)
- Rodrigues et al. (2021), *Child Development* 92(2):554–577. Meta-analyses of paternal sensitivity (children aged 7 months to 9 years), mostly correlational.
  - Associated with overall cognitive functioning (k = 23, N = 3,193, r = .19), emotion regulation (r = .22) and externalizing problems (r = −.08).
  - Not associated with overall socioemotional functioning (k = 24, N = 2,924, r = −.03) or internalizing. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1111/cdev.13545](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13545)
- Rohner & Veneziano (2001), *Review of General Psychology* 5(4):382–405. Review: father love's influence equals and sometimes exceeds mother love's; some studies find father love the sole significant predictor. Grade: [M/T]. [doi:10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.382](https://doi.org/10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.382)
- Paternal acceptance correlates more strongly with children's adjustment than maternal acceptance ([Khaleque & Rohner 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022111406120)). Father-based parent effects were more frequent than mother effects across nine countries ([Rothenberg et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0954579419001214)).
- Floyd & Morman (1998), *Communication Quarterly* 46(2):144–162. Developed and validated the Affectionate Communication Index (ACI) in a multi-phase study with 781 participants (US). [doi:10.1080/01463379809370092](https://doi.org/10.1080/01463379809370092)
- Morman & Floyd (1999), *Communication Studies* 50(4):294–309.
  - 55 father–young adult son pairs. Fathers and sons conveyed affection mainly by providing social support rather than through direct verbal or nonverbal expressions; fathers were more affectionate to sons than sons were to fathers.
  - Affection was associated with psychological femininity, endorsement of father–son affection, closeness, self-disclosure and communication satisfaction.
  - Grade: [P]; WEIRD-only. [doi:10.1080/10510979909388501](https://doi.org/10.1080/10510979909388501)
- Floyd & Morman (2000), *Communication Monographs* 67(4):347–361. 506 fathers of sons: a curvilinear relationship between affection received from one's own father and affection given to one's son supported a combined modelling–compensation hypothesis. Retrospective self-report. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1080/03637750009376516](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637750009376516)
- Morman & Floyd (2002), *Western Journal of Communication* 66(4):395–411.
  - 139 father–son dyads. Men felt closer to, were more satisfied with, and expressed more verbal, nonverbal and supportive affection with their sons than with their own fathers; both fathers' and sons' reports agreed.
  - Fathers reported more closeness and affection toward sons than sons felt or expressed toward them.
  - The authors read this as a changing culture of fatherhood, but the comparison confounds generation with relational role. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1080/10570310209374746](https://doi.org/10.1080/10570310209374746)
- Floyd & Morman (2003), *Journal of Social Psychology* 143(5):599–612. US men were more affectionate with biological sons than with stepsons or adopted sons, and more affectionate with sons than sons were with them. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1080/00224540309598466](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224540309598466)
- Floyd & Morman (2005), *Journal of Social and Personal Relationships* 22(1):99–109.
  - 115 father–adult son dyads. Fathers' and sons' reports of fathers' affection were linearly related, but fathers rated themselves as more affectionate than their sons rated them.
  - Sons' number of siblings related inversely to sons' reports, not fathers' (consistent with a naïve theory of affection). Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1177/0265407505049323](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407505049323)
- Finley & Schwartz (2004), *Educational and Psychological Measurement* 64(1):143–164.
  - The Nurturant Fathering Scale measures the affective quality of fathering; the Father Involvement Scale measures reported and desired involvement across life domains. Internal consistency was high.
  - The Father Involvement Scale's factor structure matched Parsons and Bales's instrumental and socioemotional (expressive) dimensions. [doi:10.1177/0013164403258453](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164403258453)
- Rane & McBride (2000), *Journal of Family Issues* 21(3):347–366. 89 married US couples with preschoolers: fathers' involvement did not differ by whether they rated the parent or worker status as most central, but fathers high on nurturing-role centrality were significantly more involved. Grade: [P]; WEIRD-only. [doi:10.1177/019251300021003004](https://doi.org/10.1177/019251300021003004)
- Edin & Nelson (2013), *Doing the Best I Can: Fatherhood in the Inner City* (University of California Press; 2019 e-edition DOI).
  - An ethnography following 110 low-income fathers in Philadelphia and Camden, NJ, over seven years. The publisher describes the book as documenting a redefinition of family life in which the father–child bond, rather than the parents' relationship, is central.
  - Per reviews, men often defined a good father in opposition to their own fathers. Details come from publisher and review summaries.
  - Grade: [P]; qualitative. [doi:10.1525/9780520955134](https://doi.org/10.1525/9780520955134); [UC Press](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/doing-the-best-i-can/paper)

### Inferences
- A father who equates love with provision follows a historically and socially situated script, not a fixed male trait. In the US, men's affection to sons rose across generations (Morman & Floyd 2002). Nurturing-role centrality, not worker identity, predicts involvement (Rane & McBride 2000). Low-income fathers emphasize the relational bond (Edin & Nelson). [M]
- The father–son data show a robust giver-reports-more-than-receiver asymmetry (2002, 2005), consistent with "intended ≠ perceived." But it could equally reflect self-enhancement, social desirability or different reference standards; none of the studies tests which.
- The evidence on supportive (instrumental) versus verbal affection comes entirely from father–son dyads. Extending it to fathers and daughters is [SPEC].

### Gaps
- No cross-cultural validation of the ACI, or of fathers' supportive vs verbal affection norms in East Asian, African or Latin American samples, was found.
- Finley & Schwartz (2006), "Parsons and Bales revisited: Young adult children's characterization of the fathering role," *Psychology of Men & Masculinity* 7(1):42–55: title verified, findings not retrieved. [doi:10.1037/1524-9220.7.1.42](https://doi.org/10.1037/1524-9220.7.1.42)
- No study was found on how fathers express affection to daughters specifically (verbal vs supportive).

## Q4. Intergenerational relations: solidarity, ambivalence and cross-national exchanges of time and money

### Takeaway
Adult parent–child relationships are multidimensional (solidarity) and often simultaneously positive and negative (ambivalence). Across Europe, support flows mainly downward, from parents to adult children, even past age 70. Welfare regimes shape the form of family support (frequency versus intensity; light help versus personal care) more than they crowd it out.

Older parents' life satisfaction tracks being able to give, and emotional closeness, more than receiving help. Strong filial norms are not uniformly good for elders. Filial norms themselves shift over the life course and historical time.

### Cited Findings
- Bengtson & Roberts (1991), *JMF* 53(4):856–870. Formal theory construction for intergenerational solidarity in ageing families. Grade: [T]. [doi:10.2307/352993](https://doi.org/10.2307/352993)

- Luescher & Pillemer (1998), *JMF* 60(2):413–425. Proposes ambivalence as an alternative lens to solidarity or conflict. Grade: [T]. [doi:10.2307/353858](https://doi.org/10.2307/353858)

- Even in old age the modal flow of care runs from parent to child. Expecting adult children to repay care "equivalently" fits neither the observed European patterns nor the finding that elders' well-being tracks being able to give. [M]

- Most cross-national intergenerational evidence retrieved is European or Israeli. ## Q5. Filial piety (dual model), family obligation and perceived parental sacrifice

### Takeaway
The dual filial piety model separates reciprocal filial piety (based on affection and gratitude) from authoritarian filial piety (based on hierarchy and obligation). Its two-factor structure was found in Taiwan, Hong Kong and mainland China, with some alterations in China.

In poor Hong Kong adolescents, perceived parental sacrifice related to life satisfaction partly through *both* forms of filial piety. So a simple "authoritarian = harmful" summary is not supported there. Parents perceive more sacrifice than their adolescents do.

Family obligation and daily assistance generally go with better family relationships and even daily happiness. Role reversal (parentification) is associated with later psychopathology.

### Cited Findings
- Yeh & Bedford (2003), *Asian Journal of Social Psychology* 6(3):215–228. Empirical test of the Dual Filial Piety model, which integrates conflicting findings on whether filial piety helps or harms development. Abstract-level only; effect sizes not retrieved. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1046/j.1467-839X.2003.00122.x](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1467-839X.2003.00122.x)
- Bedford & Yeh (2019), *Frontiers in Psychology* 10:100. Reconceptualizes filial piety from Chinese-specific norms to a "contextualized personality construct": a pair of culturally sensitive schemas of parent–child interaction, reciprocal and authoritarian. It is offered as a framework for intergenerational research in other cultures. Grade: [T]. [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00100](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00100)
- Yeh, Yi, Tsao & Wan (2013), *International Sociology* 28(3):277–296. 2006 East Asian Social Survey: dual filial piety was found in Taiwan, Hong Kong and China, with some component differences in China, and was associated with various aspects of daily life. Cross-sectional. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1177/0268580913484345](https://doi.org/10.1177/0268580913484345)
- Leung & Shek (2020; online 2018), *Applied Research in Quality of Life* 15(1):259–272.

[doi:10.1007/s11482-018-9678-0](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11482-018-9678-0); accepted manuscript at [PolyU](https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/95560/1/Leung_Parental_Sacrifice_Filial.pdf)

  - [doi:10.1007/s12187-015-9332-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-015-9332-4); abstract via [RePEc/IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/chinre/v9y2016i3d10.1007_s12187-015-9332-4.html)

  - Qin is fostered mainly by parental devotion and sacrifice. Grade: [P]; qualitative. [doi:10.1037/aap0000092](https://doi.org/10.1037/aap0000092)

  - Daily diaries from 752 US adolescents aged 14–15 of Latin American, Asian and European backgrounds; within-person design.
  - Providing daily family assistance was generally not stressful. It was associated with higher happiness, largely through a sense of role fulfilment, with few group differences. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1037/a0014728](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014728)
- Hooper, DeCoster, White & Voltz (2011), *Journal of Clinical Psychology* 67(10):1028–1043.

- Leung & Shek (2020) does not support "authoritarian filial piety = harmful." In that poor Chinese sample both forms carried positive indirect associations with life satisfaction, so effects likely depend on context and outcome. [P]
- Parental sacrifice is a culturally elaborated care signal that adolescents register less than parents believe (Leung & Shek 2016), consistent with "intended ≠ perceived."
  - The larger father–child gap did not predict the measured outcome. That cuts against the claim that the *size* of a translation gap is what does the harm. [P]
- Obligation and assistance are not inherently burdensome when meaningful and proportionate (Telzer & Fuligni 2009). The paper should keep normative reciprocity distinct from role reversal (parentification) when it discusses what a daughter "owes."

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of the correlates of reciprocal vs authoritarian filial piety (relationship quality, perceived parental care, mental health) was found. The common claim that reciprocal filial piety relates positively and authoritarian filial piety negatively or weakly to relationship quality could not be confirmed with effect sizes in this pass.
- Korean adaptations of the Dual Filial Piety Scale are for the Korea researcher. A Polish version exists ([PsycTESTS doi:10.1037/t85657-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t85657-000)).
- No longitudinal evidence was found on whether perceived parental sacrifice produces gratitude or guilt/indebtedness.

## Q6. Culture and family-change theories: what is empirically supported and what is theoretical?

### Takeaway
At the aggregate level, two patterns are well supported:
- Socioeconomic development is robustly associated with rising individualist practices and values (78 countries over 51 years).
- The value attributed to children has shifted from utilitarian toward psychological, with persistent cultural path dependence.

The individual- and family-level mechanisms (Kağıtçıbaşı's persisting emotional interdependence; Greenfield's ecology → values → developmental pathways) rest mostly on cross-sectional, repeated cross-sectional or book-word-frequency data. The Google Books data have documented limits.

Marriage-change theories (deinstitutionalization, suffocation) are contested and class-specific, and their authors have qualified them.

## Q7. Culture and support: who seeks it, how it is given, and which forms help

### Takeaway
On average, Asian and Asian American samples report seeking explicit support less than European Americans. Relationship concerns explain the difference, and these groups appear to benefit more from implicit or unsolicited support. Japanese provide relatively more problem-focused support.

Caveats:
- The evidence comes mostly from small US college samples.

- MacGeorge et al. (2004) warns against attributing a father–daughter mismatch to "gender cultures."

- No meta-analysis of cultural differences in support seeking or benefit was found.
- Almost no culture-and-support research concerns parents and their children specifically; most uses friends or generic close others.
- The replication status of Taylor et al. (2007)'s implicit-support cortisol effect was not verified.

## Q8. Emotion-expression norms and verbal affection across cultures

### Takeaway
Display rules vary across countries along individualism–collectivism, but some effects are universal, notably greater expression toward in-groups. A methodologically careful study found Japanese valued *directly verbalizing feelings* with mothers and fathers more than Americans, the opposite of the usual stereotype.

Ideal affect (excited vs calm) differs across cultures and is socialized through children's books. Folk models of happiness differ (personal achievement vs social harmony).

Mothers value warmth similarly across groups but express it partly through different channels: nurturance and instrumental support versus direct affection. Within one country, mothers' beliefs about which behaviours count as loving differ by ethnicity more than by income.

### Cited Findings
- Matsumoto, Yoo, Fontaine et al. (2008), *JCCP* 39(1):55–74.
  - More than 5,000 respondents in 32 countries completed the Display Rule Assessment Inventory.
  - Universal effects included greater expression toward in-groups than out-groups and an overall regulation effect. Individualistic and collectivistic cultures differed in overall endorsement of expressivity and in emotion-specific norms.
  - These are self-reported norms, not observed behaviour. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1177/0022022107311854](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022107311854)
- Wilkins & Gareis (2006), *International Journal of Intercultural Relations* 30(1):51–75. Cross-cultural study of the locution "I love you." The citation is verified, but the sample and findings could not be accessed (UNVERIFIED). Do not use the figures that circulate on blogs. [doi:10.1016/j.ijintrel.2005.07.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijintrel.2005.07.003)
- Gareis & Wilkins (2011), *IJIR* 35(3):307–319. Love expression in the United States and Germany; title verified. [doi:10.1016/j.ijintrel.2010.06.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ijintrel.2010.06.006)
- Caldwell-Harris, Kronrod & Yang (2013), "Do more, say less: Saying 'I love you' in Chinese and American cultures," *Intercultural Pragmatics* 10(1). Per a secondary snippet only: Chinese respondents were more reticent and avoided "I love you" with family, especially parents. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1515/ip-2013-0002](https://doi.org/10.1515/ip-2013-0002)

Ideal affect and affect valuation theory: American contexts value high-arousal positive states more, East Asian contexts low-arousal positive states. Grade: [M/T]. [doi:10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00043.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00043.x)

  - In an experiment, exposure to exciting vs calm storybooks shifted children's preferences. Grade: [M]. [doi:10.1177/0146167206292749](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167206292749)

- Verbal affection is a culturally variable *signal* of love. Whether its absence reads as a lack of love plausibly depends on the receiver's norms, which supports translation.
- But Seki et al. (2002) show that stereotypes about which cultures verbalize are themselves method-dependent and unreliable.
- Within-country variation (Buchanan et al. 2021) means a father–daughter mismatch can arise from class, ethnicity, gender or cohort inside one culture, not only between cultures.

### Gaps
- Wilkins & Gareis (2006) details (samples, proportions) remain unverified.
- No study was found linking parents' frequency of verbal affection to children's perceived love across cultures while controlling for nonverbal and instrumental care.

## Q9. Same behaviour, different meaning? Parental control, warmth, sacrifice, discipline and intergenerational cultural dissonance

### Takeaway
The meaning-moderation idea has partial support:
- Korean adolescents (1985) perceived parental control as correlated with warmth and low neglect, the opposite of North American findings.
- Chinese American adolescents whose ideal parental warmth exceeds what they perceive have more internalizing problems, and appreciating parental sacrifice buffers this.
- Sacrifice is a distinct dimension of parental support.

Against it, large meta-analyses and multi-country studies find more similarities than differences in how parenting relates to child outcomes, and cultural normativeness only partly buffers harsh practices. The 1985 Korean pattern did not hold in a Korean American sample 20 years later. Meanings shift with acculturation and time, and they attenuate rather than reverse associations with harm.

### Cited Findings
- Rohner & Pettengill (1985), *Child Development* 56(2):524–528.
  - Korean adolescents who perceived more parental control also perceived more warmth and less neglect, a pattern the authors contrast sharply with studies of North American youth.
  - The direction is control ↔ warmth/acceptance. The North American comparison comes from other studies, not from a same-design US sample. Sample size was not retrieved.
  - Grade: [P]. [doi:10.2307/1129739](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129739) (JSTOR DOI; the Wiley DOI [10.1111/j.1467-8624.1985.tb00125.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1985.tb00125.x) is the same paper)
- Kim, E. (2005), *Ethos* 33(3):347–366.
  - 106 Korean American families in the US Midwest, with independent reports from adolescents, mothers and fathers; cross-sectional. Parents were reported as warm, with moderate-to-firm behavioural control.
  - Within those ranges, adolescents' and mothers' reports of maternal control correlated with *less* acceptance. Fathers who reported more control also reported more warmth and more hostility.
  - The author interprets this as adaptation to mainstream American ideology. Grade: [P]. [doi:10.1525/eth.2005.33.3.347](https://doi.org/10.1525/eth.2005.33.3.347)
- Kim, K. & Rohner (2002), *JCCP* 33(2):127–140.

- Rohner & Pettengill (1985) is often cited as proof that "control means love in Korea." Kim (2005) found the opposite pattern for mothers in a Korean American sample. [P]
- The translation-relevant mechanisms with the best evidence are:
  - a child's ideal-versus-perceived warmth gap (Wu & Chao 2005, 2011);
  - the parent–child acculturation gap (Lui 2015; Kim 2013), which predicts internalizing problems;
  - appreciation of parental sacrifice as a buffer.
  - Effects are small (around r = .2) and mostly cross-sectional. [M]

- Rohner & Pettengill's sample size and measures were not retrieved, and no direct replication in contemporary Korea was found in this pass (Korea researcher to check).
- Most "meaning" studies infer meaning from group membership instead of measuring the attributions individuals actually make about the parent's behaviour.

## Q10. Developmental change: expectancy violation–realignment and parents updating their model of the child's needs

### Takeaway
Collins's expectancy violation–realignment perspective holds that rapid change in adolescence violates parents' and adolescents' expectations, producing conflict that prompts realignment of the relationship.

What the evidence supports:
- Meta-analytic and Dutch longitudinal data show normative transformation: parental power falls, perceived support dips in mid-adolescence, conflict affect rises from early to mid-adolescence, and relationships improve from about 16 to 20.
- Individual differences are substantial.

What it does not show:
- One 4-wave study found conflict was associated with relational change but did not drive it.
- No study was found that directly measures parents updating their beliefs about a child's needs and links that to the child's perceived care.

### Cited Findings
- Hadiwijaya, Klimstra, Vermunt, Branje & Meeus (2017), *Journal of Youth and Adolescence* 46(8):1772–1788.
  - Two Dutch cohorts over five waves (ages 12–16, n = 919; ages 16–20, n = 392), analysed with latent transition analysis.
  - Explicitly tested the separation-individuation, evolutionary, maturational and expectancy violation–realignment perspectives, each of which proposes relationships deteriorate as adolescents become independent.
  - From 12 to 16, adolescents moved away from parental authority and perceived more conflict; from 16 to 20 they perceived independence and improved relationships, with substantial individual differences.
  - Grade: [M]; WEIRD-only. [doi:10.1007/s10964-016-0627-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-016-0627-7)
- Laursen, Coy & Collins (1998), *Child Development* 69(3):817–832.
  - The meta-analyses found little support for the common view that parent–child conflict rises and then falls.
  - Conflict rate and total conflict decline with age; conflict affect increases from early to mid-adolescence and with pubertal maturation. Grade: [E]. [doi:10.1111/j.1467-8624.1998.00817.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.1998.00817.x)
- De Goede, Branje & Meeus (2009; online 2008), *Journal of Youth and Adolescence* 38(1):75–88.
  - Four waves of Dutch data (951 early and 390 middle adolescents). Perceived support declined from early to middle adolescence and rose again from middle to late adolescence for girls.
  - Conflict rose temporarily in middle adolescence, and parental power declined as relationships became more egalitarian. Conflict was associated with relationship change but was not its impetus.
  - Grade: [M]; WEIRD-only. [doi:10.1007/s10964-008-9286-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-008-9286-7)
- Branje (2018), *Child Development Perspectives* 12(3):171–176. Conflicts become more frequent and intense in adolescence but serve to negotiate relational change; parent–adolescent dyads with more emotional variability during conflict adapt and reorganize more effectively. Grade: [M/T]. [doi:10.1111/cdep.12278](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdep.12278)
- Collins & Luebker (1994), "Parent and adolescent expectancies: Individual and relational significance," *New Directions for Child and Adolescent Development* 1994(66):65–80; title verified. [doi:10.1002/cd.23219946607](https://doi.org/10.1002/cd.23219946607)
- Collins & Laursen (2004), *Journal of Early Adolescence* 24(1):55–62. The field has shifted toward adolescents' relationships, their expanding networks and the interrelations among them. [doi:10.1177/0272431603260882](https://doi.org/10.1177/0272431603260882)
- Laursen & Collins (2009), "Parent–child relationships during adolescence," *Handbook of Adolescent Psychology*. [doi:10.1002/9780470479193.adlpsy002002](https://doi.org/10.1002/9780470479193.adlpsy002002)

### Inferences
- Part of the father–daughter mismatch may be developmental. A provision-centred care script that fit a young child may not have been updated as the daughter's needs shifted toward understanding and respect for her autonomy. The expectancy violation–realignment perspective predicts conflict as the signal for realignment; the data suggest most families realign by late adolescence, but not all. [T/SPEC]
- Alternative explanation: perceiving less care during adolescence is normative and often temporary. A mismatch that persists into adulthood needs other explanations (personality, unresolved conflict, family stress) and is not automatically a translation failure.

### Gaps
- The original expectancy violation–realignment chapter (Collins, 1995, in an edited volume) could not be verified in a database; its claims are reported here as described by Hadiwijaya et al. (2017).
- Data on this model are Dutch or US; no cross-cultural test was found.
- No study was found that measures parents updating their model of the child over time and links it to the child's perceived care.

## Q11. Parental responsibilities versus romantic reciprocity: communal norms and what adult children owe (or feel they owe)

### Takeaway
Parent–child relationships are prototypically communal: benefits are given in response to need, not to create debts. Care flows mainly downward over the life course. Elders are better off when they can give than when they mainly receive. Filial-responsibility norms vary over the life course and across historical time and generations.

Meaningful, proportionate family assistance relates to adolescents' daily happiness, while role reversal (parentification) is associated with later psychopathology. The evidence supports treating a child's obligations as normatively different from a romantic partner's reciprocity.

### Cited Findings
- Clark & Mills (1979), "Interpersonal attraction in exchange and communal relationships," *JPSP* 37(1):12–24. Title verified; this is the foundational communal/exchange distinction. Grade: [E] as a distinction. [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.37.1.12](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.1.12)
- Clark & Mills (1993), *PSPB* 19(6):684–691. Defends the communal–exchange distinction against Batson's critique: it is not reducible to ingratiation, etiquette or the breadth of benefits exchanged. Grade: [T]. [doi:10.1177/0146167293196003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167293196003); Batson (1993) critique: [doi:10.1177/0146167293196002](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167293196002)
- Mills, Clark, Ford & Johnson (2004), *Personal Relationships* 11(2):213–230. A 10-item communal strength measure, distinct from behavioural interdependence and from liking; it predicts helping and the spouse's marital satisfaction. [doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2004.00079.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2004.00079.x)
- Stein et al. (1998), "'Because they're my parents': An intergenerational study of felt obligation and parental caregiving," *JMF* 60(3):611–622; title verified. [doi:10.2307/353532](https://doi.org/10.2307/353532)
- Filial norms weakened after midlife, after a parent's death and over historical time, but were stronger in later-born generations ([Gans & Silverstein 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2006.00307.x)).
- Elders who are active providers have higher life satisfaction; being mainly a recipient is associated with lower satisfaction, and filial norms are negatively related to it ([Lowenstein, Katz & Gur-Yaish 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1540-4560.2007.00541.x)).
- Parents over 70 remain net givers in Europe ([Albertini, Kohli & Vogel 2007](https://doi.org/10.1177/0958928707081068)).
- Grown children benefit from intense parental support, while parents who see their children as needing too much support report lower life satisfaction ([Fingerman et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2012.00987.x)).
- Daily family assistance goes with higher adolescent happiness, via role fulfilment ([Telzer & Fuligni 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014728)).
- Self-reported childhood parentification correlates r = .14 with adult psychopathology ([Hooper et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.20807)).

### Inferences
- Normatively and empirically, parental care is not a loan. A model in which a daughter "owes" equivalent emotional or financial care conflicts with communal norms and with the observed downward flows. The paper's translation framing should treat a child's "failure to reciprocate" differently from a partner's. [M]
- Conversely, adult children's felt obligation and assistance are common, often rewarding and culturally variable (Q5). Framing them as purely burdensome would be a WEIRD-centric error.

### Gaps
- No direct cross-cultural test of communal norms in parent–adult-child dyads was found; most communal-relationship research uses friends or spouses.

## Q12. WEIRD sampling, ecological fallacy and measurement-invariance caveats

### Takeaway
Family and developmental research remains heavily WEIRD-sampled. Value differences between countries are small relative to the consensus and variation within them. The textbook contrasts (Japanese or Koreans more collectivist than Americans) are weakly supported or unsupported in individual-level data. Many cross-cultural comparisons never establish measurement invariance. Cultural averages must not be used to describe individual fathers or daughters.

### Cited Findings
- Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan (2010), *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 33(2–3):61–83. WEIRD samples are unusually poor bases for generalizing about humans and are frequent outliers. [doi:10.1017/S0140525X0999152X](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X0999152X)
- Nielsen, Haun, Kärtner & Legare (2017), *Journal of Experimental Child Psychology* 162:31–38. High-impact developmental journals are heavily skewed toward WEIRD samples, with little sign of change. [doi:10.1016/j.jecp.2017.04.017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jecp.2017.04.017)
- Apicella, Norenzayan & Henrich (2020), *Evolution and Human Behavior* 41(5):319–329. A review of the decade since the WEIRD paper. [doi:10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2020.07.015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2020.07.015)
- Muthukrishna et al. (2020), *Psychological Science* 31(6):678–701. A cultural-distance metric (fixation index on World Values Survey data) to guide sampling relative to the US or China. [doi:10.1177/0956797620916782](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620916782)
- Ghai (2021), *Nature Human Behaviour* 5(8):971–972. Argues for retiring the WEIRD dichotomy and reimagining sample diversity. [doi:10.1038/s41562-021-01175-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-021-01175-9)
- Draper et al. (2023; online 2022), *Infant and Child Development* 32(6):e2375. Structural barriers to publishing child-development research from the Majority World. [doi:10.1002/icd.2375](https://doi.org/10.1002/icd.2375)
- Oyserman, Coon & Kemmelmeier (2002), *Psychological Bulletin* 128(1):3–72.
  - European Americans were more individualistic and less collectivistic than others overall. But they were not more individualistic than African Americans or Latinos, nor less collectivistic than Japanese or Koreans.
  - Among Asians, only Chinese samples showed large effects. [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.128.1.3](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.1.3)
- Takano & Osaka (2018), *Asian Journal of Social Psychology* 21(4):301–316. Of 35 empirical studies comparing Japanese and Americans on individualism/collectivism, 19 found no clear difference, 11 found Japanese more individualistic, and only 5 supported the common view. [doi:10.1111/ajsp.12322](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajsp.12322)
- Fischer & Schwartz (2011; online 2010), *JCCP* 42(7):1127–1144.
  - Three datasets: 67 countries (N = 41,968), 19 countries (N = 42,359) and 62 countries (N = 84,887). Countries agreed more than they disagreed on value priorities, which the authors take as refuting strong claims that culture determines values.
  - Only conformity values behave like culture as a shared meaning system. [doi:10.1177/0022022110381429](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022110381429)
- Boer, Hanke & He (2018), *JCCP* 49(5):713–734. Reviews methods for detecting bias and non-equivalence, and content-analyses more than 500 culture-comparative studies from 2008–2015 for current practice. [doi:10.1177/0022022117749042](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022117749042)
- Measurement invariance in the studies above:
  - Chentsova Dutton et al. (2020) reported good metric invariance for the support scales, but the distress scale's fit fell below the authors' own criteria ([doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)).
  - PARQ invariance has been tested only between the US and Australia ([Gomez & Rohner 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1069397111403111)).

### Inferences
- Any statement like "fathers in culture X express love through provision" describes a group-average tendency with large overlap between groups. Individuals vary more within than between cultures, which is the Fischer & Schwartz pattern for values. [M]
- Mean differences in "warmth" or "support" across cultures should be interpreted only after metric, and ideally scalar, invariance has been shown. Most studies cited in this dossier did not report it.

### Gaps
- No invariance tests between Western and East Asian samples were found for the Affectionate Communication Index, the Nurturant Fathering Scale or the parental-sacrifice measures.

## Q13. Validated measurement instruments by construct

### Takeaway
Instruments exist for most constructs in the translation chain, but few have invariance evidence between Western and East Asian samples. None measures *intended* parental love and *perceived* love on matched scales across cultures. Instruments for the father–daughter case (paternal affection modalities, parental sacrifice, filial piety) have mostly been validated in a single culture.

### Cited Findings
- **Perceived parental acceptance–rejection.** The Parental Acceptance–Rejection Questionnaire (PARQ) comes in child, adult and parent versions, each with mother and father forms, plus short forms.
  - Four scales: warmth/affection, hostility/aggression, indifference/neglect, undifferentiated rejection ([Rohner et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1525/eth.2005.33.3.299)).
  - Reliability meta-analysis: [Khaleque & Rohner 2002b](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022102033001006).
  - Invariance between the US and Australia: [Gomez & Rohner 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/1069397111403111). Italian vs US short forms: [Senese et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/15295192.2016.1180943).
  - PsycTESTS records: Child PARQ Mother ([10.1037/t25781-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t25781-000)); Adult PARQ Mother ([10.1037/t05622-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t05622-000)); Adult PARQ Mother short form ([10.1037/t05617-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t05617-000)).
  - A Korean-language PARQ was used with adolescents in Korea in 1985 ([Rohner & Pettengill 1985](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129739)); its psychometrics are deferred to the Korea researcher.
- **Psychological adjustment (IPARTheory).** The Personality Assessment Questionnaire (PAQ) is reliable among US ethnic groups, but cross-cultural reliability evidence is insufficient ([Khaleque & Rohner 2002b](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022102033001006)).
- **Ideal vs perceived parental warmth.** The acceptance–rejection subscale of the Children's Report on Parent Behavior Inventory (CRPBI), completed once for ideal and once for perceived warmth ([Wu & Chao 2005](https://doi.org/10.1080/01650250500147444)). Parental warmth and open communication measures are also used, together with a *qin* measure covering devotion, sacrifice, thoughtfulness and *guan* ([Wu & Chao 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021063)).
- **Types of perceived parental support.** Emotional, informational and financial support, 3 items each; the financial scale is weak (α = .52–.72) ([Chentsova Dutton et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)).
- **Parental sacrifice.**
  - Chinese Parental Sacrifice Scale, confirmatory factor analyses in Hong Kong: Leung, Shek & Ma (2016), *Child Indicators Research* 9(1):173–192 ([doi:10.1007/s12187-015-9313-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-015-9313-7)).
  - Sacrifice and acceptance as distinct factors, equivalent across Chinese and Filipino Americans ([Chao & Kaeochinda 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4419-5728-3_4)).
- **Filial piety.**
  - Dual Filial Piety Scale: reciprocal and authoritarian subscales ([Yeh & Bedford 2003](https://doi.org/10.1046/j.1467-839X.2003.00122.x)).
  - 12-item short form (6 + 6) used in Hong Kong ([Leung & Shek 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11482-018-9678-0)).
  - Polish version ([PsycTESTS 10.1037/t85657-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t85657-000)).
  - Three-Dimensional Filial Piety Scale, 15 items, Chinese working adults: exploratory factor analysis n = 617, confirmatory n = 585, test–retest n = 67 ([Shi & Wang 2019, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02040](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02040)).
- **Family obligation and assistance.** Attitudes toward family obligations ([Fuligni et al. 1999](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00075)); daily-diary measure of family assistance ([Telzer & Fuligni 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014728)).
- **Affectionate communication.** The Affectionate Communication Index ([Floyd & Morman 1998](https://doi.org/10.1080/01463379809370092)), validated with US samples only. Verbal, nonverbal and supportive affection are distinguished in [Morman & Floyd 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/10570310209374746).
- **Father involvement and nurturance.** Nurturant Fathering Scale and Father Involvement Scale, reported and desired involvement; the factors map onto instrumental vs expressive roles ([Finley & Schwartz 2004](https://doi.org/10.1177/0013164403258453)).
- **Paternal sensitivity.** Observational coding systems, pooled in [Rodrigues et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdev.13545).
- **Intergenerational solidarity and ambivalence.**
  - Solidarity dimensions ([Bengtson & Roberts 1991](https://doi.org/10.2307/352993)) and latent-class typology ([Silverstein & Bengtson 1997](https://doi.org/10.1086/231213)).
  - Ambivalence: direct measures ([Pillemer & Suitor 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00602.x)); indirect ambivalence modelled multilevel ([Willson et al. 2003](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2003.01055.x)).
- **Communal motivation.** 10-item Communal Strength measure ([Mills et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2004.00079.x)).
- **Emotion display norms.** Display Rule Assessment Inventory, used in 32 countries ([Matsumoto et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022107311854)).
- **Support seeking.** Brief COPE plus social-support items from the full COPE ([Taylor et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.3.354)).
- **Discipline and its normativeness.** Maternal Discipline Techniques Questionnaire ([10.1037/t30800-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t30800-000)) and Perceived Normativeness of Discipline Techniques Measure ([10.1037/t30801-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t30801-000)), from [Gershoff et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01409.x).
- **Basic psychological needs.** The Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scales across four cultures ([Chen et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-014-9450-1)). A 2022 *Psychological Assessment* article argues the frustration subscales are probably not valid measures of need frustration (title verified via its Crossref supplement record, 10.1037/pas0001193.supp).

### Inferences
- The paper's translation chain (intended, expressed, perceived, received, compatibility) lacks a validated cross-cultural instrument for "intended" and "received" care. Most instruments capture the child's perception, and parent-report versions (Parent PARQ, Chinese Parental Sacrifice Scale) are rarely compared on matched items.

### Gaps
- An Affect Valuation Index citation (for ideal affect) was not verified in this pass.
- Spanish and Korean adaptations of the fathering scales were not verified.

## Q14. Synthesis for the paper: evidence for and against the translation hypothesis, and the father–daughter worked example

### Takeaway
On the family and culture evidence, translation is a credible, partly supported *moderator* of how care is perceived. It is not the main determinant of child outcomes.

Supporting the translation premises:
- Perception is what predicts adjustment.
- Some signals of care are culturally variable: sacrifice, control, verbal affection, solicited or explicit support, and instrumental vs expressive warmth.
- Givers report more care than receivers perceive: fathers vs sons on affection, parents vs adolescents on sacrifice.
- Ideal–perceived and acculturation gaps predict internalizing problems.

Against strong versions, or offering alternative explanations:
- Core acceptance cues and their links to outcomes are largely shared across cultures.
- Perceived emotional support, not financial support, predicts lower adolescent distress in four countries alike.
- Hostility and neglect predict outcomes regardless of cultural meaning.
- Child effects and family stress or structural strain explain much of parent–child covariation.
- Money's effects on children are real but contingent and modest.
- One gap measure (the father–adolescent sacrifice gap) did not predict the outcome it was tested against.

### Cited Findings
**For the translation premises**
- Perceived acceptance, not parental intent, is what correlates with adjustment pan-culturally ([Khaleque & Rohner 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x); [Putnick et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12366)). [E/M]
- The same support act has different benefits by culture: implicit vs explicit ([Taylor et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01987.x)); unsolicited vs solicited ([Mojaverian & Kim 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212465319)). [P–M]
- Gaps between ideal and perceived warmth predict internalizing among second-generation Chinese Americans, and appreciating devotion and sacrifice buffers the link ([Wu & Chao 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0021063)). [P–M]
- Givers perceive more than receivers:
  - Fathers report more affection than sons perceive ([Morman & Floyd 2002](https://doi.org/10.1080/10570310209374746); [Floyd & Morman 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407505049323)).
  - Parents report more sacrifice than adolescents perceive, and the father–adolescent gap is the largest ([Leung & Shek 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-015-9332-4)). [P]
- Channels of warmth differ (instrumental vs direct) ([Cheah et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0039855)), and beliefs about what counts as loving differ by ethnicity within the US ([Buchanan et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-020-01347-2)). [P]
- Provision tends to be invisible to the family ([Christiansen & Palkovitz 2001](https://doi.org/10.1177/019251301022001004)). [T]
- Parent–child acculturation gaps are associated with intergenerational conflict and poorer mental health ([Lui 2015](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038449)), with a longitudinal pathway that is stronger with fathers ([Kim et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029169)). [M]

**Against, or alternative explanations**
- Shared "dictionary": parenting–outcome associations show more similarities than differences across cultures ([Pinquart & Kauser 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/cdp0000149); [Sorkhabi 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/01650250500172640)), and rejection effects are nearly universal ([Putnick et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12366)). [E/M]
- In four countries, perceived emotional support predicted lower adolescent distress; perceived financial support did not (β = −.01), with no individual culture × support interactions ([Chentsova Dutton et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)). [P]
- Meaning attenuates but does not neutralize: harsh practices predict worse outcomes even where they are normative ([Lansford et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2005.00847.x); [Gershoff et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01409.x)). [M]
- Reverse causation: child-to-parent effects are ubiquitous across nine countries ([Rothenberg et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0954579419001214); [Lansford et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jora.12381); [Pinquart 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/dev0000295)). [M–E]
- Structural explanations: economic stress affects parenting and relationships ([Conger et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2010.00725.x)), and adult children's financial dependence predicts parental ambivalence ([Pillemer & Suitor 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00602.x)). [M]
- The dose of money matters, not only its meaning: \$333 a month produced no developmental or maternal mental-health benefits by age 4 ([Noble et al. 2025](https://www.nber.org/papers/w33844); [Duncan et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamapediatrics.2025.1612)), while larger casino transfers did help ([Akee et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1257/app.2.1.86)). [M]
- Gap size versus harm: the larger father–adolescent sacrifice gap did not predict achievement motivation; only the mother–adolescent gap did ([Leung & Shek 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-015-9332-4)). [P]
- Stereotyped "cultural codes" are unreliable: Japanese valued direct verbalization with parents *more* than Americans ([Seki et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022102033003006)); Koreans and Japanese were not less collectivistic than European Americans ([Oyserman et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.1.3)). [M]
- Gender: similarities outweigh differences in giving and responding to support ([MacGeorge et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1023/b:sers.0000015549.88984.8d)). [M]

**Worked example: a father who equates love with provision and sacrifice, and a daughter who prioritizes emotional presence and being understood**
- The father's script (provision and sacrifice = love) is well documented as a cultural and historical pattern. Provider-role theory ([Christiansen & Palkovitz 2001](https://doi.org/10.1177/019251301022001004)), parental sacrifice as a cultural form of care ([Wu & Chao 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/aap0000092); [Chao & Kaeochinda 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4419-5728-3_4)) and fathers' supportive rather than verbal affection ([Morman & Floyd 1999](https://doi.org/10.1080/10510979909388501)) all describe it.
  - Grade: [M] as a group pattern; [SPEC] for any individual father.
- The daughter's priority matches what predicts outcomes almost everywhere: perceived warmth and acceptance ([Khaleque & Rohner 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022111406120)) and perceived emotional support ([Chentsova Dutton et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)). Adult daughters' recalled paternal acceptance is a relatively strong correlate of their adjustment ([Ali et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022115597754)). [M/P]
- Where translation plausibly fails, by link in the chain:
  - Expressed → perceived: provision is low-salience. Parents and children diverge in how much sacrifice they see ([Leung & Shek 2016](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12187-015-9332-4)).
  - Perceived → received: the daughter's ideal warmth exceeds the warmth she perceives ([Wu & Chao 2005](https://doi.org/10.1080/01650250500147444)).
  - Support-seeking mismatch: implicit vs explicit support norms ([Kim, Sherman & Taylor 2008](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1037/0003-066X)).
  - Developmental non-updating of the father's model of his child (expectancy violation–realignment; [Hadiwijaya et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-016-0627-7)).
  - Grade: [T/SPEC] as applied to one dyad.

### Inferences
- A refined, testable version of the hypothesis consistent with this evidence: translation matters most for *peripheral, culturally variable signals of care*: provision, sacrifice, control, verbal "I love you," and solicited or explicit emotional support. *Core acceptance cues* (attentive responsiveness and the absence of coldness, hostility and indifference) appear largely shared across cultures and drive most of the outcome variance. [inference]
- What would falsify translation in the worked example:
  1. The daughter accurately recognizes her father's provision as love yet still feels unaccepted, because of coldness, hostility or indifference (the PARQ dimensions). That is a problem of quality or amount, not of translation.
  2. Coaching the father to add verbal affection, without increasing attentiveness, leaves her perceived acceptance unchanged.
  3. Longitudinal data show her distress preceding her low perceived paternal warmth (a child effect).
  4. The mismatch disappears once family economic stress is resolved (a stress explanation).
- What would support it:
  1. Experimentally or longitudinally, *reframing* the father's provision as care (e.g., through appreciation of sacrifice, as in Wu & Chao's *qin* buffering) raises her perceived acceptance and lowers her distress without any change in his behaviour.
  2. The father's intended acceptance (from a parent-report PARQ) is high, while her perceived acceptance is low only on dimensions carried by culturally variable signals.
- Do not generalize from cultural averages to this father or daughter, or to their family, without individual-level measurement (Q12).

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures all links of the chain (intended, expressed, perceived, received, compatibility) in parent–child dyads across cultures.
- No intervention study was found that tests reframing provision or sacrifice as love against changing behaviour.
- Father–daughter-specific cross-cultural data are very scarce.
