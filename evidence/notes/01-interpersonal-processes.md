# Interpersonal processes: how care is given, perceived and received in close relationships (evidence dossier for the "love lost in translation" hypothesis)

Scope: perceived partner responsiveness (PPR), the intimacy process model, social support (enacted/received vs perceived, invisible support, matching, over/under-provision), sacrifice, gratitude/appreciation, capitalization, and communication/conflict/repair, including couple-intervention evidence. Love languages, attachment, need fulfilment, empathic accuracy/attribution, abuse/coercion, family/cultural (incl. Korean) research and formal modelling are covered by other researchers and are touched on only where unavoidable.

Conventions used throughout:
- Grades: **[Established]** = meta-analytic or multiply replicated with consistent direction; **[Moderate/mixed]** = several studies, or consistent but with notable inconsistency, small samples, or single-lab dependence; **[Preliminary]** = single study, conference abstract, preprint, or secondary-source-only verification; **[Theory]** = theoretical proposition/review claim not itself an empirical test.
- **WEIRD flag** = sample from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic societies (US, Canada, NZ, Western Europe). Country is marked "not stated" when the accessible abstract/text did not give it.
- **Causal ID** notes how well causation is identified (RCT > lab experiment with manipulation > within-person longitudinal/diary > between-person longitudinal > cross-sectional).
- "Measured" vs "authors infer" is separated where the distinction matters.
- Verification: every DOI below was resolved in this session against OpenAlex, Crossref, PubMed or Europe PMC unless marked UNVERIFIED (full ledger in the final section). Abstract-level content was read from those databases; full-text passages (methods/results sentences) were extracted where the article is on PubMed Central (Johnson et al. 2022; Slatcher et al. 2015; Selcuk et al. 2016; Stanton et al. 2019).
- Hypothesis being tested (to be falsified if possible): "Love intended ≠ love expressed ≠ love perceived ≠ love received ≠ relationship compatibility; many relationship problems come from the translation of care between people rather than from the amount of love."

## Q1. Perceived partner responsiveness (PPR): definition, strongest longitudinal evidence, and how much PPR reflects the partner's enacted responsiveness vs the perceiver's projection

### Takeaway
PPR (feeling understood, validated and cared for) is a robust correlate and a modest prospective predictor of relationship and personal well-being, but its long-term effects on health and well-being in the flagship MIDUS cohort are small and partly fragile to covariates. PPR tracks the partner's enacted behaviour only partially: in dyadic studies, the perceiver's own responsiveness (projection) predicts PPR as strongly or more strongly than the partner's self-reported responsiveness, which complicates any "translation" account that treats perception as a decoding of the partner's signal.

### Cited Findings

**Definition and operationalisation**
- The Perceived Partner Responsiveness Scale (PPRS) is a self-report of how responsive partners have been; its authors define responsiveness through two properties, understanding and validation, and report excellent psychometric properties (chapter-level claim; item-level psychometrics not accessed) — [Reis, Crasta, Rogge, Maniaci & Carmichael 2017, PPRS chapter](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119102991.ch57). [Theory/measurement]
- MIDUS (the dataset behind Selcuk 2016, Slatcher 2015 and Stanton 2019) measures PPR with only three items (how much the partner really cares, understands the way you feel, appreciates you), which the authors map onto the understanding/validation/caring components of Reis et al. (2004) — [Selcuk et al. 2016, PMC full text](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12272); [Stanton et al. 2019, PMC full text](https://doi.org/10.1097/PSY.0000000000000618).
- When 246 candidate items from 19 existing PPR measures were factor-analysed (n = 2,334), the structure that emerged was two valence-based dimensions, Responsiveness and Insensitivity, not three theoretical components; IRT produced two 8-item subscales (the PRI) with incremental prediction over global satisfaction — [Crasta, Rogge, Maniaci & Reis 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000986). [Moderate/mixed: single development paper]
- Reis & Gable's review "Responsiveness" exists as cited (Current Opinion in Psychology 1:67–71) but its content was not accessible in this session — [Reis & Gable 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2015.01.001).

**Longitudinal/health evidence (all MIDUS, US, WEIRD; observational; no causal ID)**
- Selcuk et al. (2016): >2,000 married US adults over ~10 years (longitudinal models N = 2,348; cross-sectional N = 4,167; diary subsample N = 555, per table notes). Cross-sectionally, PPR was associated with hedonic (B = 0.322) and eudaimonic well-being (B = 0.311; standardized), shrinking to B = 0.126 and 0.088 after covariates. Prospectively, PPR predicted change in eudaimonic well-being after all covariates (B = 0.057, SE = 0.020, p = .004) but NOT change in hedonic well-being (B = 0.027, p = .172). Daily affective reactivity partially mediated — [Selcuk, Gunaydin, Ong & Almeida 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12272) (full text: [PMC5458635](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5458635/)). [Moderate/mixed: one cohort, 3-item measure, null for hedonic outcome]
- Slatcher et al. (2015): N = 1,078 adults (95.1% White). Wave-1 PPR predicted higher waking cortisol and steeper (healthier) diurnal slopes ~10 years later, explaining 2.27% of variance in slope and 0.5% in waking cortisol; concurrent Wave-2 PPR was unrelated to cortisol; declines in negative affect mediated. PPR correlated only r = .10 with the person's own hours of emotional support provision, and support provision independently predicted steeper slopes — [Slatcher, Selcuk & Ong 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615575022) (full text: [PMC4504783](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4504783/)). [Preliminary/Moderate: single study, small effect]
- Stanton et al. (2019): N = 1,208 over 20 years. The indirect path from absolute Wave-1 PPR to mortality via negative-affect reactivity did NOT survive covariates (β = .004, 95% CI −.03 to .04); declines/increases in PPR between Waves 1 and 2 predicted negative-affect reactivity, which predicted mortality (β = −.04, 95% CI −.09 to −.002). Waves 1–2 PPR correlated r = .51 — [Stanton, Selcuk, Farrell, Slatcher & Ong 2019](https://doi.org/10.1097/PSY.0000000000000618) (full text: [PMC6298854](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6298854/)). [Preliminary: indirect effect with CI bound near zero]
- Cross-cultural boundary: in US (n = 3,079) and Japanese (n = 861) married/cohabiting adults, PPR positively predicted hedonic and eudaimonic well-being in both countries, but more weakly in Japan, especially for eudaimonic well-being; held after demographic and personality controls (cross-sectional) — [Tasfiliz et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000378). [Moderate: large but cross-sectional]
- A Chinese-language PPRS (C-PPRS) was constructed rather than translated because some original wording was judged culturally inappropriate (Study 1 n = 441, EFA/CFA) — [Zhou, Cai, Huang & Chen 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-022-00865-x). [Preliminary]

**How much of PPR is the partner's actual responsiveness vs the perceiver's projection?**
- Lemay, Clark & Feeney (2007), two dyadic marriage studies plus an experiment (country not stated in abstract): spouses' own self-reported responsiveness to the partner predicted their perception of the partner's responsiveness MORE strongly than the partner's self-reported responsiveness did; projected perceptions in turn predicted the perceiver's satisfaction, independent of attachment, self-esteem, depression and communal orientation; a diary component showed projection of daily enacted support onto perceived benefits received; and in Study 3 experimentally making it feel difficult to recall one's own support provision reduced perceived partner responsiveness (causal evidence for projection, lab analogue) — [Lemay, Clark & Feeney 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.5.834). [Moderate: replicated across studies within paper and by other labs below; coefficients not extracted]
- Lemay & Clark (2008), five studies: manipulating a person's felt communal responsiveness toward a partner (Study 1) or new acquaintance (Study 2) shifted their perception of that person's responsiveness to them; projected perceptions predicted self-disclosure, evaluation and support provision, and predicted longitudinal change in the perceiver's own responsiveness toward friends and spouses (Studies 3–5) — [Lemay & Clark 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.4.647). [Moderate]
- Debrot et al. (2012): 102 young heterosexual dating couples in Switzerland (WEIRD), both partners sampled 4×/day for one week. Measured: partner's own report of enacted responsive acts; perceiver's PPR; intimacy. The partner's enacted responsiveness predicted the perceiver's PPR (a real transmission path), BUT the perceiver's own enacted responsiveness also predicted their PPR (projection); PPR mediated both effects on intimacy and also predicted the partner's intimacy — [Debrot, Cook, Perrez & Horn 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028666). [Moderate; within-person diary, no causal ID]
- Canevello & Crocker (2010): roommate dyads (115 dyads over a semester; 65 dyads over 3 weeks; country not stated in abstract). Compassionate goals predicted cycles of responsiveness that operated through BOTH intrapersonal projection and reciprocal interpersonal processes, improving both people's relationship quality — [Canevello & Crocker 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018186). [Moderate]
- In 161 heterosexual couples, PRI subscales were associated with the partner's self-reported responsive and insensitive behaviours even after controlling for the perceiver's own behaviours (APIM), i.e., there is a detectable partner-behaviour signal in PPR beyond projection — [Crasta et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000986). [Moderate]
- Observer-coded responsive behaviours during disclosure conversations (79 US dating couples) were associated with disclosers' post-interaction perceived responsiveness — [Maisel, Gable & Strachman 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2008.00201.x). [Moderate]
- Review conclusion: people's perceptions of being understood are only modestly related to actually being understood by others — [Reis, Lemay & Finkenauer 2017](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12308). [Moderate: review]
- Pooled machine-learning analysis of 43 dyadic longitudinal datasets from 29 labs: actor-reported variables predicted 2–4 times more variance in relationship quality than partner-reported variables; partner reports (and individual differences) had NO predictive effects beyond the actor's own relationship-specific perceptions; top predictors were perceived-partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived-partner satisfaction and conflict; relationship-specific variables predicted up to 45% of variance at baseline but change in relationship quality was largely unpredictable — [Joel et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117). [Established for prediction from self-reports; not causal; total N not extracted; overlaps the modelling researcher's scope]
- Preregistered preprint (100 couples, 14-day diary, 2-month follow-up): actors' relationship mindfulness predicted their own PPR and own relationship-quality change; no partner effects emerged in any analysis — [Stanton, Chan & Gazder 2021 (PsyArXiv preprint)](https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/zd639). [Preliminary: not peer reviewed at time of record]
- A review proposes high-quality listening as one behavioural route to PPR (theoretical model, not a new test) — [Itzchakov, Reis & Weinstein 2022](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12648). [Theory]

### Inferences
- Supports one link of the hypothesis: "love expressed ≠ love perceived" is real; the partner's enacted responsiveness registers in PPR only partially (Debrot 2012; Crasta 2021 APIM partner effects; Reis et al. 2017).
- Contradicts the specific "translation" mechanism: the strongest single predictor of perceived responsiveness in the Lemay studies is the perceiver's own responsiveness. A large part of "love perceived" is therefore a function of the perceiver's own love (a perceiver-side "amount of love" effect), not a sender-to-receiver encoding/decoding failure. Projection also means that in loving perceivers perception can be positively biased, i.e., the perception gap may often favour the relationship rather than harm it.
- The MIDUS health findings cannot be read as "the partner's care, once correctly received, improves health": PPR there is a 3-item self-perception confounded with perceiver traits, effects are small (≈2% variance), and some paths did not survive covariates.
- Joel et al.'s finding that partner reports add nothing beyond the actor's perceptions is compatible with two opposite readings: (a) everything the partner does matters only through how it is perceived (consistent with a "reception" emphasis), or (b) perceptions are largely self-generated and disconnected from the partner's behaviour (inconsistent with a "transmission" emphasis). The data cannot distinguish them.

### Gaps
- Exact actor (projection) vs partner (accuracy) coefficients for Lemay et al. 2007, Debrot et al. 2012 and Crasta et al. 2021 Study 3 were not extracted (paywalled full texts).
- No meta-analysis of PPR and well-being/health (2020–2026) was found; a search returned only a 2023 book chapter and primary MIDUS studies.
- No variance decomposition of PPR into perceiver/partner/relationship components exists for romantic couples (a monogamous dyad gives each perceiver only one partner, so social-relations-model decomposition is not identifiable).
- Item count and psychometrics of the PPRS (chapter) were not verified; no Korean validation of PPRS or PRI was located (Crossref search) — defer to the cultural researcher.

## Q2. Intimacy process model (Reis & Shaver 1988; Laurenceau et al. 1998, 2005): what has been tested, how, and with what results

### Takeaway
Diary studies support the core claim that disclosure (own and partner's) predicts momentary intimacy and that perceived partner responsiveness partially mediates this; dyadic and observational studies show partner behaviour feeds into perceived responsiveness. But the tests are mostly within-interaction self-reports by the same person, mediation is only partial, and experimental tests of the full sequence in established couples are rare.

### Cited Findings
- The model (Reis & Shaver 1988, a book chapter without DOI) posits that both self-disclosure and partner responsiveness contribute to intimacy — as summarized in [Laurenceau, Barrett & Pietromonaco 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1238). [Theory]
- Laurenceau et al. (1998): two event-contingent diary studies (participants reported immediately after social interactions for 1 week and 2 weeks; N and country not stated in abstract). Measured: self-disclosure, partner disclosure, PPR and intimacy, all reported by the same participant per interaction. Self- and partner disclosure predicted intimacy, with PPR as a PARTIAL mediator; emotional self-disclosure predicted intimacy more than factual disclosure (Study 2) — [Laurenceau et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.74.5.1238). [Moderate; no causal ID; shared-method variance]
- Laurenceau, Barrett & Rovine (2005): both partners of 96 married couples, 42 consecutive days. Self-disclosure and partner disclosure uniquely predicted same-day intimacy; PPR partially mediated both; global marital satisfaction, relationship intimacy and demand–withdraw communication were related to daily intimacy — [Laurenceau, Barrett & Rovine 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.19.2.314). [Moderate; concurrent within-day associations]
- Dyadic test using the partner's report of enacted responsiveness (102 Swiss dating couples): partner's enacted responsiveness → perceiver's PPR → intimacy, with a parallel projection path from the perceiver's own enacted responsiveness — [Debrot et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028666). [Moderate]
- Observer-coded behaviour: in 79 dating couples, outside observers' codes of responders' behaviour to disclosed positive/negative events, and disclosers' ratings of feeling understood/validated/cared for, predicted relationship well-being and break-up 2 months later; responses to POSITIVE events were more predictive than responses to negative events — [Gable, Gonzaga & Strachman 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.904); responsive-behaviour coding systems linked to post-interaction PPR — [Maisel et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2008.00201.x). [Moderate]
- Experimental component (strangers and diary): four experiments and one diary study found that enthusiastic responses to shared good news increase the perceived value of the event and build trust and prosocial orientation toward the responder — [Reis et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018344). [Moderate; causal for stranger interactions]
- Perception can be moved without changing the partner's behaviour: manipulating the perceiver's own felt responsiveness changed perceived partner responsiveness — [Lemay & Clark 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.4.647). [Moderate]

### Inferences
- What has been shown well: momentary covariation of disclosure, perceived responsiveness and intimacy within persons; partial mediation through PPR; some transmission from partner-reported or observer-coded behaviour to PPR.
- What has not been shown well: that changing the partner's responsive behaviour in established couples causes later intimacy via PPR (no RCT located), or how much of the "responsiveness" link reflects the partner's actual response vs the perceiver's construal. The model is compatible with "translation" (behaviour must be perceived to matter) but its evidence base largely measures the perception end only.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and countries for Laurenceau et al. 1998 were not in the accessible abstract.
- No randomized manipulation of partner responsiveness within long-term couples with intimacy outcomes was located; no non-WEIRD test of the full model was located.

## Q3. Received vs perceived support: their correlation, why perceived support predicts health better, and how much perceived supportiveness is due to perceiver, provider, and the unique perceiver × provider relationship

### Takeaway
Self-reported received (enacted) support and perceived support correlate only r ≈ .35 (≈12% shared variance). Generalizability and social-relations-model studies indicate that judgments of a provider's supportiveness are driven mostly by the specific perceiver × provider pairing and by perceiver tendencies, with the provider's general supportiveness contributing least; in families, perceiver effects exceed partner effects. Relational regulation theory reinterprets the "relational" share as matching of personal taste in ordinary conversation, not as stress-related support transactions.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 23 studies using the Inventory of Socially Supportive Behaviors (ISSB) and any perceived-support measure: mean r = .35 between received and perceived support — [Haber, Cohen, Lucas & Baltes 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10464-007-9100-9). [Established for this measure pairing; limited to ISSB studies through ~2006]
- Uchino argues that perceived and received support are separable constructs with distinct antecedent processes across the life span, and that their links to health should be studied separately by disease type and stage — [Uchino 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2009.01122.x). [Theory/review]
- Diary research on support transactions had failed to show that actual receipt of support is associated with adjustment to stressors — as summarized in [Bolger, Zuckerman & Kessler 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953); receipt of support from close others is often associated with negative outcomes even though perceived availability is associated with positive outcomes — as summarized in [Maisel & Gable 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02388.x). [Moderate/mixed]
- Relational regulation theory (RRT): the main effect of perceived support on mental health arises from ordinary, affectively consequential conversations and shared activities, not from conversations about coping with stress; which people regulate a recipient is mostly a matter of the recipient's personal taste (i.e., relational) — [Lakey & Orehek 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023477). [Theory with supporting G-studies]
- Three generalizability studies in which multiple perceivers rated the same targets: perceivers, supporters and their interaction all contributed significantly; the Perceiver × Supporter interaction contributed the most variance, then supporter characteristics, then perceiver biases (an erratum was published, JPSP 71(2):210) — [Lakey, McCabe, Fisicaro & Drew 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.70.6.1270) (PubMed record: [8667167](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8667167/)). [Moderate; exact percentages not extracted]
- Social relations model in 288 Dutch families (two parents, two adolescents rating support from each other; WEIRD): support perceptions were MORE determined by the perceiver than by the partner, and partly relationship-, generation- and family-specific; relationship-specific support and reciprocity were more important in horizontal (marital, sibling) than vertical (parent–child) relationships; adolescents' perceptions were more perceiver-determined than parents' — [Branje, van Aken & van Lieshout 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.16.3.351) (abstract via [PubMed 12238416](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12238416/)). [Moderate; exact percentages not extracted]
- Perceived support primarily reflects relationships among specific recipients and providers; across two studies, which providers a recipient would find unusually supportive could be forecast from the recipient's reactions to one brief conversation, even 4 months later — [Veenstra et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01340.x). [Moderate/Preliminary]
- Conference abstract, round-robin of 100 US Marine Corps reservists rating each other within 4-person teams: 54% of variance in perceived support and 67% of variance in conversation quality was relational; relational perceived support correlated r = .70 with relational conversation quality — [Lakey & Andrews 2014, APS abstract](https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/fsdg/858). [Preliminary: not peer reviewed]
- Conference abstract, 16 groups of 4 student flatmates (SRM): relationship effects accounted for over 52% of variance across perceived support, ordinary conversation and shared activity; controlling ordinary conversation and shared activity reduced the variance in positive affect explained by perceived support from 35% to 3% — [Lakey 2015, APS abstract](https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/fsdg/542). [Preliminary]
- Dyadic coping meta-analysis (72 samples, 57 reports, N = 17,856): total dyadic coping correlated r = .45 with relationship satisfaction, regardless of gender, age, relationship length, education and nationality; perceptions of the PARTNER's dyadic coping (and of joint coping) predicted satisfaction more strongly than perceptions of one's own dyadic coping; positive forms predicted more than negative forms — [Falconier, Jackson, Hilpert & Bodenmann 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2015.07.002). [Established as an association; cross-sectional single-reporter dominated]
- Non-WEIRD dyadic sample (300 Pakistani married couples, APIMeM): gratitude in response to a partner's positive dyadic coping partially mediated the coping–satisfaction link; actor (own-perception) indirect effects were stronger than cross-partner effects — [Shujja, Bodenmann, Randall, Adil & Malik 2022](https://doi.org/10.1017/sjp.2022.28). [Preliminary]

### Inferences
- Partly SUPPORTS the hypothesis's "compatibility" link: the largest variance component in perceived supportiveness is the unique pairing (fit), not the provider's general supportiveness. A provider who is supportive "in general" is not reliably perceived as supportive by everyone.
- Partly CONTRADICTS the "translation of care" mechanism: (i) perceiver effects (a recipient's tendency to see everyone as supportive) are large and, in families, larger than provider effects (Branje 2002); (ii) RRT attributes the relational share to shared taste in ordinary conversation/activities, i.e., compatibility of people, not successful transmission of supportive acts; (iii) received support explains only ≈12% of perceived support.
- Methodological caution: in generalizability/SRM designs, relationship variance is confounded with measurement error unless multiple indicators or occasions are used; relational percentages from single-indicator designs are upper bounds.
- None of these decompositions is from romantic couples per se (teams, flatmates, families, rated targets), so transferring the percentages to romantic love is an extrapolation.

### Gaps
- The exact variance-component percentages in Lakey et al. 1996, Branje et al. 2002 and Veenstra et al. 2011 could not be extracted (paywalled; not in abstracts). Secondary summaries surfaced in search attribute to a Lakey (2010) review figures of 21–72% relational variance (≈62% on average in real-world samples), ≈27% perceiver and ≈7% target variance, but the primary text could not be accessed, so these figures are UNVERIFIED and should not be cited without checking Lakey (2010) directly.
- Lakey et al. (2010, PSPB) on enacted support was not retrievable (fetch failed), so its numbers are not reported.

## Q4. Invisible support (Bolger et al. 2000; Bolger & Amarel 2007; Girme et al. 2013, 2018): when support works better unseen, and the costs

### Takeaway
In acute-stress contexts, support that the recipient does not notice, or that is delivered subtly, tends to avoid the emotional costs of visible support (diary and experimental evidence); but visible support helps highly distressed recipients and raises same-day relationship satisfaction, while invisible support's relational benefits appear only later and it cannot be credited (no gratitude). Whether support is seen matters less than whether it is responsive and meets the recipient's regulatory needs.

### Cited Findings
- Bolger, Zuckerman & Kessler (2000): daily diaries of couples in which one partner faced a major stressor (New York bar exam; US, WEIRD). Many support transactions reported by providers were NOT reported by recipients; these invisible transactions predicted better adjustment, consistent with an emotional cost of being aware of receiving support — [Bolger et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953). [Moderate; diary, no causal ID] The same bar-exam diary paradigm (68 recent law graduates and partners) showed that partner-reported practical support reduced examinee fatigue and increased vigour, whereas recipients' recognition of emotional support was associated with more anger, anxiety and depressed mood — [Shrout, Herman & Bolger 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2006.00108.x). [Moderate]
- Bolger & Amarel (2007): three experiments, N = 257 female participants (US) anticipating a stressful speech; a confederate gave visible or invisible support. Invisible support (practical and emotional) reduced emotional reactivity relative to visible support and no support; visible support was ineffective or exacerbated reactivity; support was effective when it avoided communicating a sense of inefficacy to the recipient — [Bolger & Amarel 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.458). [Moderate; causal for the lab analogue; women only; strangers]
- Girme, Overall & Simpson (2013): 61 couples, video-recorded goal discussions with coded visible and invisible support, then goal achievement every 3 months for a year (country not stated in abstract; authors NZ/US-based). Visible emotional support increased perceived support and discussion success for HIGHLY distressed recipients but was costly (lower discussion success) for non-distressed recipients; invisible emotional support was unrelated to perceived support or discussion success but predicted greater goal achievement over the year — [Girme et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167213497802). [Moderate/Preliminary; small N; observational]
- Girme et al. (2018): three dyadic daily-diary studies, N = 322 married couples. Visible support → greater same-day relationship satisfaction AND greater same-day anxiety; invisible support → no same-day effects (no mood cost) but greater recipient relationship satisfaction the NEXT day, explained (Study 3) by greater next-day satisfaction with the partner's helpful behaviours and shared interactions — [Girme et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000453) ([PMC6205907](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6205907/)). [Moderate]
- Maisel & Gable (2009): 67 cohabiting couples, daily reports from both partners. BOTH visible and invisible support were beneficial (less sadness and anxiety, better relationship quality) ONLY when the support was responsive — [Maisel & Gable 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02388.x). [Moderate]
- Synthesis/extension: invisible support circumvents negative effects that can follow direct, recognized help; the key questions are how visibility varies, why invisible support helps and when visible support is needed — [Zee & Bolger 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721419835214). Eight studies plus an internal meta-analysis found that support high on "regulatory effectiveness" (helping recipients understand their situation and feel capable of managing it) predicted better mood and motivation, and these effects were STRONGER than effects of perceived responsiveness — [Zee, Bolger & Higgins 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000235). [Moderate]
- Missed acts of care elicit no gratitude and lower the giver's satisfaction (sacrifice data; see Q6) — [Visserman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599). [Moderate]

### Inferences
- Invisible support is the cleanest empirical case where "love received" (benefit) dissociates from "love perceived": benefit can occur without perception. This SUPPORTS the hypothesis's claim that these are distinct stages, but CONTRADICTS any version holding that perception is the necessary channel through which care helps.
- The cost of visible support appears to lie in what the act signals (inefficacy, dependence), so it is partly a meaning problem about the act. That is translation in a broad sense, but in the opposite direction from the hypothesis: the trouble is being received too clearly, not being lost.
- Likely long-term trade-off: unseen care cannot build perceived availability, gratitude or credit for the giver (Girme 2013 null for perceived support; Visserman 2019), so a relationship running mostly on invisible support may under-credit the giver. Direct long-term tests in couples are scarce.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of support visibility effects was located. The Howland & Simpson (2010) Psychological Science paper on observed invisible support could not be verified (recalled DOI 10.1177/0956797610389185 returned 404) and is not used.
- Sample sizes per study for Girme 2018 and the country of several samples were not extracted; nearly all samples are WEIRD; the experimental evidence uses strangers and women only.

## Q5. Support matching and adequacy: optimal matching, Cutrona et al. 2007, over- vs under-provision (Brock & Lawrence 2009, 2014), skilled support, "mixed blessing", responsiveness as moderator, gender (Neff & Karney 2005)

### Takeaway
Fit between the support given and the support wanted matters: in one 5-year newlywed sample, both under- and over-provision independently predicted declining satisfaction, and over-provision was the bigger risk. Matching evidence for emotional support is reasonable but weak to partial for informational support. Received support is a mixed blessing (closeness up, distress up), and its benefits depend on responsiveness and on meeting the recipient's regulatory needs. Gender differences lie in the timing/responsiveness of support, not in skill.

### Cited Findings
- Optimal matching theory (Cutrona & Russell 1990, a book chapter without DOI) predicts that support matching the stressed person's needs is most beneficial — as summarized in [Cutrona, Shaffer, Wesner & Gardner 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.21.4.754). [Theory]
- Early test: 60 married individuals disclosed stressors to their spouse; coded with the Social Support Behavior Code (SSBC). Informational support was given most when the stressed person had high control over the event, but of five support types only information was evaluated differently by controllability: limited support for optimal matching — [Cutrona & Suhr 1992](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365092019002002). [Preliminary/mixed]
- Cutrona et al. (2007): 59 married couples, videotaped self-disclosure task. Emotional support following emotional disclosure (a match) predicted perceived partner sensitivity; mismatched support after emotional disclosure predicted lower marital satisfaction via lower perceived sensitivity; matching informational support after a request for information did NOT predict perceived sensitivity, though negative responses (criticism, sarcasm) to information requests did predict lower sensitivity. Authors call this "partial evidence" — [Cutrona et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.21.4.754). [Moderate/mixed; small N; cross-sectional]
- Brock & Lawrence (2009): 103 newlywed couples (country not stated in abstract; US-based authors), 5 assessments over the first 5 years, APIM and growth curves. Increases in under-provision (less support than desired) and over-provision (more than desired) were each uniquely associated with declines in marital satisfaction; over-provision was the GREATER risk; for each of four support types (informational, emotional, esteem, tangible) over-provision was at least as detrimental as under-provision — [Brock & Lawrence 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015402). [Moderate/Preliminary: single sample; perception-based measure; effect sizes not extracted]
- Brock & Lawrence (2014), same sample: over-provision was greater when husbands were higher in avoidant attachment, husbands reported more chronic role strain, and couples had lower emotional intimacy (intimacy partially mediated husbands' avoidance); risk factors for over-provision differed from those for under-provision — [Brock & Lawrence 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035280). [Preliminary]
- Perceived support adequacy (SIRRS) recorded daily for 7 days by 177 married individuals from a college sample predicted unique variance in marital quality, depressive symptoms and perceived stress, controlling for social desirability — [Dehle, Larsen & Landers 2001](https://doi.org/10.1080/01926180152588725); factor analysis across three dating/married samples produced a 4-factor received-support structure (esteem/emotional, physical comfort, informational, tangible) with incremental longitudinal validity — [Barry, Bunde, Brock & Lawrence 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014174). [Moderate as measurement evidence]
- Skilled support (review): support attempts are often unskilled and can cause feelings of inadequacy, indebtedness and inequity; four ways dyadic support goes wrong: timing, content, process and reciprocation — [Rafaeli & Gleason 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1756-2589.2009.00003.x). [Theory]
- "Mixed blessing": in a large 31-day daily diary study of couples leading up to a major stressor, daily support receipt was associated with BOTH greater closeness and greater negative mood; average effects masked large heterogeneity (recipients with larger closeness benefits showed smaller mood costs and vice versa); self-esteem moderated only for a subset — [Gleason, Iida, Shrout & Bolger 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.5.824). [Moderate; N not extracted]
- Responsiveness as moderator: received support (visible or invisible) was beneficial only when responsive — [Maisel & Gable 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02388.x); support addressing regulatory needs (truth and control) outperformed perceived responsiveness in predicting self-regulatory outcomes — [Zee et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000235). [Moderate]
- Gender: observational and diary data from newlywed couples showed husbands and wives did not differ on average in the support they provided, but differed in timing: wives provided better support on days their husbands were more stressed, whereas on wives' high-stress days husbands showed both support and negativity — [Neff & Karney 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.88.1.79). [Moderate; N not extracted; US]
- Theoretical integration: support should be studied as an interpersonal process that promotes thriving both in coping with adversity and in pursuing opportunities, with two distinct relational support functions — [Feeney & Collins 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868314544222). [Theory]

### Inferences
- This cluster is the strongest SUPPORT for "fit/translation over amount": more care (over-provision) can hurt, emotional mismatch lowers perceived sensitivity, and the same support has opposite effects depending on responsiveness and regulatory fit.
- Caveats that weaken this support: the over/under-provision evidence is essentially one US sample of 103 couples; "adequacy" is itself a perception by the recipient (so the measure already contains the translation); matching effects for informational support are null or partial; Neff & Karney locate the problem in when support is given (responsiveness to need), which is closer to attention/motivation than to decoding.

### Gaps
- No independent replication of the Brock & Lawrence over-provision effect in another sample, and no meta-analysis of support matching, was located; effect sizes were not extracted.
- Sample sizes for Neff & Karney (2005) and Gleason et al. (2008) were not in the accessible abstracts.

## Q6. Sacrifice and perceived sacrifice: giver vs recipient effects (Righetti et al. 2020), motives (Impett et al. 2005; Visserman et al. 2018) and detection (Visserman et al. 2019)

### Takeaway
Willingness to sacrifice and satisfaction with sacrifice go with better well-being for both partners, but actual behavioural sacrifice is slightly negatively related to the giver's personal well-being and, per secondary coverage, unrelated to relationship well-being. Partners detect only about half of actual sacrifices, and about half of perceived sacrifices are "false alarms"; gratitude follows perceived, not actual, sacrifice. This is among the clearest evidence that care given and care received come apart, but what drives the recipient's response is the perception, accurate or not.

### Cited Findings
- Meta-analysis of 82 data sets, 9,547 effect sizes, N = 32,053: willingness to sacrifice was positively associated with the giver's personal and relationship well-being and with the PARTNER's relationship well-being (.09 < rs < .27); behavioural sacrifice was negatively associated with the giver's personal well-being (r = −.07); satisfaction with sacrifice was positively associated with own and partner well-being (.11 < rs < .43); costs of sacrifice were negatively associated with own personal and relationship well-being and the partner's relationship well-being (−.10 to −.26) — [Righetti, Sakaluk, Faure & Impett 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000297). [Established for associations; mostly correlational/diary primary studies]
- Secondary coverage of the same meta-analysis: data from nine countries; behavioural sacrifice did not relate to relationship well-being; associations of all four facets with well-being were more negative for women than men (authors suggest women may sacrifice more and receive less appreciation) — [BPS Research Digest, 2020](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-making-sacrifices-partner-or-saying-you-will-affects-wellbeing). [Moderate; secondary source]
- Impett, Gable & Peplau (2005): a measure of approach vs avoidance sacrifice motives (Study 1) and a 2-week daily experience study of college students in dating relationships (Study 2, with dyadic and longitudinal components; country not stated in abstract, US-based authors). Approach motives were positively, and avoidance motives negatively, associated with personal well-being and relationship quality; avoidance-motivated sacrifice was particularly detrimental to relationship maintenance over time; PERCEPTIONS of the partner's motives were also associated with well-being and relationship quality — [Impett et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.3.327). [Moderate]
- Visserman et al. (2018): two studies of romantic couples (N = 413; diaries and a lab conversation about a major sacrifice). Perceiving that the partner sacrificed for partner-focused approach motives (to promote one's well-being) evoked gratitude, partly via perceived partner responsiveness; perceived relationship-focused or self-focused motives (approach or avoidance) were NOT associated with gratitude — [Visserman, Righetti, Impett, Keltner & Van Lange 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000344). [Moderate]
- Visserman et al. (2019): two daily-experience studies of romantic couples (total N = 426), quasi-signal-detection analysis comparing the giver's report with the partner's perception. Sacrifices were as likely to be missed as accurately detected, and about half the time people "saw" a sacrifice when the partner reported none; seeing a sacrifice, accurately or not, boosted gratitude; missed sacrifices elicited no gratitude, and the lack of appreciation lowered the giver's relationship satisfaction on days they sacrificed — [Visserman, Impett, Righetti, Muise, Keltner & Van Lange 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599). The first author's lay summary specifies diaries of 8 or 14 days, a ~50% detection rate (the other ~50% missed) and ~50% of perceived sacrifices being false alarms — [Visserman 2019, SPSP Character & Context blog](https://spsp.org/news-center/character-context-blog/do-you-see-your-romantic-partners-sacrifices). [Moderate; two studies, same lab; countries not extracted]
- Willingness to sacrifice was originally conceptualized and measured in the investment-model tradition — [Van Lange et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.72.6.1373). [Measurement]

### Inferences
- This is direct, quantified SUPPORT for "love expressed ≠ love perceived" (≈50% misses) and for a downstream cost (unrecognized givers feel less satisfied).
- It also CONTRADICTS a pure "translation failure harms" reading: false alarms (perceiving sacrifice where the giver reports none) boost gratitude just as accurate detection does. Positively biased reception helps, so the problem is not inaccuracy as such but the direction of the error (missing care vs over-crediting it).
- The meta-analysis shows that the giver's motivation and attitude (willingness, satisfaction, low cost; approach motives) relate to both partners' well-being more than the sheer behaviour does. That fits "how care is given" (motives and how they are perceived) over "how much is given", but willingness to sacrifice is itself arguably an index of commitment/love, i.e., an "amount of love" variable.

### Gaps
- Exact hit/miss/false-alarm counts and gratitude coefficients for Visserman 2019 were not extracted from the paper (the accepted manuscript on Sussex Figshare could not be fetched; numbers come from the abstract and the author's blog).
- The partner-side (recipient) association for behavioural sacrifice in Righetti 2020 was not in the abstract; per the secondary source it was null for relationship well-being. Verify in the full text.

## Q7. Gratitude/appreciation and capitalization (Algoe 2012; Algoe, Gable & Maisel 2010; Gordon et al. 2012; Gable et al. 2004, 2006)

### Takeaway
Gratitude for a partner's thoughtful benefits predicts next-day connection and satisfaction for both recipient and benefactor. Feeling appreciated and appreciating the partner travel together and predict responsiveness, commitment and staying together. How gratitude is expressed matters: other-praising expressions are perceived as responsive. Responses to a partner's good news (capitalization) predict relationship well-being and break-up more strongly than responses to bad news. Evidence is mostly diary and observational, with experimental support from stranger paradigms.

### Cited Findings
- Find-remind-and-bind theory: gratitude functions to strengthen relationships with responsive partners, signals communal norms, and may fuel upward spirals of mutually responsive behaviour — [Algoe 2012](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1751-9004.2012.00439.x). [Theory]
- Daily experience sampling of both members of cohabiting couples: receipt of thoughtful benefits predicted both gratitude and indebtedness (men more mixed); gratitude predicted increases in relationship connection and satisfaction the next day for BOTH recipient and benefactor; indebtedness did not carry this promotive effect — [Algoe, Gable & Maisel 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01273.x). [Moderate; N not in abstract]
- Multimethod series that developed the Appreciation in Relationships (AIR) scale: people who feel more appreciated are more appreciative; appreciative people report more responsiveness (Study 1), are more committed and more likely to stay in the relationship over time (Study 2, longitudinal), and are rated by outside observers as more responsive and committed in lab interactions (Study 3) — [Gordon, Impett, Kogan, Oveis & Keltner 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028723). [Moderate]
- Lab gratitude-expression task in romantic couples: the target's perception of the expresser's responsiveness after the interaction predicted improvements in relationship quality over 6 months, independent of responsiveness perceived in two other emotionally evocative interactions — [Algoe, Fredrickson & Gable 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0032701). [Moderate]
- In an original study plus a conceptual replication (n = 370 across couples), the expresser's OTHER-PRAISING behaviour (vs self-benefit talk) was robustly associated with the benefactor's perception of the expresser's responsiveness, positive feelings and feeling loved — [Algoe, Kurtz & Hilaire 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616651681). [Moderate]
- Capitalization: across four studies, sharing positive events was associated with increased daily positive affect and well-being beyond the event itself, further enhanced when others were perceived to respond actively and constructively; relationships where partners typically respond enthusiastically had higher intimacy and daily marital satisfaction — [Gable, Reis, Impett & Asher 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228). [Moderate; largely perception-based]
- 79 dating couples (the Maisel et al. 2008 coding paper describes its 79 dating couples as U.S.; likely the same sample, not confirmed): self-reported and observer-coded responses to positive-event disclosures predicted relationship well-being and break-up 2 months later more closely than responses to negative-event disclosures — [Gable, Gonzaga & Strachman 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.904). [Moderate; small N]
- Experiments (mostly with strangers) plus a diary study: enthusiastic responses increase the perceived value of the shared event and build trust and prosocial orientation — [Reis et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018344); review/model of capitalization as an iterative, two-person process (InterCAP) — [Peters, Reis & Gable 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12407). [Moderate / Theory]

### Inferences
- Supportive of "how" over "how much": what is expressed when thanking (other-praise) and how a partner responds to good news (active-constructive) predict outcomes beyond whether a benefit occurred.
- Contradicting or complicating: gratitude appears to require the benefit to be perceived (see Q6), and appreciation correlates with commitment (Gordon 2012), so gratitude may be as much a marker of love/commitment as a channel of translation. Reverse causation (satisfied people feel and express more gratitude) is not excluded by the diary designs.

### Gaps
- No meta-analysis of gratitude in romantic relationships or of capitalization responses was located in this session. N and country for Algoe 2010, Gordon 2012 and Algoe 2013 were not extracted.

## Q8. Communication, conflict and repair: direction of effects, meta-analytic magnitudes, and intervention evidence relevant to "translation"

### Takeaway
Communication and satisfaction are robustly correlated, but longitudinal and within-couple analyses show weak and inconsistent prospective effects in either direction; concurrent covariation dominates. The demand/withdraw pattern (r ≈ .36 with outcomes) and observed hostility are reliable correlates of distress, and forgiveness tracks the offender's perceived intent, empathy and apology. Intervention evidence undercuts the idea that teaching communication skills is the key lever: skills-based education shows modest effects that vanish in some contexts (premarital with unpublished studies; low-income parents), a no-skills "relationship awareness" session matched skills programs in preventing break-up, and acceptance-based couple therapy did not beat behaviour-change therapy at 5 years.

### Cited Findings

**Direction of effects**
- Lavner, Karney & Bradbury (2016): newlyweds' observed positivity, negativity and effectiveness at four assessments 9 months apart. Satisfied couples communicated better cross-sectionally; longitudinally, reliable communication→satisfaction and satisfaction→communication associations were found but neither pathway was particularly robust; the authors say this raises important doubts about theories and interventions that prioritize communication skills — [Lavner et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12301). Per a later description, this sample was socioeconomically disadvantaged newlyweds, and only 7 of 36 longitudinal communication→satisfaction paths were significant, all small — [Johnson et al. 2022, PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8915221/). [Moderate; US low-income sample]
- Johnson et al. (2022): within-couple (random-intercept cross-lagged) analyses in three longitudinal studies: US unmarried couples (n = 316; 5 waves at 4-month intervals), Swiss couples (n = 365; annual for 5 years; observed and self-reported communication) and German pairfam partner pairs (baseline partner subsample of 3,743 pairs drawn from 12,402 anchors; first 5 annual waves). There were some lagged links between deviations in negative communication and later satisfaction (and vice versa), but the most robust finding was CONCURRENT: at times of less negative communication than usual, couples were more satisfied than usual. Positive communication was rarely associated with satisfaction within couples, and where consistent (Study 3) the coefficients were small (rs .05–.07) — [Johnson, Lavner, Mund, Zemp, Stanley, Neyer et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672211016920). [Established-to-Moderate; WEIRD (US, CH, DE); no causal ID]
- Decade review: negative communication is hard to change, changing it does not necessarily make relationships more satisfying, and it does not always predict distress; adaptive processes in middle-class couples may work differently in lower-income couples — [Karney & Bradbury 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12635). [Moderate: review]
- Counterpoint: among 136 couples who were stably highly satisfied over the first 4 years of marriage, those who divorced by 10 years had shown more negative communication, more negative emotion and more negative social-support behaviour as newlyweds, with no differences in satisfaction, commitment, stress, personality or positive behaviours — [Lavner & Bradbury 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0025966). [Moderate; observational; US]
- Counterpoint: in 172 newlywed couples over 8 waves/4 years, coded problem-solving skills, affect and their interaction predicted rates of change in satisfaction; high positive affect buffered the effect of negative skills — [Johnson et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.73.1.15). [Moderate]
- Context dependence: in two longitudinal newlywed studies, direct negative behaviours (blaming, commanding, rejecting) predicted steeper satisfaction declines when problems were minor, but MORE STABLE satisfaction when problems were severe (mediated by problem severity declining), whereas indirect negative communication predicted stably lower satisfaction regardless — [McNulty & Russell 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0017479). Direct opposition helps when serious problems need change and partners can respond, while affectionate/validating communication can be harmful when serious problems need change — [Overall & McNulty 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2016.03.002) ([PMC5181851](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5181851/)). [Moderate]

**Meta-analytic magnitudes**
- Demand/withdraw: 74 studies, N = 14,255; overall r = .360 with outcomes; wife-demand/husband-withdraw r = .380; husband-demand/wife-withdraw r = .392; distressed/clinical samples r = .413 vs non-distressed r = .345; relational outcomes r = .423, communicative r = .418, well-being r = .249 — [Schrodt, Witt & Shimkowski 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2013.813632). [Established association; mostly cross-sectional, mostly US]
- Observed conflict behaviour: 64 studies, 5,071 couples; hostility associated with lower satisfaction (medium effect), distress and withdrawal small effects, intimacy and problem solving positively associated (medium effects); gender differences small — [Woodin 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023791). [Established association]
- Forgiveness: 175 studies, 26,006 participants; strongest correlates were the offender's perceived intent (r = −.49), state empathy (r = .51), apology (r = .42) and state anger (r = −.41); situational constructs outweighed victim dispositions; gender (r = .01) and age (r = .06) negligible — [Fehr, Gelfand & Nag 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019993). [Established; not couple-specific]
- Observational prediction: in 130 newlywed couples followed 6 years, active listening (paraphrasing/validating) occurred very infrequently in conflict and did not predict marital outcomes, per the university's release on the study — [UW News, 20 Feb 1998](https://www.washington.edu/news/1998/02/20/husbands-willingness-to-be-influenced-by-wife-share-power-are-key-predictors-of-newlywed-happiness-stability-uw-study-shows/); article: [Gottman, Coan, Carrère & Swanson 1998](https://doi.org/10.2307/353438). The very high divorce-prediction accuracies from such studies fall precipitously under cross-validation — [Heyman & Slep 2001](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2001.00473.x). [Preliminary: the 1998 article's abstract/full text was not accessible; finding verified only via the press release]

**Interventions (randomized or meta-analytic)**
- Marriage and relationship education (MRE) meta-analysis: 86 reports, 117 studies, >500 effect sizes; experimental studies d = .30–.36 for relationship quality and d = .43–.45 for communication skills; published studies had larger communication effects at follow-up than unpublished ones; samples lacked racial/ethnic and economic diversity; stability rarely assessed — [Hawkins, Blanchard, Baldwin & Fawcett 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0012584). [Established for short-term effects in mostly WEIRD middle-class samples]
- Premarital education meta-analysis (47 studies): no improvement in relationship quality/satisfaction when unpublished studies are included, though communication improved (large effects with observational measures) — [Fawcett, Hawkins, Blanchard & Carroll 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2010.00598.x). [Moderate]
- Building Strong Families RCT (>4,000 low-income unmarried US couples, 8 sites, 3-year follow-up): no improvement in couple relationship, coparenting or father involvement, modest NEGATIVE effects on some outcomes, and little evidence of effects even among regular attenders — [Wood, Moore, Clarkwest & Killewald 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12094). [Established null for this program/population; RCT]
- Rogge et al. (2013), RCT with 174 engaged/newlywed couples: two 4-session, 15-hour small-group skills programs, PREP (conflict management and problem resolution skills) and CARE (acceptance, support and empathy skills), vs a 1-session relationship-awareness intervention (RA, no skills training) vs no treatment. 3-year dissolution: 24% no treatment vs 11% for PREP, CARE and RA, which did not differ. Unintended effects: wives in PREP showed slower declines in hostile conflict than wives in CARE, and both spouses in CARE showed faster declines in positive behaviours than in PREP. Authors conclude this casts doubt on unique benefits of skills training and raise the possibility that skills interventions sensitize couples to skill deficits — [Rogge, Cobb, Lawrence, Johnson & Bradbury 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034209). [Moderate: RCT, modest N; abstract compares couples "completing" interventions, so a completer bias is possible]
- IBCT vs TBCT RCT (134 seriously and chronically distressed married couples; country not stated in abstract, US-based trial team): end-of-treatment clinically significant improvement 71% IBCT vs 59% TBCT on the DAS; TBCT improved faster early then plateaued — [Christensen et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.72.2.176). At 5 years: pre-to-follow-up d = 1.03 (IBCT) vs 0.92 (TBCT); clinically significant improvement 50.0% vs 45.9%; separated/divorced 25.7% vs 27.9%; IBCT's superiority was significant through 2 years and then converged — [Christensen, Atkins, Baucom & Yi 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0018132). [Established for efficacy vs baseline; differences between approaches not durable]
- Mechanisms in the same trial: TBCT produced greater early change in the frequency of target behaviours, IBCT greater change in ACCEPTANCE of target behaviours early and late; behaviour-frequency change related strongly to satisfaction early in therapy, but emotional acceptance related more strongly in the second half — [Doss, Thum, Sevier, Atkins & Christensen 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.73.4.624). [Moderate; mediation in an RCT, mechanism not randomized]

### Inferences
- Reverse causation and common causes are live alternatives: the most reliable within-couple pattern is concurrent covariation, consistent with a third factor (stress, mood, love/commitment) moving both, or with satisfaction colouring how communication is enacted and reported.
- Interventions are the strongest test of a "fix the translation" account, and they disappoint: skills effects are modest and context-dependent, a no-skills awareness session prevented break-up as well as skills programs, and acceptance (changing reception) beat behaviour change only transiently. The acceptance finding fits "reception matters", but the 5-year convergence means reception-focused therapy is not demonstrably superior.
- Forgiveness is driven mostly by how the offence is construed (perceived intent, empathy) and by apology, a repair "message". That supports the importance of interpretation, but these are mostly cross-sectional and scenario/recall correlates.

### Gaps
- Sample size for Lavner et al. 2016 not extracted (described only as the same low-income newlywed sample used by Nguyen et al. 2020). The Gottman et al. 1998 primary text was not accessible.
- Content of the Rogge RA condition beyond "1 session, no skills training" (e.g., film-watching and discussion) was not verified from the abstract.

## Q9. The "intent vs impact" paradigm: what Gottman et al. (1976) actually found with the "talk table"

### Takeaway
In the talk-table studies, spouses rated the intended impact of their own messages and the actual impact of their partner's messages. Distressed and non-distressed couples reportedly did not differ in intent, but distressed spouses' messages were received as more negative than intended, a "communication deficit" pattern. This is the classic empirical anchor for a translation gap. However, it is a small 1970s laboratory study whose details could be verified only through secondary sources here, and it has no large modern replication.

### Cited Findings
- Bibliographic verification: Gottman, J., Notarius, C., Markman, H., Bank, S., Yoppi, B., & Rubin, M. E. (1976). Behavior exchange theory and marital decision making. JPSP, 34(1), 14–23 (six authors, including Rubin) — [Gottman et al. 1976](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.34.1.14).
- Couples were classified as distressed ("experiencing difficulties") or non-distressed ("mutually satisfying"), made decisions on high- and low-conflict tasks, and continuously rated the intended impact of their own behaviour and the impact of the spouse's behaviour on them. In Study 1, distressed and non-distressed spouses did not differ in intent, but distressed spouses' behaviour was received more negatively by their partners; in Study 2, distressed spouses' behaviour was likely to be coded by partners as far more negative than intended. The reciprocity hypothesis did not hold, and the data favoured a "bank account" model of non-distressed marriage (positives not contingent on the partner's immediately preceding behaviour) — [The Gottman Institute summary](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-behavior-exchange-theory-and-marital-decision-making/); consistent with a search-engine reading of the original PDF ([typeset.io copy](https://typeset.io/pdf/behavior-exchange-theory-and-marital-decision-making-10dygdl895.pdf); direct fetch of the PDF failed in this session). [Preliminary: small, dated lab study; primary statistics and N not verified; secondary source is promotional]

### Inferences
- The talk-table finding is direct support for "intended ≠ received" specifically in distressed couples. But it cannot establish direction: distress may cause negative reception (a sentiment-override reading, consistent with Q8's satisfaction→communication paths) rather than reception gaps causing distress. Intent ratings are self-reports and may be self-serving.
- The modern literature most comparable to this paradigm (projection in PPR, Q1; sacrifice detection, Q6) suggests that reception is shaped by the receiver's own state and sentiment, which aligns with the bank-account interpretation more than with a pure decoding-skill deficit.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes, rating scales and test statistics of Gottman et al. 1976 were not verified (PDF inaccessible; PsycNET and PubMed had no abstract). No large-sample modern replication of the intent/impact discrepancy in distressed vs non-distressed couples was located in this session.

## Q10. Where this literature contradicts or complicates the "translation" framing (perceiver effects, reverse causation, received support harming, the amount of love/commitment mattering)

### Takeaway
The literature supports that intended, expressed, perceived and received care are distinguishable. It does NOT support the strong claim that relationship problems arise mainly from translation rather than from the amount of love. Perceiver-side effects (projection; perceiver variance) are large; communication–satisfaction links are weak prospectively and plausibly reverse-causal; received support can harm even when accurately perceived; and indices of the amount of love and commitment (and their decline) are among the strongest predictors of relationship outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- **Amount of love/commitment predicts dissolution strongly.** Meta-analysis of 137 studies (37,761 participants) of non-marital break-up: commitment, love, inclusion of other in the self and dependence were among the strongest predictors; satisfaction, alternatives and investments were modest; social network support was robust; personality had limited utility — [Le, Dove, Agnew, Korn & Mutso 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x). [Established; effect sizes not extracted]
- **Perceived partner commitment is the top predictor.** Across 43 datasets, perceived-partner commitment was the top relationship-specific predictor of relationship quality; actor perceptions dominated partner reports — [Joel et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117). [Established for prediction]
- **Loss of love, not emergent conflict, distinguishes divorcing couples.** Newlyweds followed 13 years (sample size and country not in abstract): intensity of newlywed romance and expressed negativity predicted later happiness and time to divorce; there was little support for the emergent-distress model (rising negativity); instead disillusionment (abating love, declining overt affection, a weaker conviction that the spouse is responsive, rising ambivalence) distinguished couples headed for divorce — [Huston, Caughlin, Houts, Smith & George 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.2.237). [Moderate; one cohort]
- **Perceiver effects outweigh provider effects.** Family SRM: support perceptions are more determined by the perceiver than by the partner — [Branje et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.16.3.351); PPR is predicted more by one's own responsiveness than by the spouse's reported responsiveness — [Lemay et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.5.834). [Moderate]
- **Reverse causation / concurrence.** Communication→satisfaction paths are few and small, and satisfaction→communication paths also exist — [Lavner et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12301); within couples, negative communication mainly covaries with rather than predicts satisfaction — [Johnson et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672211016920). [Established-to-Moderate]
- **Received support can harm even when perceived.** Recognized emotional support predicted more anger, anxiety and depressed mood — [Shrout et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2006.00108.x); visible support was ineffective or harmful experimentally — [Bolger & Amarel 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.458); support receipt raises negative mood on average — [Gleason et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.94.5.824). [Moderate]
- **More care can be worse (over-provision).** — [Brock & Lawrence 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015402). [Moderate/Preliminary] (This supports "fit", i.e., the hypothesis, rather than "amount".)
- **Fixing communication does not reliably fix relationships.** — [Karney & Bradbury 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12635); [Rogge et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034209); [Wood et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12094); [Fawcett et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2010.00598.x). [Moderate-to-Established]
- **Accurate reception is not required for benefit.** False-alarm sacrifices boost gratitude as much as detected ones — [Visserman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599); invisible support benefits without perception — [Bolger et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953). [Moderate]
- **Evidence that does favour "translation/fit" over "amount".** Matched vs mismatched emotional support — [Cutrona et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.21.4.754); responsiveness determines whether received support helps — [Maisel & Gable 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02388.x); the motive perceived behind a sacrifice determines gratitude — [Visserman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000344); other-praising vs self-focused expressions of gratitude — [Algoe et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616651681); unique perceiver × provider fit dominates supportiveness judgments — [Lakey et al. 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.70.6.1270); the intent/impact gap in distressed couples — [Gottman Institute summary of Gottman et al. 1976](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-behavior-exchange-theory-and-marital-decision-making/). [Moderate/mixed; Gottman 1976 Preliminary]

### Inferences
- Best-supported reformulation: the stages (intended → expressed → perceived → received) are empirically separable, and transmission between adjacent stages is partial (e.g., r ≈ .35 received vs perceived support; ~50% of sacrifices missed). But variance in perceptions is driven heavily by the perceiver (projection, perceiver effects, sentiment), and global love/commitment predicts outcomes at least as strongly as any translation variable. A defensible thesis would be "translation failures are common and consequential in specific domains (support fit, recognition of sacrifice, responses to good news), but they are not demonstrably the main cause of relationship problems."
- Many "translation" variables are themselves partly downstream of love: perceived responsiveness, gratitude, perceived sacrifice and charitable reception all rise with the perceiver's own commitment and responsiveness (projection; sentiment override). This makes "translation vs amount" a false dichotomy unless studies separate them with dyadic designs (partner-reported enacted behaviour plus perceiver's own love/commitment as covariates).
- No study located directly tests the comparative claim (translation failures explain more variance in relationship problems than the amount of love); such a test would need both constructs measured dyadically in the same longitudinal sample.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head study comparing variance explained by "translation" indices (e.g., discrepancy between partner-reported enacted care and perceived care) versus "amount of love" indices (e.g., love/commitment scales) in predicting dissolution or satisfaction change was found.
- Effect sizes for Le et al. 2010 and N for Huston et al. 2001 were not extracted.

## Q11. Validated measurement instruments by construct (citation + DOI, psychometric notes, cross-cultural/Korean validations)

### Takeaway
Self-report PPR has two modern instruments (PPRS; the IRT-optimized 16-item PRI) plus the 3-item MIDUS index used in most health studies. Support research distinguishes perceived-availability measures (ISEL, MSPSS, Social Provisions Scale) from received-support inventories (ISSB, SIRRS) and observational codes (SSBC). Sacrifice, appreciation, capitalization, demand/withdraw, forgiveness and satisfaction all have established scales. Korean validations of the relationship-specific instruments were not located here (defer to the cultural researcher).

### Cited Findings
- **PPR (self-report):** PPRS, defined via understanding and validation — [Reis et al. 2017](https://doi.org/10.1002/9781119102991.ch57); PsycTESTS also holds instruments titled "Perceived Partner Responsiveness Scale" deposited by other authors (Pansera & La Guardia 2012, [10.1037/t55599-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t55599-000); Segal & Fraley 2016, [10.1037/t58788-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t58788-000)), so several variant PPR scales circulate under the same name; check which version a study used. PRI (Perceived Responsiveness and Insensitivity scale): 2 × 8-item subscales via EFA/IRT (n = 2,334), more sensitive to change than global evaluations (n = 173, biweekly × 8 weeks), associated with partners' self-reported behaviours (161 couples) — [Crasta et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000986). MIDUS 3-item PPR (care, understand, appreciate) — [Selcuk et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12272). Chinese adaptation C-PPRS (romantic partners only; n = 441 EFA/CFA) — [Zhou et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-022-00865-x).
- **PPR (observational):** two coding systems for responsive behaviours during disclosure interactions — [Maisel et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2008.00201.x).
- **Received/enacted support:** ISSB (Barrera, Sandler & Ramsay 1981, AJCP 9:435–447, as cited in [Haber et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10464-007-9100-9); DOI not verified). SIRRS (daily support adequacy) — [Dehle et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1080/01926180152588725); SIRRS 4-factor structure (esteem/emotional, physical comfort, informational, tangible), invariant across dating/married, sexes and time — [Barry et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014174). Over/under-provision ratings by support type — [Brock & Lawrence 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015402).
- **Observational support coding:** Social Support Behavior Code (five support types) — [Cutrona & Suhr 1992](https://doi.org/10.1177/009365092019002002); coded visible vs invisible support — [Girme et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167213497802).
- **Perceived support availability:** Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support — [Zimet, Dahlem, Zimet & Farley 1988](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327752jpa5201_2); Social Provisions Scale used in Lakey's SRM work — [Lakey 2015 abstract](https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/fsdg/542) (original Cutrona & Russell 1987 citation/DOI not verified).
- **Support quality constructs:** regulatory effectiveness of support (RES) scale with construct validation across 8 studies — [Zee et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000235).
- **Dyadic coping:** meta-analysed with r = .45 to satisfaction — [Falconier et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2015.07.002) (the Dyadic Coping Inventory source citation/DOI not verified here).
- **Sacrifice:** willingness to sacrifice — [Van Lange et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.72.6.1373); approach/avoidance sacrifice motives — [Impett et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.89.3.327); perceived partner-, relationship- and self-focused sacrifice motives — [Visserman et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000344); quasi-signal-detection dyadic diary paradigm — [Visserman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599).
- **Gratitude/appreciation:** Appreciation in Relationships (AIR) scale — [Gordon et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028723) (PsycTESTS [10.1037/t14379-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t14379-000)); dispositional GQ-6 — [McCullough, Emmons & Tsang 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.1.112). A Korean "Appreciation Scale" validation exists — [Korean Journal of Clinical Psychology 2012, 31(1):329–354](https://doi.org/10.15842/kjcp.2012.31.1.017) — but which source scale it adapts (likely a dispositional measure, not the relationship-specific AIR) was not verified.
- **Capitalization:** perceived responses to capitalization attempts (active-constructive, passive-constructive, active-destructive, passive-destructive) — [Gable et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.2.228); observer coding of responses to positive vs negative disclosures — [Gable et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.904).
- **Demand/withdraw:** Communication Patterns Questionnaire (CPQ): original subscales often had low reliability; EFA/CFA across 4 samples supported 3 factors (constructive communication plus two demand/withdraw scales) with better reliability, stronger associations with satisfaction and sensitivity to change in therapy — [Crenshaw, Christensen, Baucom, Epstein & Baucom 2017](https://doi.org/10.1037/pas0000385).
- **Forgiveness:** TRIM (Transgression-Related Interpersonal Motivations) — [McCullough et al. 1998](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.6.1586).
- **Relationship satisfaction outcome:** Couples Satisfaction Index (IRT-based precision) — [Funk & Rogge 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.21.4.572); PsycTESTS lists Russian, Arabic and Romanian versions (e.g., Romanian: [10.1037/t60781-000](https://doi.org/10.1037/t60781-000)). The Dyadic Adjustment Scale was the outcome in the IBCT trial — [Christensen et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.72.2.176).

### Inferences
- The two PPR scales measure the perception end only. Separating "love expressed" from "love perceived" requires pairing them with partner-reported enacted behaviour (as in Debrot 2012 and Crasta 2021 Study 3), observer coding (Maisel 2008), or signal-detection designs (Visserman 2019).
- The PRI's two-factor, valence-based structure suggests that perception of responsiveness vs insensitivity, rather than the three theoretical components, is what respondents distinguish, which bears on how finely "translation" failures can be measured.

### Gaps
- No Korean validations of PPRS, PRI, AIR, SIRRS, CPQ or CSI were located through Crossref searches in this session (Korean-language databases such as KCI/RISS were not searched); Korean validations of MSPSS, GQ-6 and TRIM likely exist but were not verified. Defer to the cultural researcher.
- Reliability and validity coefficients for most scales were not extracted.
