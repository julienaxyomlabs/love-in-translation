# Primary-source audit: culture, financial provision, perceived acceptance, safety and giver error (C16–C20) and four secondary-only claims (S1–S4)

Audit run 5 November 2026 against this repository (paper chapters, README.md, CORRECTIONS.md; nothing was modified). Verification depth uses four levels: **full text** (the article body was read), **abstract** (publisher, PubMed or Crossref abstract only), **secondary** (press or practitioner summaries only), **inaccessible**. Coverage is not exhaustive. Supplements, errata, editorials and later replications were mostly not read. Working files (extracted full texts, API responses) are in `working-files/audit_R4/`.

## 1. Where and how does the paper state or use each claim?

### Takeaway
C16–C20 and S1–S3 are all used in the paper's chapters. S4 (Levine et al. 1995) is not cited anywhere in the paper, README, corrections log or bibliography; it appears only in a research-notes file. The highest-stakes uses are C19, which carries the empirical case for the safety admissibility constraint (README row 10, §8.10), and C16, which carries the cultural argument and part of the father–daughter example.

## 3. What was measured and found?

### Takeaway
- **C16:** the coefficients the paper quotes are exact. The "unrelated" financial-support result is a *unique* (partial) effect; zero-order correlations with distress were negative and significant in all four countries. The Korea–US financial-support difference is tiny, and the source is internally inconsistent about whether it is significant.
- **C17:** Costello 2003 is accurately summarized at abstract level. Akee 2010 shows income effects concentrated in previously poor households, with parenting quality as the authors' proposed mechanism.
- **C18:** the abstracts confirm r = .51 and .46 for associations between two self-reports.
- **C19:** what shifts in the source is tolerance standards, moderated by measured commitment. Love was not measured.
- **C20:** only press accounts of three small US gift studies were available.

### Cited Findings
#### C16 — Chentsova Dutton, Choi & Choi (2020), *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:353
- **Design:** cross-sectional school survey.
  - Items were developed by Korean adolescence experts for the Korean National Youth Policy Institute (Choi et al., 2010), then translated and back-translated.
  - Multigroup CFA tested configural and metric invariance only.
  - Analysis used hierarchical regressions with contrast-coded culture (US vs East Asia; China vs Korea/Japan; Korea vs Japan), then support types, then culture × support interactions.
  - Source: [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
- **Population:** 7,279 students in grades 10–12, surveyed in 2010 — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
  - South Korea: 3,993 students from 24 schools (June–July). Korea is 55% of the pooled sample.
  - US: 1,002 from 13 schools in 11 states. Japan: 1,112 from 10 schools. China: 1,172 from 10 schools. These were surveyed in September–October.
  - Schools were randomly drawn within sampling units chosen by city size.
  - Regression degrees of freedom imply about 7,044 cases with complete data for the distress model (my calculation from F(3,7040)).
- **Measures:**
  - Three 3-item perceived-support scales on a 1–4 *agreement* scale:
    - Financial: allowance and buying necessary items.
    - Emotional: felt understood, listened to, helped in difficult times.
    - Informational: advice on careers, studies and attitudes.
  - Items refer to "parents" generically, not mothers versus fathers.
  - Financial-support α was .64 (US), .69 (Korea), .72 (Japan) and .52 (China). The authors say financial results need more caution.
  - Distress: 11 items (gloomy, worried, anger; past week), α = .88–.90.
  - Source: [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
- **Invariance:** metric invariance was good for the support scales (CFI ≥ .998, RMSEA ≤ .037). For distress it was RMSEA = .102 and SRMR = .078, beyond the authors' own criteria (RMSEA < .08, SRMR < .06). Scalar invariance was not tested — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
- **Levels (Table 2, 1–4 scale)** — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/):

  | Support type | US | China | Korea | Japan |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Emotional | 3.02 | 2.99 | 2.78 | 2.78 |
  | Financial | 3.06 | 3.38 | 3.13 | 3.15 |
  | Informational | 3.24 | 2.96 | 2.81 | 2.57 |

  - Table 2's Scheffé subscripts mark the US (a) and Korea (ab) financial means as **not** significantly different.
  - The results text says Americans perceived the least financial support, with Koreans and Japanese in the middle (ps < .001). The source contradicts itself.
- **Effect sizes (my calculation, pooled SDs from Table 2):** Korea − US d ≈ +0.11 for financial support, −0.32 for emotional and −0.58 for informational. These are computed, not reported.
- **Distress (Table 5, step 2)** — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/):
  - Emotional β = −.188 (p < .001); informational β = −.046 (p = .004); financial β = −.011 (B = −.01, SE = .013, t = −0.85, p = .393). Support types together added ΔR² = .05.
  - Step 3: the culture × support interaction block was **significant** (ΔR² = .003, F(9,7028) = 2.34, p = .012), but no individual interaction term was.
- **Positive self-beliefs (Table 4):** emotional β = .18, informational .12, **financial .10** (all p < .001). The interaction block was not significant (ΔR² = .001, p = .750). Financial support therefore had a unique positive association with self-beliefs in all four countries alike — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
- **Zero-order correlations (Table 3):** financial support correlated with distress at r = −.22 (US), −.11 (China), −.11 (Korea) and −.07 (Japan), all significant. It correlated .39–.51 with emotional and .40–.52 with informational support — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
- **Authors' own reading** — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/):
  - The abstract says informational support affected distress more for American than East Asian adolescents, and the conclusion says culture shapes the *impact* of support on some outcomes. Neither statement matches the non-significant individual interactions in Table 5.
  - The discussion calls the financial-support null for distress possibly conservative, because of low reliability.
  - It also speculates, untested, that financial investment may signal to adolescents that they are valued.
- **Authors' limitations:** global self-reports with no parent reports; only three support types (implicit support, such as accompanying a child to exams, was omitted); no direct measure of culture; uncertain generalization — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
- **Causal identification:** none. The study is cross-sectional, with a single informant and shared method.

#### C17 — Costello et al. (2003), *JAMA* 290(15); Akee et al. (2010), *AEJ: Applied* 2(1)
- **Costello 2003 design and population:**
  - Quasi-experimental, longitudinal: a representative sample of 1,420 rural children aged 9–13 at intake, assessed annually 1993–2000. About a quarter were American Indian.
  - A casino opened on the reservation midway through and gave every American Indian an income supplement that rose annually.
  - 14% of families moved out of poverty, 53% stayed poor and 32% were never poor. Non-Indian incomes were unaffected.
  - DSM-IV symptom levels were compared for the four years before and after the opening.
  - Source: [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14559956/).
- **Costello 2003 results:**
  - Before the opening, persistently poor and ex-poor children had more symptoms (4.38 and 4.28) than never-poor children (2.75).
  - Afterwards the ex-poor fell to never-poor levels while the persistently poor stayed high: OR 1.50 (95% CI 1.08–2.09) versus 0.91 (0.77–1.07).
  - The effect was specific to conduct and oppositional defiant symptoms. Anxiety and depression were unaffected.
  - Similar results appeared in non-Indian children whose families left poverty in the same period.
  - The authors read this as social causation for conduct and oppositional disorders but not for anxiety or depression.
  - Source: [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14559956/).
- **Independent description:** D'Onofrio et al. describe the ex-poor children as matching poor children on oppositional and conduct symptoms before the casino and reaching never-poor levels four years after — [D'Onofrio et al. 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933111/).
- **Akee 2010 design:**
  - Same Great Smoky Mountains Study: 1,420 children, 350 American Indian and 1,070 non-Indian, from 11 western North Carolina counties.
  - Difference-in-differences: the cohorts aged 9 and 11 at intake are compared with the cohort aged 13, scaled by the number of American Indian parents, with survey weights.
  - Outcomes were measured at ages 16–21 and include administrative arrest records.
  - Source: [author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2891175/).
- **Akee 2010 results** — [author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2891175/):
  - Full-sample education effects were positive but **not significant** (about four months).
  - In previously poor households, about $4,000 a year added 1.1 years of schooling by age 21 (p < .05).
  - Arrests at 16–17 fell by 22%. There was no effect on arrests at 18–21, and reductions were confined to minor offences.
  - Mothers' receipt of the income related to education outcomes; fathers' receipt related to crime outcomes.
- **Akee 2010 mechanisms** — [author manuscript](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2891175/):
  - Parents did not cut working hours, and fathers' arrests roughly halved.
  - Parental supervision improved.
  - Child-reported positive interactions improved significantly with mothers. With fathers the change had the same sign but was not significant.
  - The authors infer "improved parental quality" as a likely mechanism.
  - Akee 2010 did not analyse anxiety or depression.

#### C18 — Khaleque & Rohner (2002), *JMF* 64(1); (2012), *JCCP* 43(5)
- **2002 meta-analysis:** 43 studies worldwide using child and adult versions of the PARQ and PAQ — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x).
  - The predicted relation appeared in every study, with no exceptions.
  - Mean weighted r = .51 for children and .46 for adults.
  - Fail-safe N was 3,433 for children and 941 for adults.
  - Not in the abstract: total N, number of countries, weighting model, heterogeneity statistics and the range of study-level effects.
- **2012 meta-analysis:** 66 studies, 19,511 respondents, 22 countries — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022022111406120).
  - Both maternal and paternal acceptance correlated significantly with adjustment in all cultures.
  - Paternal acceptance correlated more strongly than maternal for children, but not for adults.
  - The abstract gives no effect sizes.
- **Reliability meta-analysis (51 studies):** the PARQ is reliable in US ethnic groups and internationally. The PAQ is reliable in US ethnic groups, but the evidence is insufficient for widespread cross-cultural use — [Khaleque & Rohner 2002b abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022022102033001006).
- **Design:** correlational synthesis of associations between two self-report questionnaires, usually completed by the same respondent. It has no causal identification.

#### C19 — Arriaga, Capezza & Daly (2016), *JPSP* 110(1)
- **Studies 1a/1b (n = 689):** experiencing aggression in the current relationship, and greater commitment, predicted more tolerant standards for common partner aggression — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26461796/).
- **Study 2 (longitudinal, n = 52, none with prior partner aggression):** once aggression occurred, people adopted more tolerant standards, but only if highly committed — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26461796/).
- **Study 3 (experiment, n = 73 reporting current aggression):** the manipulation was whether imagined acts came from the partner or from a stranger. Participants were more tolerant of the partner's acts, again only if highly committed — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26461796/).
- **Outcome measured:** personal standards for judging aggressive acts. **Moderator:** commitment, which was measured, not manipulated. The abstract mentions no measure of love or perceived love, no change in commitment as an outcome, and no harm outcomes. Sample origins are unstated; the authors were at Purdue and Stonehill — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26461796/).

#### C20 — Flynn & Adams (2009), *JESP* 45(2) (secondary accounts only)
- **Study 1:** 33 recently engaged individuals (15 couples and 3 more men), recruited from a wedding-planning website.
  - Real rings costing several hundred to several thousand dollars.
  - Recipients rated their own appreciation on 7-point scales; givers rated their fiancées' appreciation.
  - Givers expected appreciation to rise with cost. Recipients reported no more appreciation for pricier rings.
  - Source: [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/when-giving-gifts-price-wrong).
- **Study 2:** 232 online adults, randomly assigned to recall a birthday gift they gave or received (gifts up to $1,365). Givers, but not receivers, linked higher price to more appreciation. Social desirability was measured — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/when-giving-gifts-price-wrong).
- **Study 3:** 197 participants in a hypothetical graduation-gift scenario (a CD versus an iPod), randomly assigned to giver or receiver. Givers expected the iPod to be more appreciated; receivers' appreciation was comparable for both — [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/when-giving-gifts-price-wrong); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/research-confirms-its-thought-counts).
- **Commentary in the same news report:**
  - An outside marketing scholar called it a first step and asked for studies in which paired givers and recipients rate the same gift.
  - Flynn predicted different results for children; this was not tested.
  - Source: [Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/when-giving-gifts-price-wrong).

#### S1 — Gottman et al. (1976), *JPSP* 34(1) (secondary only)
- The blog summary describes:
  - couples self-classified as distressed or non-distressed;
  - high- and low-conflict decision tasks;
  - continuous coding of intended impact and of received impact.
- Study 1: no difference in intent, but distressed spouses' behaviour was received more negatively. Study 2: partners coded it as far more negative than intended.
- The reciprocity hypothesis failed, and the authors favoured a "bank account" model.
- The summary gives no sample sizes or statistics — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-behavior-exchange-theory-and-marital-decision-making/).

#### S2 — Lakey, McCabe, Fisicaro & Drew (1996), *JPSP* 70(6)
- Three generalizability-theory studies in which participants rated the same targets' supportiveness.
- Supporters, perceivers and their interaction each contributed significantly. The perceiver × supporter interaction, supporter characteristics and perceiver biases made the largest contributions, in that order.
- An erratum was published in 71(2):210.
- Source: [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8667167/).

#### S3 — Rohner & Pettengill (1985), *Child Development* 56(2)
- Korean youths' perceived parental control correlated positively with perceived warmth and low neglect.
- The authors contrast this sharply with *studies* of North American youth, not with a same-design US sample, and discuss cultural factors.
- The abstract gives no N.
- Source: [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3987420/).

#### S4 — Levine, Sato, Hashimoto & Verma (1995), *JCCP* 26(5)
- College students from secondary population centres in 11 countries rated how important love is for establishing and for maintaining a marriage. The countries were India, Pakistan, Thailand, Mexico, Brazil, Japan, Hong Kong, the Philippines, Australia, England and the US.
- Love was rated most important in Western and Westernized nations and least in nations the 1995 abstract calls "underdeveloped Eastern".
- Differences were larger for establishing a marriage than for maintaining or ending one, and there were few sex differences.
- Individualistic cultures gave love more weight. That weight correlated with higher living standards, higher marriage and divorce rates and lower fertility.
- The abstract reports no "marry without love" percentages and no per-country N.
- Source: [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022022195265007).

### Inferences
- **C16:** the null partial coefficient for financial support is uninterpretable as "no relation", for three reasons:
  - collinearity with the other support types (r = .39–.52);
  - low reliability of the financial scale, which attenuates its own coefficient;
  - the Korean sample making up 55% of the pooled data.
- **C16 levels:** cross-country mean comparisons need scalar invariance, which was not tested. Response-style differences (for example in use of scale extremes) are a known rival explanation. This is a methodological inference, not tested here.
- **C17:** neither study measured how children *perceived* the income. They show that household income matters, not that its benefits bypass perception. Akee's mechanism evidence (better supervision and mother–child interactions) points partly to relational channels.
- **C18:** perceived acceptance and adjustment are both reported by the same person. The association therefore cannot show that acts work "through perceived regard"; adjustment can also colour perceived acceptance.

### Gaps
- **C16:** item wording in Supplementary Appendix A was not read. Per-country regressions were not reported, so the claim of "four countries alike" rests on contrast-coded interaction terms.
- **C17:** the JAMA full text was not read: group sizes, the model, symptom-specific estimates, any tested mediators (for example supervision) and stated limitations remain unverified. Akee et al. (2018), which the paper's notes say found emotional and behavioural benefits in the same cohort (`evidence/notes/05-family-and-culture.md:124-126`), was not checked. If accurate, it would sit in tension with the "not anxiety or depression" contrast.
- **C18:** heterogeneity, moderator analyses and the number of countries in the 2002 meta-analysis are unverified. A secondary figure of 7,563 respondents in 15 countries appeared in search results and is unverified.
- **C19:** sample sources, the aggression and commitment measures, and whether commitment itself changed after aggression are unverified.
- **C20:** statistics, effect sizes and the exact appreciation measures are unverified. The press accounts disagree on recruitment details.
- **S1:** N, test statistics and the paradigm details are unverified.
- **S2:** variance percentages, the targets used (strangers versus real network members) and the erratum's content are unverified.
- **S3:** N and measures are unverified.
- **S4:** item wording and percentages are unverified.

## 4. Does each source support the paper's interpretation? Verdicts and corrected wording

### Takeaway
- **Supported:** none of the five C-claims without qualification.
- **Qualified:** C16, C17, C18 and C20.
- **Overstated:** C19, as used in the README, §8.10 and ch. 19b. The source shows tolerance standards shifting among highly committed people, not love or commitment rising as safety falls.

### Cited Findings
**C16 — qualified**
- **Accurate:**
  - β = −.01 (p = .39) and β = −.19 — [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7079630/).
  - "No *individual* culture × support interaction" (`paper/05-evidence-against-it.md:61`).
  - [Preliminary] and "cross-sectional, weak financial scale".
- **Overstated or omitted:**
  1. "Unrelated to distress" (`05:61`, `07:52`). The null is a partial effect net of emotional and informational support. Zero-order correlations were negative and significant in every country (r = −.07 to −.22).
  2. The significant interaction block for distress (p = .012) is omitted, as are the authors' own abstract claim of a stronger US effect for informational support and their conclusion that culture shapes impact.
  3. The unique positive link between financial support and positive self-beliefs (β = .10, similar across countries) is omitted.
  4. "Korean adolescents report *more* financial support" (`10:28`): d ≈ 0.11, marked non-significant by Table 2's Scheffé subscripts but called significant in the text, with no scalar invariance.
  5. "Alike" and "predicts" (`13:5`) overstate a cross-sectional association whose omnibus moderation test was significant.
  6. The measure covers "parents" (not fathers) and allowance or necessities (not breadwinning or educational investment). That limits its use in the father–daughter example.
  7. "Cuts against" cultural relativity: the study never measured whether provision is read as care.
- **Proposed wording for `05:61`:**
  > Among 7,279 high-school students surveyed in 2010 in the US, China, South Korea and Japan, perceived financial support (allowance and necessities; α = .52–.72) was not *uniquely* associated with distress once emotional and informational support were controlled (β = −.01, p = .39). Its zero-order correlations with distress were small and negative in every sample (r = −.07 to −.22). Perceived emotional support was uniquely associated with lower distress (β = −.19), and financial support with higher positive self-beliefs (β = .10). No single culture × support interaction was significant, though the interaction block for distress was (ΔR² = .003, p = .012) [Preliminary; cross-sectional, single informant]. The study did not measure whether adolescents read provision as care, so it gives no support to, rather than refuting, a strong cultural-relativity version of translation.
- **Proposed wording for `10:28`:**
  > Korean adolescents reported less emotional support than US adolescents (2.78 vs 3.02 on a 1–4 agreement scale, d ≈ 0.3) and about the same financial support (3.13 vs 3.06, d ≈ 0.1; the source's table and text disagree on significance; cross-country mean comparisons assume untested scalar invariance).

**C17 — qualified**

  - Verification is at abstract level only.

**C18 — qualified**
- **Supported:** the association and the figures r = .51 and .46 — [Crossref abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x). The caveat at `03:105` is good.

  - The adjustment measure (PAQ) lacks established cross-cultural reliability — [2002b abstract](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1177/0022022102033001006).

**S1 — cannot determine.** The paper's sentence matches the only accessible summary, and its hedges ("reportedly", "secondary summary", [Preliminary]) are appropriate — [Gottman Institute](https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-research-behavior-exchange-theory-and-marital-decision-making/).
