# Korea as an Empirical Case Study: Provision, Fathering, Filial Obligation and Parent–Child Communication (dossier compiled 4 Nov 2026)

*Grading legend used throughout: **[Established]** = official statistics or replicated, well-identified findings; **[Moderate/mixed]** = credible but single-study, cross-sectional, or with conflicting evidence; **[Preliminary]** = small/convenience samples, conference papers, preprints, single press reports; **[Theoretical]** = interpretive framework, not a tested claim. **[OLD]** = data more than 10 years old (collected before 2016) and possibly outdated given rapid change. "Measured" vs "inferred" is flagged where the distinction matters. National averages describe populations, never individual families; nothing here should be read as describing the author's observed father–daughter case. Agency note: Statistics Korea (KOSTAT, 통계청) was reorganised in 2025 as 국가데이터처; English press renderings vary ("Ministry of Data and Statistics", "National Data Office"), so releases from 2025-26 appear under that name.*

## 1. Social change: "compressed modernity" and the changing value of children

### Takeaway
"Compressed modernity" is an influential interpretive framework (Theoretical), not a tested hypothesis; the empirical record on value-of-children change in Korea is thin (one 2005 cross-sectional mother sample compared against a 1972 study) and old, so a "shift from economic to psychological value of children" should be presented as plausible but only preliminarily evidenced.

### Cited Findings
- **Chang Kyung-Sup (1999), "Compressed modernity and its discontents: South Korean society in transition," *Economy and Society* 28(1): 30–55.** DOI verified (resolves to this article). Design: historical-theoretical essay written during the 1997–98 financial crisis. Argument (from abstract): the "very mechanisms which made [Korea's] explosive economic growth possible tend to create various hazardous consequences," naming patriarchal political authoritarianism, chaebol practices, "abuse and exclusion of labour," "neglect of basic welfare rights," and "ideological self-negation." No original quantitative data. **[Theoretical] [OLD]** — [doi:10.1080/03085149900000023](https://doi.org/10.1080/03085149900000023)
- **Chang Kyung-Sup (2010), *South Korea under Compressed Modernity: Familial Political Economy in Transition*, Routledge (Abingdon/New York), 180 pp., ISBN 9780415575874.** The candidate DOI 10.4324/9780203854150 is **WRONG**: it resolves to *Social Judgment and Decision Making* (Psychology Press, 2012). I could not find the book's correct DOI (Crossref rate-limited); cite by ISBN. Bibliographic details confirmed via a review in *International Sociology* — [Holzer 2012 review, doi:10.1177/0268580912452369](https://doi.org/10.1177/0268580912452369); [OpenAlex check of the wrong DOI](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.4324/9780203854150)
- **Kim, Park, Kwon & Koo (2005), "Values of children, parent–child relationship, and social change in Korea: Indigenous, cultural, and psychological analysis," *Applied Psychology* 54(3): 338–354.** DOI verified. Design: review of traditional family structure and of the 1972 Korean Value of Children (VOC) study (Lee & Kim 1975), plus a new cross-sectional survey of **314 young mothers and 395 older mothers**. Measured: mothers' stated reasons for having/not having children. Finding: "psychological benefits are the most important reasons for having a child and personal and financial constraints are the most important reasons for not having a child." Authors' inference: results "challenge the economic and utilitarian models." The "shift" claim rests on comparing different samples/instruments across 1972 and the 2000s, not on panel data. **[Moderate/mixed for current reasons; Preliminary for "shift"] [OLD]** — [doi:10.1111/j.1464-0597.2005.00214.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1464-0597.2005.00214.x)
- **Census microdata evidence of family change (preprint):** Dai & Esteve (2025), "The Shifting Nature of Intergenerational Coresidence in South Korea: 1975–2020," Research Square preprint, using 1% Korean census microdata 1975–2020 with decomposition. Finding: "the traditional pattern of married children living with parents has nearly disappeared," while the growing share of unmarried adults "has given rise to 'two-adult-generation' households." Not peer-reviewed. **[Preliminary]** — [doi:10.21203/rs.3.rs-8008001/v1](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-8008001/v1)
- The speed of attitudinal change documented in Section 2 (e.g., the share saying "family" should support elderly parents fell from 70.7% in 2002 to 18.2% in 2024) is the kind of compressed change Chang's framework describes — [Hankyung 2008 report of the 2008 Social Survey](https://sgsg.hankyung.com/article/2008112745451); [SBS Biz on the 2024 Social Survey](https://biz.sbs.co.kr/amp/article/20000201265)

### Inferences
- The framework predicts *coexisting* expectations within one family (e.g., a father socialised into provider-as-love norms and a daughter socialised into emotional-intimacy norms) — this is an inference from the framework, consistent with Kwon & Roy's (2007) finding of fathers negotiating "contradictory and often ambiguous" expectations (Section 3), but not itself a tested claim.
- Because the VOC "shift" is documented only among mothers and only up to the early 2000s, it says nothing direct about fathers' or children's conceptions of love.

### Gaps
- No verified, recent (post-2015) Korean value-of-children replication found; the cross-national VOC project (Trommsdorff & Nauck) includes Korea but was not verified here.
- Chang's often-cited typology of coexisting family ideologies (Confucian, instrumental, affectional, individualistic familism) is recalled from memory and was NOT verified against the text — do not cite without page numbers.
- Park & Kim (2006) indigenous-psychology chapter ("Family, parent–child relationship, and academic achievement in Korea," in Kim, Yang & Hwang, eds., *Indigenous and Cultural Psychology*, Springer) could not be located in OpenAlex: **UNVERIFIED**. A related verified item: Park & Kim (2003), *Annual Report of Educational Psychology in Japan* 42: 24–27, [doi:10.5926/arepj1962.42.0_24](https://doi.org/10.5926/arepj1962.42.0_24).

Official Social Survey data show one of the fastest documented collapses of exclusive family responsibility for elderly parents anywhere: "family" fell from 70.7% (2002) to 18.2% (2024), with "family, government and society together" now the majority (60.3% in 2024) — while Korea's old-age income poverty remains the OECD's highest (about 40% of people 66+ vs 14.8% OECD average). **[Established]** for the trend; exact values for several years (1998, 2010–2020) could not be verified.

### Inferences
- The combination "norm of filial support collapsing" + "very high old-age poverty" + "very high private-education spending on children" (Section 4) implies that many Korean parents' financial sacrifice for children is no longer reliably reciprocated by old-age support. This is consistent with — but does not prove — the idea that parental provision carries an unspoken expectation (e.g., gratitude/recognition) that children may not perceive as part of the exchange.
- The midlife gap between endorsing equal housework (64.4%) and practising it (22.1%) is a population-level instance of "attitude ≠ behaviour" relevant to the paper's intended ≠ expressed distinction (inference).

### Gaps
- Exact whole-population figures for 1998, 2010, 2012, 2016, 2018, 2020 and the "parents themselves" and "government/society" shares for 2002–2022 were not retrievable (KOSIS tables are JavaScript-rendered; press reports partial). Retrieve from KOSIS table "부모 부양에 대한 견해" before publication.
- Respondent age base of the 53.4% figure in the 2026 Statistics on the Elderly is unconfirmed.

## 3. Fathers: work hours, time use, parental leave, "friendly dads", kirogi families, and the "6 minutes" claim

### Takeaway
Structural constraints on Korean fathers' presence are real and measurable (1,833 annual hours in 2025 vs OECD 1,736), but the "emotionally absent provider father" is a moving target: official time-use data show young dual-earner fathers' daily childcare nearly doubled between 2014 and 2024, and fathers' share of parental-leave users rose to 29–39% depending on the measure. The viral "6 minutes a day" figure traces to OECD *How's Life? 2015* (per Korean wire coverage), measured narrowly defined childcare activities, rests on data probably from around 2009, and is obsolete.

### Cited Findings
**Working hours**
- Korea: **1,833 hours per worker in 2025**, down 32 from **1,865 in 2024**; **OECD average 1,736 (2025, 36 members with data)**; Korea 6th highest; Mexico highest (2,205); Germany lowest (1,332); Korea worked 235 hours more than Japan and 33 more than the US (implying ≈1,598 and ≈1,800). **[Established]** — [Korea JoongAng Daily (Yonhap, OECD data), 5 Jul 2026](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/business/koreas-yearly-working-hours-fell-by-32-hours-last-year-but-remain-higher-than-oecd-average/12755382)

**Time use (Korean Time Use Survey, 생활시간조사; diary-based)**
- **2024 TUS, married parents aged 25–39 (release 19 Oct 2026):** all married-with-children men 2h14m/day of housework + care (+58 min vs 2014); women 5h45m (−34 min); gap 3h31m (was 5h03m in 2014). **Dual-earner:** men 1h14m → 2h17m; women 4h30m → 4h00m; gap 3h16m → 1h43m. **Care only, dual-earner:** fathers 47 min → 1h22m; mothers 2h00m in both years; care gap 1h13m → 38 min. Single-earner men 1h17m → 2h11m; single-earner women 7h42m → 7h28m. Measured: diary minutes of activities, not emotional quality. **[Established]** — [inews24, 19 Oct 2026](https://www.inews24.com/view/2016351)
- **Lee & Seo (2021)**, "Trend in paternal childcare time for preschool children in Korea from 2004 to 2019," *Journal of Korean Family Resource Management Association* 25(3): 103–120 (DOI verified via OpenAlex). Design: repeated cross-sections of KTUS 2004/2009/2014/2019, workday diaries only; fathers of preschoolers n = 2,264 / 1,242 / 959 / 952. Finding: dual-earner fathers' childcare time rose by 24 min/day 2004→2019 ("roughly doubled"); essential (physical) care grew more than developmental care; market work hours significantly predicted less father care in all four waves. **[Moderate/mixed]** (workday-only; no weekend) — [doi:10.22626/jkfrma.2021.25.3.007](https://doi.org/10.22626/jkfrma.2021.25.3.007)

**Changing expectations of fathers**
- **Kwon & Roy (2007)**, "Changing social expectations for work and family involvement among Korean fathers," *Journal of Comparative Family Studies* 38(2): 285–305 (DOI verified). Qualitative interviews, **32 middle- and working-class fathers** after the 1997–98 crisis. Fathers negotiated three "contradictory and often ambiguous" expectation sets: Confucian fatherhood; work success as the measure of good fatherhood; and "new" fatherhood combining providing and caregiving. - **"Friendly dad" (프렌디):** I found no nationally representative measurement of "friendly dad" identity or behaviour; the term circulates mainly in media and marketing. Quantitative proxies (time use and parental leave above) point in the same direction as the narrative. **[Preliminary / media-driven]** (see Gaps)

**Kirogi ("wild geese") families**
- **Lee & Koo (2006)**, "'Wild geese fathers' and a globalised family strategy for education in Korea," *International Development Planning Review* 28(4): 533–553 (DOI verified). Statistics plus interviews with "a small sample of kirogi fathers." **Verification of the "Korean fathers spend only 6 minutes a day with their children" claim**
- Contemporary Yonhap wire coverage (via Seoul Shinmun, 19 Oct 2015) attributes it to the OECD's *How's Life? 2015*: Korean fathers spend **3 min/day playing with, teaching or reading to children + 3 min/day on physical care = 6 min**, the shortest in the OECD, vs an **OECD average of 47 min**; Japanese fathers 12 + 7 min; Korean children's total time with parents 48 min/day vs OECD average 151 min. The article gives **no data year**. — [Seoul Shinmun / Yonhap, 19 Oct 2015](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/2015/10/19/20151019800067); same figures in an op-ed — [Asia Economy, 20 Oct 2015](https://view.asiae.co.kr/article/2015102010554279665)
- Distortion in later circulation: a Herald piece repeats "six minutes in South Korea" vs "47 minutes in OECD member countries" and "five hours a day in Sweden," attributing them only to "a recent survey." — [Herald](https://heraldk.com/en/?p=71411)
- A different OECD-derived figure circulated in 2014: Korean husbands' childcare at 10 min/day (Yonhap via a translation site). — [Koreabang (Yonhap translation), 2014](https://www.koreabang.com/2014/stories/study-finds-korean-husbands-do-least-housework-in-oecd.html)
- **Verdict:** the number is **traceable to a real OECD publication** (per wire coverage; I could not open the OECD original, as oecd.org returned 403 errors). It **measured time-use-diary minutes of specific childcare activities** (care; play/teaching/reading), not total time fathers spend with or near their children. It is **at least 10–15 years old** (the release date suggests late-2000s diaries, probably KTUS 2009 — an inference, not verified). Present-day official data (dual-earner fathers aged 25–39: 1h22m/day of care in 2024) make it obsolete. Calling it "time spent with children" is a mislabel. **[Moderate/mixed for provenance; the claim as popularly stated is misleading]**

### Inferences
- Evidence *against* a static "emotionally absent provider father" stereotype: rapid increases in fathers' care time (2014→2024), rising leave take-up, and qualitative evidence that even kirogi fathers construct their role as supportive (Lee & Koo). Evidence *consistent with* it: long hours (1,833 in 2025), the still-large gender gap in care (38 min/day among dual-earner parents aged 25–39 in 2024), and fathers' leave concentrated in large firms.
- Lee (2023) cautions that more-involved fathers self-select into leave, so policy-driven increases may overstate attitudinal change among fathers who do not take leave.
- Time-use data measure *presence/activities* (a proxy for "love expressed"); none of these sources measure what fathers intend or what children perceive.

### Gaps
- No verified 2024 TUS figures for **all** fathers, or for fathers of adolescents (the age group most relevant to a father–adult-daughter case); the published breakdowns found cover parents aged 25–39.
- OECD *How's Life? 2015* original chart, definitions (age of children, parent population, primary vs secondary activity) and data year not directly inspected.
- No representative data on kirogi father–child relationship quality or recent kirogi prevalence (post-2014). Studies of kirogi fathers' health and loneliness were not retrieved (OpenAlex rate-limited).
- No quantitative study of "friendly dad" (프렌디) identity, prevalence or effects was found.

## 4. Provision through education: private education spending, "education fever", sacrifice and indebtedness

### Takeaway
Parental financial investment in children's education is large, measured annually, and strongly class-graded: 27.5 trillion won in 2025, with participating students' families spending a record 604,000 won/month. The indigenous-psychology claim that Korean children read parental sacrifice as love and repay it with indebtedness and effort is plausible but rests mainly on reviews by one research group; it coexists with evidence that parental pressure predicts depression (Section 6).

### Cited Findings
- **2025 Private Education Expenditures Survey (released 12 Mar 2026; National Data Office + Ministry of Education; about 3,000 schools/classes — press reports differ on the unit — and ~74,000 students):** total **27.5 trillion won** (−5.7% from 29.2 trillion in 2024; first fall in 5 years); participation **75.7%** (−4.3 points from 80.0%); monthly spending per participating student **604,000 won** (+2.0%, record high); per student including non-participants **458,000 won** (−3.5%); 7.1 hours/week. High-school participants 793,000 won/month. By household income: ≥8 million won/month households 662,000 won with 84.9% participation vs <3 million won households 192,000 won with 52.8% participation (per-student basis as reported; check whether per-participant). Only-child households 518,000 won; three or more children 358,000 won. **[Established]** — [Newsis, 12 Mar 2026](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260312_0003545284); [Seoul Shinmun, 12 Mar 2026](https://m.seoul.co.kr/news/2026/03/12/20260312500156); [Herald Economy](https://www.heraldk.com/article/2026031119000031975)
- Perceived burden: among 40–64-year-olds, 62.6% (2024) see children's education costs as a burden; 70.8% name private education as the biggest component (86.9% of those in their 40s). **[Established]** — [Korea JoongAng Daily, 21 Sep 2026](https://www.koreajoongangdaily.com/korea/koreans-increasingly-favor-shared-responsibility-for-older-adult-care-survey-finds/12885382)
- **Park, Byun & Kim (2011; online 2010)**, "Parental involvement and students' cognitive outcomes in Korea: Focusing on private tutoring," *Sociology of Education* 84(1): 3–22 (DOI verified). Longitudinal survey following 7th graders for two years; multivariate models. "Parents' efforts in selecting and monitoring private tutoring are significantly associated with increased math and English test scores." Observational (no experimental identification); measured parental *managerial* involvement, not affection. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD data]** — [doi:10.1177/0038040710392719](https://doi.org/10.1177/0038040710392719)
- **Seth (2002)**, *Education Fever: Society, Politics, and the Pursuit of Schooling in South Korea*, University of Hawai'i Press (Hawai'i Studies on Korea), vii + 305 pp. Historical account of how the drive for maximal formal education "appeared immediately before 1945 and quickly embraced every sector of society." No book DOI found; bibliographic details via reviews. **[Theoretical/historical] [OLD]** — [Choice review, doi:10.5860/choice.40-5356](https://doi.org/10.5860/choice.40-5356); [AHR review, doi:10.1086/ahr/110.1.116](https://doi.org/10.1086/ahr/110.1.116)
- **Abelmann, Choi & Park, eds. (2012)**, *No Alternative? Experiments in South Korean Education*, University of California Press. Ethnographies of private after-schooling, alternative and home schooling and adult distance education; producers and consumers "often reject mainstream education while simultaneously seeking or embracing its symbolic value." **[Preliminary — ethnographic] [OLD]** — [eScholarship record via OpenAlex search; JAS review, doi:10.1017/s0021911813002155](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0021911813002155)
- **Kim & Park (2006)**, "Indigenous psychological analysis of academic achievement in Korea: The influence of self-efficacy, parents, and culture," *International Journal of Psychology* 41(4): 287–292 (DOI verified). Review of the authors' and others' studies. Claims (from abstract): "The sacrifice and support provided by parents are essential ingredients for their children's success"; "**emotional support, rather than informational support, is reported to be the most important**"; "children feel a sense of indebtedness toward their parents for all their devotion, sacrifice, and support. This promotes filial piety and academic achievement"; "although Western theories assume guilt and external pressure to have negative consequences, the reverse is the case" in Korea. Measured in underlying studies: student self-reports (mostly cross-sectional). The causal language is the authors' inference. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD]** — [doi:10.1080/00207590544000068](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207590544000068)

### Inferences
- Even within the indigenous-psychology literature that emphasises sacrifice, students report *emotional* support as the most important parental support (Kim & Park 2006) — evidence against a reading in which Korean children simply value provision as love.
- The indebtedness pathway is a candidate mechanism by which provision *can* be perceived as love; whether a given child perceives it that way is an empirical, individual-level question (for example, Section 6 shows pressure perceived as pressure predicts depressive symptoms).
- The strong income gradient (662,000 vs 192,000 won) means "provision via education" is a markedly class-specific expression of parental investment.

### Gaps
- No verified study directly measures children's *perception* of parental financial investment as love (vs burden/pressure) in a representative Korean sample.
- Park & Kim (2006) chapter: **UNVERIFIED** (see Section 1).
- 2025 survey breakdowns by region and the share spending ≥1 million won/month were not captured from a primary source.

## 5. Parent–child relationships and child/adolescent well-being

### Takeaway
Korean children and adolescents score below most comparison countries on subjective well-being (Children's Worlds, PISA), and adolescents consistently report far less conversation with fathers than with mothers (e.g., 40.6% vs 76.2% talking for ≥30 min daily, 2020). Korean studies link father communication to life satisfaction, but they are mostly cross-sectional; I found no verified KCYPS longitudinal estimate of separate father and mother effects.

### Cited Findings
- **Children's Worlds, Wave 3 (2017–19; >128,000 children aged ~8, 10, 12 in 35 countries/territories):** 10-year-olds' mean overall well-being (0–10 composite of life satisfaction, happiness and sadness): **South Korea 7.9**; Albania 9.4 (top); France 8.3; Germany 8.3; Taiwan 7.8; Hong Kong 7.7; Vietnam 7.6; "the bottom eight countries were all in Asia." Korea's most positive relative aspect: classmates; least positive: appearance. **[Established (descriptive); comparability caveat]** — [Children's Worlds Wave 3 summary (Jacobs Foundation)](https://jacobsfoundation.org/app/uploads/2020/08/Summary-Report_ChildrensWorlds_3rdwave.pdf)
- **Children's Worlds, Wave 2 (press release 2016; ~20,000 eight-year-olds in 16 countries; >56,000 children overall):** the share of 8-year-olds with low well-being ranged from below 3% to "over 9 per cent in Ethiopia, South Korea and England"; Korean children among the least likely to like school; being hit at school was "lowest in South Korea"; being left out by classmates "lowest in South Korea and Ethiopia." **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD]** — [University of York, 16 Feb 2016](https://www.york.ac.uk/news-and-events/news/2016/research/childrens-worlds-study/)
- **PISA 2022 (15-year-olds):** 22% of Korean students in the "dissatisfied" range (0–4 on 0–10) vs OECD average 18% (23% in PISA 2018); school belonging 79% vs 75% OECD; 9% felt like an outsider vs 17%. **[Established]** — [Asia Economy, 17 Dec 2023](https://www.asiae.co.kr/en/article/2023121715533967713)
- **Youth Comprehensive Survey 2020 (Ministry of Gender Equality and Family; 7,170 youths):** talking with father ≥30 min every day **40.6%**, lowest since the survey began in 2011 (45.6%); with mother **76.2%** (rising). **[Established]** — [Kukinews, 21 Apr 2021](https://www.kukinews.com/article/view/kuk202104210278)
- **Youth Statistics 2016 (KOSTAT):** 95.0% of adolescents talk with their mother on weekdays vs 88.3% with their father; 56.5% talk with their father for less than one hour on weekdays (주중). **[Established] [OLD]** — [Maeil Shinmun, 2 May 2016](https://www.imaeil.com/page/view/2016050214433054029)
- **Chentsova-Dutton, Choi & Choi (2020)**, "Perceived parental support and adolescents' positive self-beliefs and levels of distress across four countries," *Frontiers in Psychology* 11: 353 (DOI verified). Data: NYPI International Study on Well-being of Adolescents, **2010** (Korea n = 3,993 high-school students; US 1,002; Japan 1,112; China 1,172). Korea vs US means: emotional support 2.78 vs 3.02; informational 2.81 vs 3.24; financial 3.13 vs 3.06. Within Korea, emotional support correlated more strongly with positive self-beliefs (r = .32) and lower distress (r = −.21) than financial support did (r = .27; r = −.11). Pooled: financial support did not predict distress (β = −.011, p = .393); emotional support did (β = −.188). Cultural moderation was weak. Caveats: cross-sectional, financial scale reliability low (α = .69 in Korea), "parents" not split by mother/father. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD data]** — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)
- **Korean study of middle schoolers (2010 KSYC-Familial Consciousness survey; n = 2,829):** fathers' parenting behaviour, communication with fathers and self-concept each showed direct or indirect associations with life satisfaction (cross-sectional SEM). **[Preliminary] [OLD]** — [KoreaScience record JAKO201310559988463](https://koreascience.or.kr/article/JAKO201310559988463.page)
- **KCYPS secondary analysis (Lee & Han, *Korean Journal of Human Development*):** 4th graders (n = 2,119) and 10th graders (n = 2,108); reportedly, perceived affectionate parenting related to emotional problems in *opposite* directions for children vs adolescents. Details not verified (only a paper-sales listing was found). **[Preliminary]** — [listing](https://rss.happycampus.com/paper-doc/18417821)

### Inferences
- The father–mother gap in adolescents' reported conversation is large, stable across survey years, and population-level; it is consistent with fathers' love being *expressed* less often through talk. It says nothing about intended love, and nothing about any particular father.
- Low Korean subjective well-being cannot be attributed to parenting from these data; school competition, appearance pressure (Children's Worlds' "least positive aspect"), and response styles are competing explanations (the response-style point is an inference; see Gaps).

### Gaps
- No verified KCYPS 2018 (or other Korean panel) estimate of father vs mother communication or closeness predicting adolescent depression or life satisfaction longitudinally with within-person controls.
- 2023 Youth Comprehensive Survey and 2023 Family Survey figures (reported as: ≥2 hours of conversation up 8.7 points for fathers vs 18.0 points for mothers since 2020; "sufficient conversation" with father 48.8% vs mother 79.3%) come from single press summaries I could not trace to a fetched page — **UNVERIFIED**.
- Measurement invariance of Children's Worlds and PISA life-satisfaction items for Korea (possible avoidance of extreme responses) was not checked; Japan and the US are absent from Children's Worlds Wave 3.
- No Korean data on father–*daughter* versus father–son communication were found.

## 6. Parenting and control: does control read as warmth in Korea?

### Takeaway
Rohner & Pettengill (1985) found that Korean adolescents associated parental control with warmth, unlike US youth — but later work distinguishes *psychological* control (guilt, love withdrawal, conditional pressure), whose harmful associations with depressive symptoms look similar in Korea and Belgium. Korean helicopter-parenting studies show the same behaviour carrying two meanings (affection and pressure) with opposite associations with well-being. This is the closest Korean evidence for "love expressed ≠ love perceived."

### Cited Findings
- **Rohner & Pettengill (1985)**, "Perceived parental acceptance-rejection and parental control among Korean adolescents," *Child Development* 56(2): 524–528 (DOI verified). Measured: adolescents' perceptions of parental control, warmth and neglect. Finding: "Korean youths' perceptions of parental control are correlated positively with perceived parental warmth and low neglect," contrasting "sharply" with North American findings; cultural explanation is the authors' inference. N not in the abstract. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD]** — [doi:10.2307/1129739](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129739)
- **Soenens, Park, Vansteenkiste & Mouratidis (2012; online 2011)**, "Perceived parental psychological control and adolescent depressive experiences: A cross-cultural study with Belgian and South-Korean adolescents," *Journal of Adolescence* 35(2): 261–272 (DOI verified). Belgian n = 290, Korean n = 321; dependency-oriented vs achievement-oriented psychological control; multi-group path analysis (cross-sectional). "Associations between the domains of psychological control, depressive personality, and depressive symptoms were similar between the two samples" → effects "generalize across culture." **[Moderate/mixed]** — [doi:10.1016/j.adolescence.2011.05.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adolescence.2011.05.001)
- **Kim, A. H. (2008)**, *Korean Parents' and Adolescents' Reports of Parenting Styles: A Developmental Study*, University of Maryland dissertation: behavioural control was not always positively associated with warmth; adolescents' perceptions of maternal psychological control negatively predicted warmth for older but not younger adolescents. **[Preliminary] [OLD]** — [DRUM repository](https://drum.lib.umd.edu/handle/1903/8158)
- **Korean-validated instrument:** Park & Lee (2009) validated a domain-specific psychological control measure with 289 Korean adolescents ("achievement-oriented" and "separation-anxious" psychological control). Per abstract only. **[Preliminary] [OLD]** — [KoreaScience PDF](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200927964150980.pdf)
- Korean study (2016) of 596 5th–6th graders: **paternal** psychological control predicted children's relational aggression; maternal psychological control did not. Per abstract only. **[Preliminary]** — [KoreaScience PDF](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO201627248176574.pdf)
- **Lee & Kang (2018)**, "Perceived helicopter parenting and Korean emerging adults' psychological adjustment: The mediational role of parent–child affection and pressure from parental career expectations," *Journal of Child and Family Studies* 27(11): 3672–3686 (verified). N = 562 (269 women, 293 men), ages 19–34, unmarried students or unemployed; SEM, cross-sectional. Helicopter parenting → more depressive symptoms (directly, and indirectly via career-expectation pressure), **but** → fewer depressive symptoms and greater life satisfaction indirectly via higher parent–child affection. **[Moderate/mixed]** — [doi:10.1007/s10826-018-1193-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-018-1193-2)
- **Hwang, Jung, Kim & Hadi (2023)**, *Frontiers in Psychology* 14: 1097348: latent-class analysis of maternal and paternal helicopter parenting, US (433 mother–child, 401 father–child) and Korea (207 mother–child, 195 father–child) college students. The same three classes (strong, weak, managed) appeared in both countries; students in strong and managed classes reported *better* parent–child relationships than the weak class "regardless of parents' gender." **[Preliminary]** (small Korean n; cross-sectional) — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1097348](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1097348)
- **Jung et al. (2020; online 2019)**, "Helicopter parenting, autonomy support, and student wellbeing in the United States and South Korea," *Journal of Child and Family Studies* 29(2): 358–373 — bibliographic record verified; abstract unavailable, so findings not verified. — [doi:10.1007/s10826-019-01601-7](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-019-01601-7)
- Context (general theory, covered by other researchers): a meta-analysis of 43 studies found perceived acceptance–rejection related to adjustment at r = .51 (children) and .46 (adults) across cultures. — [Khaleque & Rohner 2002, doi:10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x)

### Inferences
- **Partial support for the translation hypothesis:** the meaning of a parental behaviour (monitoring, involvement, "helicoptering") depends on whether the child interprets it as affection or pressure, and the two interpretations predict opposite outcomes within the same Korean sample (Lee & Kang 2018).
- **Partial falsification pressure:** psychological control appears harmful in Korea as in Belgium (Soenens et al. 2012), so cultural "translation" does not neutralise every form of control. The Korean finding is better described as "behavioural control/involvement can be read as care; psychological control generally is not."
- Kim & Park's (2006) claim that pressure and guilt have *positive* consequences in Korea conflicts with Soenens et al. (2012) and with the pressure pathway in Lee & Kang (2018); the conflict is unresolved and partly reflects differing constructs (indebtedness vs intrusive control).

### Gaps
- I found **no verified Korean study of parental conditional regard** (conditional positive regard / conditional negative regard); search returned none.
- No direct modern replication of Rohner & Pettengill's warmth–control correlation in a representative Korean sample was found.
- No longitudinal Korean study separating fathers' and mothers' psychological control effects on daughters specifically.
- Kim, E. (2005), "Korean American parental control: Acceptance or rejection?" (*Ethos*) and Choi et al. (2013) on Korean American parenting measures (devotion, sacrifice, shaming) were recalled as leads but **not verified** (OpenAlex rate limit); both concern Korean *Americans*.

## 7. Filial piety and intergenerational exchange

### Takeaway
Korean filial piety has been measured as two-dimensional (behavioural and emotional) since Sung (1995), and is now promoted by a 2007 law that scholars judge largely symbolic. Nationally representative data show that intergenerational exchanges are often *unbalanced by type* (instrumental and financial flowing without emotional support), and cross-national data show Korean students receive more parental support than German or US students but are *less satisfied* with it — direct, if correlational, evidence that support provided ≠ support appreciated.

### Cited Findings
- **Sung (1990)**, "A new look at filial piety: Ideals and practices of family-centered parent care in Korea," *The Gerontologist* 30(5): 610–617 (DOI verified). Content analysis of **817 stories** of Korean filial exemplars; dimensions: "respect, responsibility, family harmony and sacrifice." **[Moderate/mixed — normative exemplars, not population behaviour] [OLD]** — [doi:10.1093/geront/30.5.610](https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/30.5.610)
- **Sung (1995)**, "Measures and dimensions of filial piety in Korea," *The Gerontologist* 35(2): 240–247 (DOI verified). Survey of **1,227 adult and pre-adult children**; factor analysis with cross-validation of affection, repayment, family harmony, respect, obligation and sacrifice → two factors, **behaviourally oriented and emotionally oriented filial piety**. A Korean-developed instrument. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD]** — [doi:10.1093/geront/35.2.240](https://doi.org/10.1093/geront/35.2.240)
- **The 2007 law:** 효행 장려 및 지원에 관한 법률 (**Act on the Encouragement and Support of Filial Piety**), promulgated 3 Aug 2007, in force 4 Aug 2008. Provisions include: state and local governments should promote filial-piety education from kindergarten to high school (Art. 5); a filial-culture promotion institute (Art. 7); **October designated as "Filial Piety Month"** (Art. 9); awards for exemplary filial conduct (Art. 10); many local governments then passed matching ordinances. **[Established — statute]** — [Korean Wikisource text (2016 amended version)](https://ko.wikisource.org/wiki/%ED%9A%A8%ED%96%89_%EC%9E%A5%EB%A0%A4_%EB%B0%8F_%EC%A7%80%EC%9B%90%EC%97%90_%EA%B4%80%ED%95%9C_%EB%B2%95%EB%A5%A0); [Jeonbuk Ilbo, 2020](https://jjan.kr/article/20201101719480)
- **Park, Hong-Jae (2015)**, "Legislating for filial piety: An indirect approach to promoting family support and responsibility for older people in Korea," *Journal of Aging & Social Policy* 27(3) (DOI verified). Policy analysis: the law requires government action to encourage filial piety and support adult children caring for parents, but "the nature of the law is largely rhetorical and symbolic rather than practical," limiting its "workability and efficiency." **[Theoretical/policy analysis]** — [doi:10.1080/08959420.2015.1024536](https://doi.org/10.1080/08959420.2015.1024536)
- **Kim, Zarit, Fingerman & Han (2015)**, "Intergenerational exchanges of middle-aged adults with their parents and parents-in-law in Korea," *Journal of Marriage and Family* 77(3): 791–805 (DOI verified). **Nationally representative, N = 920 married adults aged 30–59** with at least one living parent and parent-in-law; latent class analysis of 12 indicators (financial, instrumental, emotional support given and received). Five classes: three balanced; two unbalanced, "e.g., giving instrumental and financial but not emotional support"; a mix of patrilineal-traditional and balanced patterns; exchange patterns significantly associated with marital quality. Cross-sectional. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD data]** — [doi:10.1111/jomf.12185](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12185)
- **Fingerman et al. (2016; online 2014)**, "Parental involvement with college students in Germany, Hong Kong, Korea, and the United States," *Journal of Family Issues* 37(10): 1384–1411 (DOI verified). Germany n = 458, Hong Kong 276, **Korea 257**, US 310; multilevel models. "Asian students received more frequent parental support than German or U.S. students **but were less satisfied with that support**"; Hong Kong students co-resided more and gave more support to parents. For Korea, the published abstract groups Korean with Hong Kong students as "Asian"; Korea-specific coefficients not verified. Convenience samples. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD data]** — [doi:10.1177/0192513X14541444](https://doi.org/10.1177/0192513X14541444)
- **Dual filial piety (reciprocal vs authoritarian):** a systematic review and meta-analysis of 17 studies (2004–2022) of **Chinese and Korean adolescents** found reciprocal filial piety negatively associated with depression, anxiety, aggression, self-harm and related problems; authoritarian filial piety was not related to mental disorders overall but was negatively associated with suicide and positively with eating disorders. The number of Korean studies is not stated in the abstract. **[Moderate/mixed]** — [Han & Cheung 2024, doi:10.1007/s40894-024-00234-2](https://doi.org/10.1007/s40894-024-00234-2); model background — [Bedford & Yeh 2019, doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00100](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.00100)
- Attitude change: see Section 2 (Social Survey; Korea Welfare Panel 52.6% → 20.63% agreeing that children should fully support parents, 2007 → 2025).

### Inferences
- Sung's emotional vs behavioural distinction parallels the paper's "love expressed" channels: duty-based provision and affection-based care are empirically separable in Korean data, so a parent (or child) can score high on one and low on the other.
- The Fingerman finding (more support, less satisfaction) is among the most direct cross-national quantitative evidence for "love provided ≠ love received." It is correlational and does not show why; filial obligation and co-residence are the authors' candidate explanations.

### Gaps
- No verified Korean-specific validation of the Dual Filial Piety Scale was found (Korean samples appear in the meta-analysis only).
- No verified recent (post-2016) Korean data on adult children's *emotional* support to fathers vs mothers.
- The specific Fingerman results for Korea (e.g., satisfaction means by country) require the full text.

## 8. Emotional communication norms: affection, nunchi, jeong

### Takeaway
Empirical evidence on how often Korean parents verbally or physically express affection is sparse and dated; most claims about "Korean emotional reticence" in media are not backed by representative data. Nunchi now has a psychometrically tested scale (with cross-cultural non-invariance); jeong remains largely a philosophical/theoretical construct.

### Cited Findings
- **Lee Sang-min (이상민) (1999)**, "The differences between children's perception of the empathy of each parent: Focusing on communication type and level of caring," *Korean Journal of Counseling and Psychotherapy* 11(1): 131–151. Hypotheses supported: children's perceived empathy of **fathers** depended mainly on communication type (person-oriented vs role-oriented), whereas for **mothers** it depended mainly on level of caring. Sample size and statistics not available on the abstract page. **[Preliminary] [OLD]** — [AccessOn record](https://www.accesson.kr/kcpa/v.11/1/131/25201)
- **Physical affection (mothers, infancy):** a 2004 Korean survey of 960 mothers of 3–36-month-olds found nurturing touch used more often than rough touch, varying with maternal depression, parenting stress and attitudes (Kwak & Kim, *Korean Journal of Developmental Psychology*). Per search summary of the PDF. **[Preliminary] [OLD]** — [AccessOn PDF](https://accesson.kr/jkdp/assets/pdf/17898/journal-17-1-1.pdf)
- Perceived emotional support from parents is lower among Korean than US adolescents (2.78 vs 3.02 on a 4-point scale; 2010 data) — see Section 5. **[Moderate/mixed] [OLD data]** — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)
- **Nunchi:** Kim, So Yoon (2025), "Nunchi across cultures: Cultural interpretation and psychometric validation in South Korea and the United States," *Cross-Cultural Research* 60(1): 23–50. CFA "preliminarily" supports two factors, **Grasp** (perceiving social cues) and **Adjust** (responding appropriately), with adequate reliability in both countries; measurement-invariance and DIF analyses show Koreans and Americans interpret items differently. Higher nunchi relates to lower autistic traits, greater quality of life and stronger cultural tightness in both groups; collectivism correlated with nunchi only among Americans. **[Preliminary]** — [doi:10.1177/10693971251360452](https://doi.org/10.1177/10693971251360452)
- **Jeong (정/情):** recent scholarship is philosophical and theological (e.g., an edited Palgrave volume on emotions/jeong in Korean philosophy and religion; an ecofeminist reading of jeong in Korean Confucianism and family experience). No validated jeong scale with population data was found. **[Theoretical]** — [Chung & Oh 2022, doi:10.1007/978-3-030-94747-7_1](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-94747-7_1); [Oh 2022, doi:10.1007/978-3-030-94747-7_12](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-94747-7_12)

### Inferences
- If nunchi (reading unspoken cues) is a normative communication skill, care may be *expected* to be inferred rather than stated, which would raise the stakes of "translation" when one party expects explicit verbal empathy. This is a theoretical extrapolation, **not tested** in parent–child dyads.
- Lee (1999) is the only Korean result found that directly separates which parental behaviour drives a child's *perception* of empathy for fathers vs mothers; it is old and its sample unknown, so it can motivate but not support a claim.

### Gaps
- No representative Korean data on the frequency of parents saying "I love you" (사랑해), hugging, or other verbal/physical affection toward school-age or adult children, and none comparing fathers with mothers or daughters with sons.
- No validated jeong instrument; nunchi has not been studied in parent–child relationships.
- Korean Children's Panel (PSKC) and KCYPS affection items were not mined.

## 9. Demographic context

### Takeaway
Korea's family demography is changing fast and recently reversed some trends: TFR rose to 0.80 in 2025 after a record low, marriages rose for a third year, divorces fell, single-person households are the largest type (36.1% in 2024), and about half of 19–34-year-olds live with parents.

### Cited Findings
- **Total fertility rate 2025: 0.80** (final, released 26 Aug 2026; +0.05 from 2024, i.e., 2024 = 0.75); births 254,341 (+6.7%); crude birth rate 5.0; preliminary estimate released 25 Feb 2026 (254,500 births); first return to the 0.8 range since 2021 (0.81); mean maternal age 33.8; 37.3% of births to mothers aged 35+. Monthly TFR for May 2026: 0.85 (births up for 23 consecutive months). **[Established]** — [e-나라지표 TFR indicator](https://www.index.go.kr/unify/idx-info.do?idxCd=1011); [Newsis, 25 Feb 2026](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260225_0003525439); [Newsis, 29 Jul 2026](https://mobile.newsis.com/view/NISX20260729_0003727581)
- **Single-person households 2024: about 8.05 million, 36.1% of all households**, the largest household type. **[Established]** — [Korea Times, 9 Dec 2025](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/southkorea/society/20251209/single-person-households-become-largest-household-type-in-korea-in-2024-data)
- **Divorce and marriage 2025 (released 19 Mar 2026):** about 88,000 divorces (−3.3%); about 240,000 marriages (+8.1%), the third straight annual increase. **[Established]** — [e-나라지표 marriage/divorce indicators](https://www.index.go.kr/unify/idx-info.do?idxCd=1579); [The Star (ANN), 19 Mar 2026](https://www.thestar.com.my/aseanplus/aseanplus-news/2026/03/19/weddings-in-south-korea-reach-highest-number-since-2018)
- **Young adults living with parents — 2024 Youth Life Survey (Office for Government Policy Coordination; ~15,000 households; ages 19–34; released 11 Mar 2025):** 54.4% live with parents and 45.6% live independently; among those living with parents, 38.0% have a concrete plan to move out. **[Established; definition caveat]** — [Newsis, 11 Mar 2025](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20250311_0003094021)
- Census microdata 1975–2020: married-child co-residence has nearly disappeared; unmarried-adult-child co-residence ("two-adult-generation households") has grown. **[Preliminary — preprint]** — [Dai & Esteve 2025](https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-8008001/v1)
- Seoul sample of never-married adults aged 35+ living with parents (250 adult children + 250 parents aged ≤75): the adult child's financial support, psychological reliance in both directions, and relationship quality were associated with life satisfaction. **[Preliminary]** — [Kim & Lee 2021, doi:10.6115/fer.2021.027](https://doi.org/10.6115/fer.2021.027)

### Inferences
- Prolonged co-residence of unmarried adult children with parents increases daily father–adult-child contact in many families, so "distance" between fathers and adult daughters cannot be assumed from structural absence. Inference only; no Korean data on co-resident father–daughter communication were found.

### Gaps
- Crude divorce rate for 2025 not captured; divorce by marriage duration (e.g., "grey divorce") not verified.
- Young-adult co-residence series are not harmonised across surveys (Youth Life Survey vs census vs KIHASA Family and Fertility Survey).

## 10. Cross-national comparisons on the same indicators

### Takeaway
On identically defined OECD indicators, Korea is an outlier on old-age poverty and fertility and above average on working hours, but closer to Japan, Taiwan and Hong Kong than to Europe on child well-being. On psychological processes (psychological control, helicopter parenting, perceived support), the cross-national studies mostly find **similar associations** across cultures with **different levels**.

### Inferences
- The recurring pattern "same associations, different levels" (Soenens; Chentsova-Dutton; Hwang) argues against treating Korea as psychologically exceptional; Korean distinctiveness is better located in *levels* and *structural conditions* (hours, education spending, old-age poverty) than in different *rules* linking care to well-being.
- Fingerman et al. is the main exception pointing to a culture-linked *perception* gap (more support, less satisfaction) — the most relevant cross-national datum for the paper's hypothesis, and one worth replicating.
- OECD hours-worked series are generally recommended for comparing trends rather than levels across countries because national sources differ (a standard OECD caveat, not re-verified in this session); the Korea–OECD gap should therefore be read as indicative.

### Gaps
- Comparable fathers'-share-of-leave figures for Japan, Germany, France and the US were not verified (definitions differ widely: take-up rate vs share of recipients vs share of leave days).
- 2025 TFRs for Taiwan, France, Germany and the US were not verified from official sources.
- No comparable cross-national data on fathers' verbal affection or father–adolescent conversation including Korea were found (HBSC does not include Korea).

## 11. Evidence quality, within-Korea variation, Korean instruments, and citation verification

### Takeaway
The robust Korean evidence is structural and descriptive (official statistics on hours, time use, leave, spending, attitudes, poverty, demography). Evidence on the *meaning* of care inside families (how provision is intended and perceived) is mostly cross-sectional, often old, frequently based on convenience samples, and sometimes produced by a single research group. Variation by class, cohort, earner type and firm size is documented and large; variation by child gender (daughters vs sons) is almost undocumented.

### Inferences
- For the paper's falsification goal, the strongest Korean-relevant tests are: (a) Lee & Kang (2018) — the same parental behaviour yields opposite associations depending on the child's construal (supports the translation hypothesis); (b) Soenens et al. (2012) — psychological control harms regardless of cultural framing (limits it); (c) Fingerman et al. (2016) — more support but less satisfaction among Asian students (supports provided ≠ received); (d) Chentsova-Dutton et al. (2020) — emotional support tracks well-being more closely than financial support but cultural moderation is weak (suggests the "amount and type of perceived support" matter similarly across cultures, which cuts against a strong culture-specific translation story).
- None of these studies are dyadic father–daughter designs measuring intention, expression, perception and receipt separately; the hypothesis's chain cannot be tested with existing Korean data — a genuine gap rather than evidence for or against.

### Gaps
- No Korean dyadic (parent and child both reporting) study of intended vs perceived love was found.
- Daughter vs son differences in perceiving fathers' provision are essentially undocumented in the verified sources.
- Several leads remain UNVERIFIED due to API rate limits: Kim, E. (2005, *Ethos*); Choi et al. (2013, Korean American parenting measures); Korean Dual Filial Piety Scale validation; kirogi father health studies.
- Most psychological studies use data from 2010 or earlier ([OLD]); given the 2014–2024 shifts in father time and leave, re-estimation on 2020s cohorts is needed before generalising.
