# Love, needs, attachment, commitment and compatibility: conceptual foundations and outcome evidence (dossier for "Can love be lost in translation?")

Hypothesis under test (H): "Love intended ≠ love expressed ≠ love perceived ≠ love received ≠ relationship compatibility; many relationship problems come from the translation of care between people rather than from the amount of love." These notes look for disconfirming evidence as hard as for supporting evidence.

Legend. Evidence grades: **[E]** Established; **[M]** Moderate or mixed; **[P]** Preliminary; **[T]** Theoretical proposition; **[SPEC]** speculative extrapolation (my inference, untested). Design codes: X-sec = cross-sectional correlational; Long = prospective longitudinal; Diary/ESM = daily diary or experience sampling; Exp = experiment; RCT; Meta = meta-analysis. **WEIRD** = sample drawn only from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations. "Measured" is what the study observed; "Inferred" is the authors' interpretation. Every DOI below was resolved against Crossref and/or OpenAlex/Europe PMC on 2026-11-04 unless it is marked UNVERIFIED (Section 10 has the full log). Details that come only from secondary summaries are labelled "secondary".

## 1. Is "love" one scientific construct?

### Takeaway
No. Lay "love" is a prototype-organized family concept with fuzzy borders, and the research measures split into several partly independent kinds (passionate/romantic, intimacy/companionate, commitment, compassionate/caring, attachment, plus "obsession"). A meta-analytic factor analysis does find a *general* love factor, but it sits next to separable "romantic obsession" and "practical friendship" factors. Self-reported love also loads with satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust and passion on one higher-order "perceived relationship quality" factor. So "amount of love" is not one well-defined quantity, and in self-report it is hard to separate empirically from relationship satisfaction.

### Cited Findings
**Prototype (lay-concept) analyses**
- Fehr & Russell (1991), *JPSP* 60(3):425–438. Six studies found that the natural-language concept of love is better described by prototype theory than by a classical definition built on necessary-and-sufficient features. Love has an internal structure and fuzzy borders. Subtypes (maternal love, romantic love, affection, love of work, self-love, infatuation, …) can be reliably ordered from better to poorer examples, and a subtype's prototypicality predicted how it was processed cognitively. Measured: goodness-of-example ratings and processing indices. Inferred: a scientific definition should respect this graded structure. [E for the structure of the lay concept; samples were students, country not verified] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.60.3.425](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.60.3.425)
- Fehr (1988), *JPSP* 55(4):557–579, "Prototype analysis of the concepts of love and commitment". This is a multi-study prototype analysis in which features of love and of commitment were listed and then rated for centrality. Secondary summaries (not checked against the article) report that central features were more salient in memory and that violating a central feature was judged to lower love more than violating a peripheral one. [M] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.55.4.557](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.55.4.557); secondary summary via [UBC repository record](https://circle.ubc.ca/handle/2429/27073)
- Aron & Westbay (1996), *JPSP* 70(3):535–551. They factor-analyzed centrality ratings of the 68 love-prototype features from Fehr (1988) and recovered three dimensions, passion, intimacy and commitment, which link the prototype approach to Sternberg's triangle. (The bibliographic record was verified; the content comes from a secondary summary.) [M] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.70.3.535](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.70.3.535)

**Theoretical typologies**
- Berscheid (2010), *Annual Review of Psychology* 61:1–25. Berscheid argues that the "failure to explicate the love construct" is "the major obstacle" to studying love. She calls for a *temporal* model and proposes tracking the presence or absence of **four types of love**, each with different causal conditions and therefore a different time course over a relationship. Secondary summaries list the four as romantic/passionate, companionate, compassionate and adult-attachment love. Fehr et al. (2014) confirm that compassionate love is one of Berscheid's major types. [T] — [doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.093008.100318](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.psych.093008.100318); secondary list via [Fehr et al. 2014 abstract](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407514533768)
- Sternberg (1986), *Psychological Review* 93(2):119–135. Love has three components: intimacy, passion, and decision/commitment. The *amount* of love depends on the absolute strength of the components and the *kind* of love on their relative strengths. [T] — [doi:10.1037/0033-295X.93.2.119](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.93.2.119)
- Sternberg (1997), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 27(3):313–335, a construct validation of the Triangular Love Scale (STLS) across two studies. In the author's words, the data were "generally, but not completely supportive." [M] — [doi:10.1002/(SICI)1099-0992(199705)27:3<313::AID-EJSP824>3.0.CO;2-4](https://doi.org/10.1002/%28SICI%291099-0992%28199705%2927%3A3%3C313%3A%3AAID-EJSP824%3E3.0.CO%3B2-4)
- Sorokowski et al. (2021; online 2020), *Journal of Sex Research* 58(1):106–115. More than 11,000 people were tested, and the final N was 7,332 across **25 countries on all inhabited continents**. Configural, metric and scalar invariance of the STLS were all supported, and component levels varied with relationship duration in the direction the theory predicts. Design: X-sec, convenience samples. Measured: measurement invariance. Inferred: the construct is culturally universal. [E for invariance of the three-component *measurement model*; this does not show that love is a single thing] — [doi:10.1080/00224499.2020.1787318](https://doi.org/10.1080/00224499.2020.1787318)
- Hendrick & Hendrick (1986), *JPSP* 50(2):392–402, the Love Attitudes Scale. Six love styles (Eros, Ludus, Storge, Pragma, Mania, Agape) emerged clearly in factor analyses (N = 807, replicated with N = 567), and the scales had low intercorrelations, i.e., distinct "kinds" of love orientation. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.50.2.392](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.50.2.392)
- Rubin (1970), *JPSP* 16(2):265–273. The first love scale (affiliative/dependent need, predisposition to help, exclusiveness/absorption) correlated only moderately with "liking", and high-love couples gazed at each other more. [historical; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/h0029841](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0029841)

**Is there a general factor?**
- Graham (2011; online 2010), *JSPR* 28(6):748–771. Meta-analytic factor analysis of 81 studies (103 samples, N = 19,387) yielded factors for **general love, romantic obsession and practical friendship**. Love correlated positively, and obsession negatively, with relationship satisfaction and length. Graham calls for measurement methods beyond global self-report. [M–E] — [doi:10.1177/0265407510389126](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407510389126)
- Acevedo & Aron (2009), *Review of General Psychology* 13(1):59–65. Romantic love (intensity, engagement, sexual interest) without the obsession component exists in long-term marriages. Latent factors for romantic love and obsession were independent. In a meta-analysis of 25 studies, romantic love was strongly associated with satisfaction in both long- and short-term relationships, while obsession was negatively associated in long-term and positively in short-term relationships. [M] — [doi:10.1037/a0014226](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014226)
- Fletcher, Simpson & Thomas (2000), *PSPB* 26(3):340–354 (Perceived Relationship Quality Components, PRQC). The best-fitting CFA model had items loading on **six first-order factors (satisfaction, commitment, intimacy, trust, passion, love) that in turn loaded on one second-order factor of perceived relationship quality**, replicated in a second sample and across sex. [E] — [doi:10.1177/0146167200265007](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167200265007)
- Joel et al. (2020) excluded trust, intimacy, love and passion from their main predictor models because these are components of relationship quality (overlap with the outcome); love entered only supplementary analyses. — [PMC7431040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7431040/) / [doi:10.1073/pnas.1917036117](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117)

**Passionate and compassionate love**
- Hatfield & Sprecher (1986), *Journal of Adolescence* 9(4):383–410, development of the Passionate Love Scale (PLS) with reliability and validity evidence. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1016/S0140-1971(86)80043-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-1971%2886%2980043-4). Hatfield, Bensman & Rapson (2012), *JSPR* 29(2):143–164, review numerous passionate/romantic love scales built over about 60 years and note that no single compendium exists — [doi:10.1177/0265407511431055](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407511431055)
- Sprecher & Fehr (2005), *JSPR* 22(5):629–651, the Compassionate Love Scale. It was developed in three samples (N = 529) and validated in three studies (N = 700). Compassionate love related to prosocial behavior toward close others and humanity and was distinct from empathy. The relationship-specific version was associated with *providing* social support. [M] — [doi:10.1177/0265407505056439](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407505056439)
- Fehr, Harasymchuk & Sprecher (2014), *JSPR* 31(5):575–600. A review plus new data separating compassionate love from the agape love style, linking it to prosocial relationship behavior, quality and stability, and highlighting "differences between compassionate love given versus received." This bears directly on H's given/received distinction. [M] — [doi:10.1177/0265407514533768](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407514533768)

**Love as observable behavior and signal**
- Schoenfeld, Bredow & Huston (2012), *PSPB* 38(11):1396–1409. Four waves over 13 years of marriage, analyzed with multilevel models. Both husbands and wives showed love through affection. Wives expressed love by enacting *fewer negative/antagonistic behaviors*; husbands did so by initiating sex, sharing leisure activities and doing household work together with their wives. Measured: covariation of self-reported love with behavior reports. Inferred: men and women "show love" in more nuanced ways than stereotypes suggest. [M; Long-correlational; WEIRD (US)] — [doi:10.1177/0146167212450739](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212450739)
- Gonzaga, Keltner, Londahl & Smith (2001), *JPSP* 81(2):247–262. Love correlated with approach states (desire, sympathy). Four affiliation cues (head nods, Duchenne smiles, gesticulation, forward leans) correlated with self-reports *and partner estimates* of love, and love experience and display correlated with commitment-enhancing processes under threat. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.81.2.247](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.81.2.247)

### Inferences
- For H, "love intended" has to be specified by component: (i) love as feeling or evaluation (passion, intimacy), (ii) love as motivation to promote the partner's welfare (compassionate love), (iii) love as decision or commitment, (iv) love as an attachment bond, (v) love as enacted behavior. These have distinct measures and partly distinct correlates (Berscheid; Graham; Hendrick & Hendrick; Sprecher & Fehr). [Inference, grounded in the findings above]
- **Measurement threat to H.** Global self-reported love shares a higher-order factor with satisfaction (PRQC; Graham's general factor). A test of "amount of love vs translation" that uses self-reported love as the "amount" and satisfaction as the outcome is close to tautological. "Amount" needs separate operationalization, e.g., partner-reported intended love, observed affection or compassionate-love behavior. [Inference; methodological]
- Love behaviors differ across people and genders (Schoenfeld et al.), which is a precondition for translation problems. That study, however, did not test whether spouses *misread* each other's love behaviors. Gonzaga et al. show partners' estimates of love partly track nonverbal displays, which is evidence that love is at least partly "legible" without any special language. [Inference]

### Gaps
- Fehr (1988) feature lists and centrality ratings, and Fehr & Russell's (1991) exact ordering of love subtypes, were not retrieved (abstracts only).
- Berscheid's four-type list and temporal propositions come from secondary summaries.
- PRQC inter-factor correlations (the size of the overlap) were not retrieved.
- No Korean validations of STLS, PLS, CLS, LAS or PRQC turned up in Crossref searches. Korean research is covered by another researcher.

## 2. Chapman's love languages vs. responsiveness and need-fulfillment frameworks: what has actually been tested?

### Takeaway
The love-language (LL) evidence base is small, mostly cross-sectional and single-reporter, and mostly US online or student samples (with a few Indonesian, Turkish and Chinese/Malaysian ones). The three core claims fare as follows:
- **(a) Each person has a primary language:** weak. In the only preregistered test, fewer than half of participants had an identifiable primary language.
- **(b) There are five languages:** mixed. CFA supported five factors in some small samples, failed in Indonesian couples, and 7–10-factor solutions fit better in larger US samples.
- **(c) Matching the partner's primary language raises satisfaction:** mostly null in direct tests. The positive results come from indices that confound "the right kind" with "enough" received affection, i.e., perceived responsiveness or need fulfillment.

Compared with responsiveness and need fulfillment (meta-analyses, longitudinal and multi-country studies, a 43-dataset machine-learning synthesis, physiological outcomes), the LL base is far weaker. Its defensible core, that people feel loved when a partner gives what they value, is already the core of responsiveness and need-fulfillment theory. The channel-specific "matching" increment above that has not been shown.

### Cited Findings
**The critique and the three assumptions**
- Impett, Park & Muise (2024), *Current Directions in Psychological Science* 33(2):87–92. A narrative review concluding that the "paucity of empirical work … does not provide strong empirical support for the book's three central assumptions": (a) each person has a preferred love language, (b) there are five, (c) couples are more satisfied when partners speak each other's preferred language. The authors propose a replacement metaphor: love as a **"balanced diet"** in which people need "a full range of essential nutrients." They attribute LL popularity to the framework helping people identify needs, being intuitive, and offering a simple improvement route. [review; conclusion graded M] — [doi:10.1177/09637214231217663](https://doi.org/10.1177/09637214231217663)
- Karantzas (2023), Greater Good / *The Conversation* (secondary commentary, not peer-reviewed). The book has sold about 20 million copies in 49 languages, and no reliability or validity findings for Chapman's online quiz have been published. He links the valid kernel to "responding to a partner in a way that meets their needs and wants." — [Greater Good](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/is_there_science_behind_the_five_love_languages)

**Claim (b): factor structure / number of languages**
- Egbert & Polk (2006), *Communication Research Reports* 23(1):19–26. A five-factor CFA fit better than one-, three- and four-factor solutions. LL factors correlated with Stafford, Dainton & Haas's (2000) relational maintenance typology, so "Chapman's love languages may reflect behaviors performed to enact intended relational maintenance." N and sample were not retrieved. [P] — [doi:10.1080/17464090500535822](https://doi.org/10.1080/17464090500535822)
- Surijah & Septiarly (2016), *ANIMA Indonesian Psychological Journal* 31(2):65–76. N = 400 Indonesian students (M age 19.85). 17 items were retained, composite reliability was .884, and CFA supported five factors. [P; non-WEIRD; students] — [doi:10.24123/aipj.v31i2.565](https://doi.org/10.24123/aipj.v31i2.565)
- Surijah, Prasetyaningsih & Supriyadi (2021), *Psympathic* 7(2):155–168. 250 Indonesian married couples, using rating-scale and forced-choice formats. **The five-factor solution was not supported, and LL compatibility did not affect marital satisfaction.** [P; null; non-WEIRD] — [doi:10.15575/psy.v7i2.6634](https://doi.org/10.15575/psy.v7i2.6634)
- Pett, Lozano & Varga (2022), *Communication Reports* 36(1):54–67. Qualitative coding of 648 open-ended responses from 324 college-aged participants found "substantial support" for the five plus "a novel, sixth love language." [P; qualitative; students] — [doi:10.1080/08934215.2022.2113549](https://doi.org/10.1080/08934215.2022.2113549)
- Flicker, Sancier-Barbosa, Clemons-Castanos, Field, Jackson-Zambon, Phelan & Impett (2025), *Journal of Marital and Family Therapy* 51(4). Three US online studies (N = 696, 500, 499) used a bottom-up revision with EFA and CFA. **Solutions with 7–10 love languages fit better and predicted relationship quality better than the five-factor solution.** [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1111/jmft.70072](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.70072)
- Surijah & Kirana (2020), "Five Love Languages Scale Factor Analysis," *Makara Human Behavior Studies in Asia* 24(1). The bibliographic record was verified but the results were not retrieved. — [doi:10.7454/hubs.asia.2201118](https://doi.org/10.7454/hubs.asia.2201118)

**Claim (a): does each person have one primary language?**
- Flicker, Sancier-Barbosa & Impett (2025), *JMFT* 51(4), "Revisiting the Five Love Languages Framework." **Preregistered**, N = 499 individuals in long-term cohabiting relationships (US). **"Less than half of participants had an identifiable primary love language."** Satisfaction with the partner's expression of one's primary LL was "no stronger a predictor of relationship quality than satisfaction with other love language behaviors." Quality was more strongly linked to satisfaction across a wider range of behaviors. Robust predictors were verbal affirmations, encouragement for individual pursuits, support during difficult times, and accountability. Design: X-sec, single reporter. [M; null on core claims; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1111/jmft.70078](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.70078) (PMID 40977357)
- Hughes & Camden (2020), *Psi Chi Journal of Psychological Research* 25(3):234–244. The primary text was inaccessible (the publisher returned 403 and the OpenAlex abstract is corrupted), so these details are secondary. About 1,000 US adults took part ("nearly a thousand"; one secondary source says 981). Preferred-language percentages were time 40.8%, touch 40.0%, words 22.7%, service 13.6% and gifts 4.0%. These sum to about 121%, implying many respondents endorsed more than one "primary" language. Perceiving that one's partner used one's preferred LL well was associated with more love and satisfaction, which commentators describe as "partial support." [P; secondary; X-sec] — [doi:10.24839/2325-7342.JN25.3.234](https://doi.org/10.24839/2325-7342.JN25.3.234); [Lewandowski, Psychology Today 2021](https://psychologytoday.com/ie/blog/the-psychology-relationships/202111/how-does-your-love-language-impact-your-relationship); [Her Campus summary (N = 981)](https://hercampus.com/?p=1335357)
- No taxometric or latent-class test of whether people fall into LL "types" was found (see Gaps). By analogy, a taxometric study of attachment, another popular typology, found individual differences to be **dimensional rather than categorical** — Fraley, Hudson, Heffernan & Segal (2015), *JPSP* 109(2):354–368. [E for attachment; analogy only] — [doi:10.1037/pspp0000027](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000027)

**Claim (c): does matching predict satisfaction?**
- Polk (2013), *The Open Communication Journal* 7(1):1–11 (often cited as Polk & Egbert; Crossref and OpenAlex list D. M. Polk as sole author). 83 couples. Few couples met Chapman's criteria for high relational quality. Matched and mismatched couples showed *less* discrepancy in relational quality than partially matched couples. Other ANOVA/MANOVA tests gave "little empirical support." [P; null/mixed] — [doi:10.2174/1874916X20130423001](https://doi.org/10.2174/1874916X20130423001)
- Bunt & Hazelwood (2017), *Personal Relationships* 24(2):280–290. 67 heterosexual couples, dyadic X-sec. **"Limited evidence that love language alignment promotes satisfaction; self-regulation contributed greater variance."** Female self-regulation predicted both partners' satisfaction when primary LLs were dissimilar. [P; null on matching] — [doi:10.1111/pere.12182](https://doi.org/10.1111/pere.12182)
- Mostova, Stolarski & Matthews (2022), *PLOS ONE* 17(6):e0269429. 100 heterosexual couples recruited via social media and personal contacts. They were English-speaking (native or B2+ fluency, multicultural, authors in Warsaw), and data were collected Oct 2018–Mar 2019. "Mismatch" was the **sum of discrepancies across all five LLs between one's preferred reception and the partner's expression**. Mismatch predicted satisfaction: for men's relationship satisfaction, men's own mismatch β = −.36 (ΔR² = .13) and women's mismatch β = −.40 (ΔR² = .16). Empathy related to matching for men only. LL mismatch was uncorrelated with relationship length (r = .01). The authors read this as partners not adjusting over time, though the design is cross-sectional. They state that the study "is the first … that found empirical support" for the LL hypothesis. [P; X-sec; mixed-culture sample, a confound the authors acknowledge] — [doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0269429](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0269429) / [PMC9216579](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9216579/)
  - The discrepancy index rises when the partner expresses *less* of what one wants, so it mixes "wrong kind" with "not enough." It therefore cannot separate translation from amount. [Inference]
- Coy & Rodriguez (2023), *JMFT* 49(4):741–761. 84 couples, explicitly framed "through the lens of responsiveness." **People held biased understandings of their partner's preferences, and those biases shaped how they expressed affection. Accurate understanding of partner preferences was associated with greater satisfaction.** [P; X-sec dyadic] — [doi:10.1111/jmft.12655](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.12655)
- Flicker & Sancier-Barbosa (2024; print 2025), *JMFT* 51(1). n = 696 US adults (CloudResearch; M age 43.8; 70% married; 97.3% cohabiting). **"Chapman's hypothesis was not supported."** Satisfaction with the partner's primary-LL behavior predicted relationship satisfaction and perceived love no better than lower-ranked LLs, and Words of Affirmation and Quality Time predicted better than one's own primary LL. [M; null; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1111/jmft.12747](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.12747)
- İnce & Işık (2021; print 2022), *American Journal of Family Therapy* 50(4):407–423. 161 Turkish married couples, analyzed with a Common Fate Model. Four of the five LL components (physical touch, words of affirmation, quality time, receiving gifts) mediated the link from differentiation of self to marital satisfaction. This tests *levels* of LL behaviors, not matching. [P; X-sec; non-WEIRD] — [doi:10.1080/01926187.2021.1930607](https://doi.org/10.1080/01926187.2021.1930607)
- Pagan, Langlais & Chacón (2025), *The Family Journal* 33(4):577–586. Qualitative, N = 49 students. "Intentionality behind the actions … takes precedence over any categorization"; LLs "categorize different approaches to love, but do not appear to contribute to experiences within relationships." [P; qualitative] — [doi:10.1177/10664807241312204](https://doi.org/10.1177/10664807241312204)

**Interventions**
- Chen et al. (2025), *BMC Psychology* (PMID 40635016). A mixed-methods **RCT** of a "Love Language Game" against an **active control** (an alternative relationship-enhancement workshop series), with Chinese couples in China (n = 40) and Malaysia (n = 40; unit as reported in Table 1). The authors report improved satisfaction in the LL arm, moderated by individualism–collectivism. Stated limitations: small sample and brief follow-up. [P; tiny RCT; non-WEIRD] — [doi:10.1186/s40359-025-02865-z](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-025-02865-z) / [PMC12243193](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12243193/)
- Nichols et al. (2018), *Journal of Human Sciences and Extension*. An exploratory Extension program in seven rural US counties comparing a booster group with a no-booster group, with **no untreated control**. Knowledge and confidence rose, and partner empathy improved in all participants and more in the booster group. [P] — [doi:10.54718/vbdz5406](https://doi.org/10.54718/vbdz5406)

**Overlap with relational maintenance**
- Stafford & Canary (1991), *JSPR* 8(2):217–242. Five maintenance strategies (positivity, openness, assurances, social networks, sharing tasks). Positivity, assurances and sharing tasks were consistent, strong predictors of control mutuality, commitment, liking and satisfaction. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1177/0265407591082004](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407591082004)
- Ogolsky & Bowers (2013; online 2012), *JSPR* 30(3):343–367. Meta-analysis of 35 studies (N = 12,273). All five maintenance factors correlated positively with satisfaction, commitment, control mutuality, love and liking. **Effects were larger for perceptions of the partner's maintenance than for one's own enactment**, and larger for women. [E; mostly WEIRD] — [doi:10.1177/0265407512463338](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407512463338)

**Received/perceived vs given: the most relevant nearby evidence**
- Halamová (2026), *Frontiers in Psychology* 17:1773641. 545 Slovak heterosexual couples (1,090 people; quota-sampled online panel) in an extreme-groups design: distressed (CSI < 51.5, n = 213) vs highly satisfied (CSI > 78, n = 198). **Perceptions of the partner's behavior separated the groups more than perceptions of one's own behavior** (perceived partner attachment behavior d = 2.64 vs own d = 2.32; perceived partner regard d = 2.45 vs own d = 1.96). Only partner-perception measures uniquely predicted group membership (attraction OR = 27.58; perceived partner attachment OR = 4.46). Design: X-sec. The author notes that causal direction is unknown. [P; non-Anglophone but WEIRD] — [doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1773641](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1773641)

**Comparison: responsiveness and need fulfillment evidence (detail is covered by other dossiers)**
- Joel et al. (2020), *PNAS* 117(32):19061–19071: 43 datasets and 11,196 couples. Perceived partner responsiveness (PPR) was among the top relationship-specific predictors of quality (69% success rate, tied with conflict), behind perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction and perceived partner satisfaction. [E for predictive importance; correlational] — [PMC7431040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7431040/)
- Slatcher, Selcuk & Ong (2015), *Psychological Science* 26(7):972–982. In a large US sample of married and cohabiting couples, PPR ("feel understood, cared for, and appreciated") predicted healthier diurnal cortisol **10 years later**, robust to demographic, depressive, agreeableness and other relationship controls, and mediated by declines in negative affect. [M; Long; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1177/0956797615575022](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797615575022)
- Balzarini et al. (2023; online 2022), *SPPS* 14(3):342–355. N = 3,593 from **57 countries**, with N = 1,125 followed for 3 months. COVID-related stressors were associated with lower relationship quality, and this was buffered by high PPR. [M; observational, multi-country] — [doi:10.1177/19485506221094437](https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506221094437)
- Reis & Gable (2015), "Responsiveness," *Current Opinion in Psychology* 1:67–71 (bibliographic record verified; content not retrieved) — [doi:10.1016/j.copsyc.2015.01.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.copsyc.2015.01.001)
- For need fulfillment (diary, longitudinal, 123-country data), see Section 3.

**The closest well-powered analog to "matching" tests: ideal-partner preference matching**
- Eastwick et al. (2025; online 2024), *JPSP* 128(1):123–146. A **registered report** with the Psychological Science Accelerator: N = 10,358, 43 countries, 22 languages. Preference matching was significant. The "corrected pattern metric" across 35 traits gave β = .19 zero-order but **β = .11 when entered alongside a normative (everyone-likes-this) matching metric**, and trait-specific "level metric" interactions averaged **β = .04**. Results were similar for participants who reported their ideals before the relationship began, and individual differences did not consistently moderate the effects. [E; X-sec] — [doi:10.1037/pspp0000524](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000524)

### Inferences
- **Is LL matching a special case of responsiveness or need fulfillment?** Probably yes. Every positive LL result measures some version of "my partner gives me what I want or value": Hughes & Camden's perceived partner skill, Mostova's discrepancy between one's preferences and the partner's expression, and Coy & Rodriguez's accuracy about partner preferences. That is close to the definition of PPR ("understands, validates, cares") and of need fulfillment. The two direct tests of the *distinctive* LL claim, that the primary channel carries special weight (Flicker & Sancier-Barbosa 2024; Flicker et al. 2025 preregistered), found no special weight. Breadth of loving behavior mattered more. [M-level inference]
- **The test H needs is pattern vs level.** Does receiving love in the "wrong kind" hurt beyond receiving "too little"? Eastwick et al. (2025) is the best-powered analog. There, idiosyncratic matching shrinks by about 40% once normative desirability is controlled, and trait-specific matching is about β = .04. This suggests that channel-specific "translation" effects, if they exist, are small relative to the overall level of perceived care. [SPEC: an analogy from partner traits to love behaviors]
- The three Flicker papers report N = 696 and N = 499 in more than one article, so they may reuse samples. Treat them as about two independent US samples, not five. [Inference]
- What partially supports H: perceptions of the partner's behavior predict satisfaction more than one's own loving behavior (Halamová 2026; Ogolsky & Bowers 2013), and partners hold biased models of each other's preferences that shape what they express (Coy & Rodriguez 2023). This is consistent with "expressed ≠ received." However, all of these are cross-sectional and share method variance with satisfaction, and none rules out reverse causation (satisfied people perceive more love). [Inference]
- What disconfirms H's popular-psychology version: the strongest tests show the specific code (primary language) does not matter more than overall loving behavior. If "translation" is defined as channel mismatch, the data so far favor an "amount or breadth" account. [Inference; M]

### Gaps
- **No taxometric, latent-profile or latent-class test** of whether people have one "primary" language was found. The nearest evidence is the categorical identifiability result in Flicker et al. (2025, preregistered) and an unpublished thesis (Dobson 2024, UNM, n = 740) reporting that no CFA model was retained; it is not peer reviewed and was not verified.
- **No large multi-country validation**, no longitudinal test of matching predicting *change*, and no preregistered dyadic test of match vs level (polynomial regression or response-surface analysis) were found through November 2026.
- Hughes & Camden (2020) details are secondary only. Egbert & Polk (2006) N was not retrieved. Surijah & Kirana (2020) results were not retrieved. Effect sizes and unit of randomization in Chen et al. (2025) were not extracted.
- No study tested whether Chapman's quiz categories outperform a generic "received affection" or PPR measure in the same sample. That head-to-head comparison is the critical missing test.

## 3. Need fulfillment (self-determination theory, dependence model, need to belong): does it predict outcomes, and is aggregation additive or non-compensatory?

### Takeaway
Need fulfillment is one of the best-supported explanatory constructs. Relationship-specific need fulfillment predicts attachment security with that partner, daily emotions, relationship functioning, and staying vs leaving (diary and longitudinal data). Need satisfaction predicts well-being across 123 countries and four cultures with measurement equivalence. On aggregation, each need has a unique additive effect, but balanced satisfaction predicts well-being beyond the sum (a non-compensatory component). Cross-nationally, needs relate to well-being largely independently of whether other needs are met, which argues against a strict Maslow-type hierarchy.

### Cited Findings
- Baumeister & Leary (1995), *Psychological Bulletin* 117(3):497–529. This review argues that the need to belong (frequent, nonaversive interactions within an ongoing bond) is a fundamental motive. Supporting evidence includes satiation and substitution effects: one bond can stand in for another. [E as a review; "fundamental" status is T] — [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.497](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.117.3.497)
- Drigotas & Rusbult (1992), *JPSP* 62(1):62–87, the **dependence model**. Dependence is high "when important outcomes in the current relationship are not available elsewhere." Need-satisfaction dependence measures compare how well important needs are met in the current relationship vs alternatives. In **two longitudinal studies** these measures distinguished people who stayed from people who voluntarily broke up, and the studies tested whether commitment mediates the effect. Measured: need ratings and later breakup status. Inferred: dependence, not satisfaction alone, drives staying. [M–E; Long; WEIRD students] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.62.1.62](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.62.1.62)
- Le & Agnew (2001), *JSPR* 18(3):423–440. Daily reports over four consecutive days: fulfillment of relationship needs predicted daily emotional experience, sometimes moderated by long-distance vs local status. **Unequal dependence** (one partner more dependent) predicted less positive and more negative relationship emotion. [M; Diary; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1177/0265407501183007](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407501183007)
- La Guardia, Ryan, Couchman & Deci (2000), *JPSP* 79(3):367–384. Three studies found substantial **within-person variation in attachment security across partners** (mother, father, romantic partner, best friend). Relationship-level fulfillment of autonomy, competence and relatedness predicted security in *that* relationship. [E; multilevel; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.79.3.367](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.3.367)
- Patrick, Knee, Canevello & Lonsbary (2007), *JPSP* 92(3):434–457. Study 1: each need uniquely predicted individual and relationship well-being, with relatedness the strongest unique predictor of relationship outcomes. Study 2: **both partners'** need fulfillment uniquely predicted one's own relationship functioning. Study 3 (diary): need fulfillment predicted better relationship quality after disagreements, mediated by autonomous (intrinsic) reasons for being in the relationship. [M–E; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.434](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.434)
- Knee, Hadden, Porter & Rodriguez (2013), *PSPR* 17(4):307–324. A theoretical integration arguing that self-determination fosters openness rather than defensiveness, perspective-taking, authenticity and support of close others. [T] — [doi:10.1177/1088868313498000](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868313498000)
- Sheldon & Niemiec (2006), *JPSP* 91(2):331–341. Four studies (concurrent, prospective, daily diary, observer report): **people with balanced need satisfaction reported higher well-being than people with the same sum but more variability across needs**, independent of neuroticism. This is a non-compensatory component on top of additive effects. [M–E; WEIRD; effect sizes not retrieved] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.91.2.331](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.2.331)
- Tay & Diener (2011), *JPSP* 101(2):354–365. Gallup World Poll data from **123 countries**:
  - **Life evaluation** was most associated with **basic needs**.
  - **Positive feelings** were most associated with **social and respect needs**.
  - **Negative feelings** were most associated with **basic, respect and autonomy needs**.
  - Societal need fulfillment predicted subjective well-being beyond individual fulfillment.
  - **The associations of specific needs with well-being "were largely independent of whether other needs were fulfilled"** and held after controlling for household income.
  - The ordering of fulfillment of psychosocial needs was consistent across countries, while fulfillment of basic and safety needs depended on country.

  [E; X-sec; global] — [doi:10.1037/a0023779](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023779)
- Chen, Vansteenkiste, Beyers, Boone, Deci, et al. (2015; online 2014), *Motivation and Emotion* 39(2):216–236, the Basic Psychological Need Satisfaction and Frustration Scale (BPNSFS). Study 1: Belgian and Chinese late adolescents (N = 685). Study 2: Belgium, China, USA and Peru (N = 1,051; M age ≈ 20). **The scale showed measurement equivalence, and the effects of need satisfaction and frustration on well-being and ill-being were similar across countries and not moderated by need strength** (valuation or desire). This supports the universality claim of basic psychological need theory. [E within student samples; X-sec] — [doi:10.1007/s11031-014-9450-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-014-9450-1); abstract details via [SDT site PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/01/2014_Chen-et-al._need-satisfaction.pdf)
- Vansteenkiste, Ryan & Soenens (2020), *Motivation and Emotion* 44(1):1–31, a review of advances in basic psychological need theory (bibliographic record verified; content not extracted) — [doi:10.1007/s11031-019-09818-1](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-019-09818-1)
- Finkel, Hui, Carswell & Larson (2014), *Psychological Inquiry* 25(1):1–41, the **"suffocation model."** Americans now ask marriage to meet esteem and self-actualization needs more than in the past, which requires investment many spouses do not make, so mean marital quality is declining. Proposed remedies are to optimize resources, invest more, or ask less of the marriage. [T; US-specific; contested in commentaries] — [doi:10.1080/1047840X.2014.863723](https://doi.org/10.1080/1047840X.2014.863723)
- Le, Dove, Agnew, Korn & Mutso (2010): **dependence** was among the strongest predictors of nonmarital breakup (see Section 5). — [doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x)

### Inferences
- **For aggregation in H's model:** an additive "total care received" score is defensible as a first approximation, given unique effects of each need (Patrick et al.) and the cross-national independence result (Tay & Diener). A balance or imbalance term is empirically motivated (Sheldon & Niemiec), so a scalar "amount of love" can hide chronic deficits in one need. This is the empirically grounded form of "translation" in the need literature: a partner can give a lot overall and still leave one need unmet. [Inference; M]
- Need fulfillment frameworks measure "love received" directly, with no assumed delivery categories. They already outperform LL categories on evidence quality (meta-analytic, longitudinal, cross-cultural). [Inference]
- **The dependence model is a direct alternative to H for stability.** People stay when needs cannot be met elsewhere, even if satisfaction is middling (Drigotas & Rusbult). Stability can therefore diverge from both love and quality, and Le & Agnew (2001) show that *unequal* dependence carries its own emotional cost. [Inference]
- Chen et al. (2015) found that need *strength* does not moderate need effects. If general, this argues against "different people need different kinds of love" at the level of the three basic psychological needs. Individual differences may lie more in *how* needs are best met (means) than in *which* needs matter (ends). [SPEC]

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for the balance term (Sheldon & Niemiec) and Tay & Diener's N and coefficients were not retrieved.
- No meta-analysis of *relationship-specific* need fulfillment and relationship outcomes was found.
- Which specific needs the dependence and investment literature measured (Drigotas & Rusbult 1992; Le & Agnew 2001) was not verified here.
- No Korean BPNSFS validation was found (not searched in Korean-language databases).

## 4. Attachment as a lens on perceiving care, and how attachment changes

### Takeaway
Attachment insecurity reliably predicts lower relationship quality (meta-analyses; actor effects larger than partner effects). Experimentally, insecure people appraise *identical, standardized* support messages more negatively **only when those messages are ambiguous**. Clearly supportive notes were appraised the same by secure and insecure recipients. This is the best experimental evidence for "expressed ≠ received," and it is bounded by ambiguity and small in size. Attachment is dimensional, moderately stable (prototype dynamics), relationship-specific to a meaningful degree, and changes with age and relationship experience; partners can buffer insecurity.

### Cited Findings
**Perception of identical care (the key experiment)**
- Collins & Feeney (2004), *JPSP* 87(3):363–383. Read from the authors' PDF.
  - **Study 1** (Exp): 95 US undergraduate couples (93.5% dating; support recipients 49 women and 46 men, M age 19.4). Recipients prepared a stressful videotaped speech. Partners *copied experimenter-written notes in their own handwriting*, and everyone in a condition received the same two notes.
    - High-support notes: "Don't worry—just say how you feel and what you think and you'll do great"; "I liked your speech. That was a hard thing to do and you did a really good job."
    - Low-support notes, deliberately "somewhat ambiguous": "Try not to say anything too embarrassing—especially since so many people will be watching your tape"; "Your speech was a little hard to follow, but I guess you did the best you could under the circumstances."
  - **Results**, with relationship satisfaction controlled:
    - Positive appraisal showed a large condition effect (β = .66, sr² = .43) and **no attachment effects**.
    - Negative appraisal of the pre-speech note was higher with **avoidance only in the low-support condition** (β = .41, sr² = .06; high-support β = .06, ns).
    - Negative appraisal of the post-speech note was higher with **anxiety only in the low-support condition** (β = .40, sr² = .06; high-support β = .00).
    - **Retrospective re-rating of a pre-manipulation, spontaneous interaction:** secure participants were unaffected by the notes (β = .05, ns). Insecure participants who got low-support notes re-rated their partner's earlier behavior as less supportive (preoccupied β = .80, sr² = .10; fearful β = .35, sr² = .04; dismissing β = .36, ns).
  - **Study 2** (153 couples; partners wrote genuine notes): insecure recipients, especially fearful ones, saw their partners' messages as less supportive **even after controlling for independent observers' ratings of the messages** and relationship-specific expectations.
  - Inferred by the authors: working models bias appraisal "especially when the support message is ambiguous."

  [E for an ambiguity-bounded appraisal bias; Exp + observational; WEIRD students; small effects] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.87.3.363](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.3.363); [authors' PDF](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/collins/nancy/UCSB_Close_Relationships_Lab/Publications_files/Collins%20and%20Feeney,%202004.pdf)
- Collins & Feeney (2000), *JPSP* 78(6):1053–1073. 93 dating couples were videotaped while one partner disclosed a problem. More stress led to more direct support seeking, which led to more helpful caregiving, which led the seeker to feel cared for and improved mood. Avoidance predicted ineffective support seeking, and anxiety predicted poor caregiving. **Participants' perceptions of the interaction were biased by relationship quality and attachment.** [M–E; observational; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.78.6.1053](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.6.1053)
- Feeney (2004), *JPSP* 87(5):631–648, **secure base**. In observational and then experimental phases, **responsive, *nonintrusive*** support of a partner's goals raised the recipient's happiness, self-esteem and perceived chances of reaching goals. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.87.5.631](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.5.631)
- Feeney & Thrush (2010), *JPSP* 98(1):57–76. 167 married couples, with self-reports and coded videotaped exploration. **Availability, non-interference and encouragement** strongly predicted exploration, and attachment predicted giving and receiving these behaviors. Intrusive or interfering "support" is care that is expressed but received as a cost. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/a0016961](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016961)
- Gauvin, Maxwell, Impett & MacDonald (2025; online 2024), *PSPB* 51(12):2527–2541, "Love lost in translation." Three dyadic studies (N = 303 dyads) in which couples shared "a time of love." Higher avoidance meant **less accurate tracking of the partner's positive emotions, without systematic over- or under-perception** (Truth and Bias model). [M; WEIRD; empathic accuracy is covered in detail by another dossier] — [doi:10.1177/01461672241258391](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672241258391)

**Insecurity and relationship quality**
- Li & Chan (2012), *EJSP* 42(4):406–419. Meta-analysis of 73 studies (118 samples, N = 21,602). Both anxiety and avoidance hurt cognitive, emotional and behavioral relationship quality. **Avoidance** was more negatively associated with general satisfaction, connectedness and support, and **anxiety** more positively with conflict. Gender moderation was weak, and self-report vs observation moderated the behavioral effects. [E] — [doi:10.1002/ejsp.1842](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.1842)
- Candel & Turliuc (2019), *Personality and Individual Differences* 147:190–199. Meta-analysis of 132 eligible studies. Insecurity is negatively related to satisfaction, **actor effects are stronger than partner effects**, and associations vary with publication type, age, relationship status, length and region. Specific r values are UNVERIFIED: a secondary summary gave actor r ≈ −.32 for anxiety and −.44 for avoidance, but I could not check this against the article. [E for direction and actor > partner] — [doi:10.1016/j.paid.2019.04.037](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2019.04.037)
- Joel et al. (2020): attachment avoidance and anxiety were among the top five individual-difference predictors, but **individual differences added nothing beyond the actor's relationship-specific perceptions**. The authors read this as mediation through perceptions. — [PMC7431040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7431040/)

**Stability and change**
- Fraley (2002), *PSPR* 6(2):123–151. Meta-analysis plus dynamic modeling: attachment security is "moderately stable across the first 19 years," and stability is best explained by **prototype dynamics** (a stable underlying factor), not pure revisionism. [M–E] — [doi:10.1207/S15327957PSPR0602_03](https://doi.org/10.1207/S15327957PSPR0602_03)
- Pinquart, Feußner & Ahnert (2013), *Attachment & Human Development* 15(2):189–218. 127 papers, 21,072 attachments, intervals up to 29 years. Overall r = .39, with **no significant stability over intervals longer than 15 years**. Stability was higher for short intervals, representational measures and middle-class samples, and at-risk secure children were less likely to stay secure. [E] — [doi:10.1080/14616734.2013.746257](https://doi.org/10.1080/14616734.2013.746257)
- Fraley, Vicary, Brumbaugh & Roisman (2011), *JPSP* 101(5):974–992. Daily measurement over 30 days (N = 203) and weekly over a year (N = 388) best fit a **prototype model**, and Big Five traits did not account for attachment stability. [M–E] — [doi:10.1037/a0024150](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024150)
- Chopik, Edelstein & Grimm (2019), *JPSP* 116(4):598–611. N = 628, ages 13–72, Q-sort measure. Anxiety declined with age, especially in middle and older adulthood. Avoidance declined linearly. **Being in a relationship predicted lower anxiety and avoidance.** [M; Long; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/pspp0000167](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000167)
- La Guardia et al. (2000): security varies substantially within person across relationships (above). Fraley, Heffernan, Vicary & Brumbaugh (2011, ECR-RS): **relationship-specific attachment predicts intra- and interpersonal outcomes better than global attachment**, while global measures predict traits better (N > 21,000 online; Study 2 N = 388). [E] — [doi:10.1037/a0022898](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022898)
- Arriaga, Kumashiro, Simpson & Overall (2018; online 2017), *PSPR* 22(1):71–96, the **Attachment Security Enhancement Model (ASEM)**. Partner responses can protect against immediate insecurity, and some situations revise working models. Anxiety should fall most where personal confidence grows; avoidance should fall most with positive dependence. Adult attachment is "an emergent property of close relationships." [T + review] — [doi:10.1177/1088868317705257](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868317705257)
- Simpson & Overall (2014), *Current Directions* 23(1):54–59, the **dyadic regulation (partner buffering) model**. Some partners emotionally and behaviorally regulate anxious or avoidant individuals, which improves their feelings, behavior and relationship. [T + review of dyadic studies; M] — [doi:10.1177/0963721413510933](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721413510933)
- Fraley et al. (2015): attachment individual differences are **dimensional, not categorical** (taxometrics on general and relationship-specific measures). [E] — [doi:10.1037/pspp0000027](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000027)

### Inferences
- **The strongest support for H's "expressed ≠ perceived" link is here, with clear boundaries.**
  - Identical messages diverged in appraisal by attachment only when ambiguous. Unambiguous care was "received" similarly by everyone.
  - Effects were small (sr² ≈ .03–.10).
  - Insecure people also *rewrote the memory* of earlier care after a disappointing note, so the "received" record can change after the fact.

  H should predict translation failures concentrated in ambiguous or mixed expressions and in insecure recipients, not as a general rule. [Inference; E-level base]
- **Reverse causation is real.** Relationship experience changes the lens (Chopik et al.; ASEM; Pinquart's loss of stability over long intervals). Perceived care may therefore shape attachment as much as attachment shapes perceived care, which complicates any one-way "lens" model. [Inference]
- Joel et al.'s mediation reading (traits act through relationship-specific perceptions) fits attachment as a perception-shaping variable, but the prediction design cannot confirm the mediation causally. [Inference]
- Intrusive or "interfering" support (Feeney & Thrush) is a non-language form of translation failure: the amount and intent of care are high, but its *form* undermines the recipient's autonomy. This aligns with need fulfillment (autonomy) more than with LL categories. [Inference]

### Gaps
- The meta-analytic r values in Candel & Turliuc (2019) and the stability coefficients in Fraley (2002) were not verified.
- No experiment was found that crosses *message clarity* × attachment × *channel* (e.g., words vs acts). That design would directly test LL-style translation against attachment-style appraisal.

## 5. Trust, commitment and the investment model

### Takeaway
The investment model is among the most replicated models in relationship science. In meta-analyses, satisfaction (r = .65), investments (r = .53) and quality of alternatives (r = −.43) predict commitment and together explain about two-thirds of its variance. Commitment, love, inclusion of other in the self (IOS) and dependence are among the strongest predictors of nonmarital breakup. Trust is both a cause and an outcome: it grows when a partner *perceives* the other's pro-relationship acts (mutual cyclical growth). Behavioral commitment stabilizes marriages beyond satisfaction. These results partly **disconfirm** H's "not the amount of love" clause: love itself is a strong predictor of dissolution.

### Cited Findings
- Rempel, Holmes & Zanna (1985), *JPSP* 49(1):95–112. Trust has three dimensions: **predictability, dependability, faith**. Love and happiness were closely tied to faith and to attributing *intrinsic* motives to self and partner. People saw their own motives as less self-centered than their partner's, an actor–partner asymmetry in perceived intent. [M; established couples; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.49.1.95](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.49.1.95)
- Larzelere & Huston (1980), *Journal of Marriage and the Family* 42(3):595ff, the **Dyadic Trust Scale**: unidimensional and reliable. Trust was associated with love and depth of self-disclosure, and **partners reciprocated trust more than love or self-disclosure**. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.2307/351903](https://doi.org/10.2307/351903)
- Wieselquist, Rusbult, Foster & Agnew (1999), *JPSP* 77(5):942–966. Two longitudinal studies supported **mutual cyclical growth**:
  1. dependence leads to commitment;
  2. commitment leads to pro-relationship acts (accommodation, sacrifice);
  3. **the partner perceives those acts**;
  4. perceiving them raises the partner's trust;
  5. trust raises the partner's willingness to depend.

  Acts that depart from self-interest are "diagnostic." Self-reported attachment style added little beyond these interdependence features. [M–E; Long mediation; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.77.5.942](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.5.942)
- Murray, Holmes & Collins (2006), *Psychological Bulletin* 132(5):641–666, the **risk regulation** model. Confidence in a partner's regard and care lets people risk dependence, governed by cognitive, affective and behavioral "if–then" rules. [T + review] — [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.132.5.641](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.5.641)
- Simpson (2007), *Current Directions* 16(5):264–268, an integrative dyadic process model of trust. [T] — [doi:10.1111/j.1467-8721.2007.00517.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8721.2007.00517.x)
- Rusbult (1980), *JESP* 16(2):172–186, the original test of the investment model — [doi:10.1016/0022-1031(80)90007-4](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-1031%2880%2990007-4). Rusbult (1983), *JPSP* 45(1):101–117, the longitudinal test (bibliographic record verified) — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.45.1.101](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.45.1.101)
- Rusbult, Martz & Agnew (1998), *Personal Relationships* 5(4):357–387, the **Investment Model Scale (IMS)**. Three studies showed good internal consistency and four factors. IMS variables correlated moderately with dyadic adjustment, trust and IOS, were essentially unrelated to dispositions such as self-esteem, and prospectively predicted later adjustment and persistence vs breakup. [E] — [doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.1998.tb00177.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.1998.tb00177.x)
- Le & Agnew (2003), *Personal Relationships* 10(1):37–57. Meta-analysis of 52 studies (60 samples, N = 11,582). Satisfaction, alternatives and investments each correlated with commitment and together explained **nearly two-thirds** of its variance. Commitment predicted breakup. The model was stronger in relational than nonrelational domains, with minimal variation by ethnicity or duration. [E] — [doi:10.1111/1475-6811.00035](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6811.00035)
- Tran, Judge & Kashima (2019), *Personal Relationships* 26(1):158–180. Updated meta-analysis of 202 samples (N = 50,427; 1980–early 2016): **satisfaction r = .65, investments r = .53, alternatives r = −.43** with commitment, with several moderators. [E] — [doi:10.1111/pere.12268](https://doi.org/10.1111/pere.12268)
- Le, Dove, Agnew, Korn & Mutso (2010), *Personal Relationships* 17(3):377–390. Meta-analysis of 137 studies (N = 37,761, 33 years). **"Commitment, love, inclusion of other in the self, and dependence were among the strongest predictors of dissolution."** Satisfaction, alternatives and investments were modest predictors. Social network support was robust. Personality had limited predictive utility, with small effects for relational dispositions such as attachment. [E for rank order; d values not retrieved] — [doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x)
- Johnson, Caughlin & Huston (1999), *Journal of Marriage and the Family* 61(1):160–177. Commitment is **tripartite**: personal ("want to"), moral ("ought to") and structural ("have to") reasons to stay married. [M; details not retrieved] — [doi:10.2307/353891](https://doi.org/10.2307/353891)
- Stanley & Markman (1992), *Journal of Marriage and the Family* 54(3):595ff, the **Commitment Inventory**: personal dedication vs constraint commitment, developed with N = 141 and N = 279. [M; WEIRD] — [doi:10.2307/353245](https://doi.org/10.2307/353245)
- Schoebi, Karney & Bradbury (2012; online 2011), *JPSP* 102(4):729–742. 172 newlywed couples over the first 4 years, with divorce tracked to 11 years. Commitment as *behavioral inclination to maintain* the relationship, but not commitment as *desire for it to persist*, predicted observed interaction behavior, steps toward dissolution and **11-year divorce, independent of satisfaction**. Divorce risk followed the **weaker partner's** inclination ("weak link"). [M–E; Long; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/a0026290](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026290)
- Joel et al. (2020): **perceived partner commitment** was the top relationship-specific predictor of a person's relationship quality across 43 datasets. — [PMC7431040](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7431040/)

### Inferences
- Trust and perceived partner commitment are "received" constructs. Wieselquist et al. show trust is built from the partner's perception of costly pro-relationship acts, which supports H's claim that what is perceived matters. But the cycle also implies that perceptions *track real behavior over time*: trust rose because acts occurred. That argues against a large standing gap between expressed and received care in committed couples. [Inference]
- **Disconfirming H's "rather than the amount" clause:** love (and IOS) is among the strongest predictors of nonmarital dissolution (Le et al. 2010), and abatement of love predicts divorce (Huston et al. 2001, Section 6). Amount matters substantially. H can survive only as "translation *also* matters," not "rather than." [Inference; E-level base]
- The "weak link" (Schoebi et al.) and unequal dependence (Le & Agnew 2001) show that *asymmetry* between partners predicts outcomes. That is a structural "compatibility" variable distinct from translation. [Inference]

### Gaps
- Effect sizes (d) in Le et al. (2010) and the original Le & Agnew (2003) r values were not retrieved; the Tran et al. (2019) values are used instead.
- No Korean validations of the IMS, Trust Scale, Dyadic Trust Scale or Commitment Inventory were found.

## 6. Compatibility, similarity, and what actually predicts relationship quality (including formal "gap" models)

### Takeaway
A person's own relationship-specific perceptions predict their relationship quality best: perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction, conflict and perceived partner responsiveness explain up to 45% of baseline variance. **The partner's own reports and all individual differences add nothing beyond these actor perceptions.** **Change in quality is largely unpredictable** (≤5% of variance). Trait similarity explains less than 0.5% of variance once actor and partner effects are controlled. Actual similarity does not predict attraction in existing relationships, though perceived similarity does. Idiosyncratic preference matching is small, and dyad-specific compatibility could not be predicted from more than 100 pre-meeting traits and preferences. Ideal-standards "gap" models are supported correlationally, but ideals also shift to fit the partner (reverse causation).

### Cited Findings
**Machine-learning synthesis**
- Joel et al. ### Inferences
- **Two readings of the Joel et al. result.** (1) Supporting H: what a person *perceives* (perceived partner commitment, responsiveness, appreciation) predicts quality, and the partner's own reports add nothing, so "received" diverges from what the partner reports. (2) Undercutting H: actor perceptions and actor satisfaction share method variance and global sentiment. "Perceived partner commitment" may partly project one's own commitment (reverse causation), so the result does not show a translation gap. It shows only that a person's own appraisals carry the predictive information. [Inference; both readings are plausible]
- **Compatibility ≠ trait match.** Trait similarity explains almost nothing (Dyrenforth et al.; Weidmann et al.). Perceived similarity matters more than actual similarity (Montoya et al.). Dyad-specific compatibility is unpredictable before meeting (Joel et al. 2017). Compatibility is therefore better treated as an *emergent, relationship-specific* property (shared experiences, goal correspondence, perceived understanding) than as a match of fixed traits. Counter-evidence: similarity in emotions, values or attachment does correlate with quality in some samples (Luo & Klohnen; Gaunt; Gonzaga et al.). [Inference]
- **Change is the hard target.** Self-reports barely predict change (Joel et al.), and communication–satisfaction lags are weak and bidirectional (Lavner et al.; Johnson et al.). A translation-based theory claiming to explain *deterioration* faces a high bar, and so far no study shows that measured expression–reception mismatches predict *later decline*. [Inference]
- Ideal-standards results provide a formal "gap" model (perception vs ideal), but ideals partly bend toward the partner (Fletcher et al. 2000). Any "gap" construct in H must be modeled as dynamic and two-way. [Inference]

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly measures partner A's intended love, A's expressed behaviors, B's perception and B's need fulfillment in the same couples and predicts *change* in quality. That chain is the core empirical test of H.
- Joel et al.'s exact change R² values appear only in the figures and supplement (not retrieved).

## 7. Stress and context

### Takeaway
External stress lowers relationship quality, makes satisfaction more reactive to daily relationship events, and degrades both **noticing** a partner's needs and **acting** on them. In a daily diary study, husbands' chronic stress predicted less accurate perception of their wives' support needs, and same-day stress predicted not providing support even when the need was noticed. Perceived responsiveness buffers stress across 57 countries. Context also reverses the sign of some processes (expectations × skills) and differs by income. Not all large stressors change average satisfaction: early COVID showed no mean change.

### Cited Findings
- Karney & Bradbury (1995): an integrative model linking enduring vulnerabilities, stressful events and adaptive processes. [E as review] — [doi:10.1037/0033-2909.118.1.3](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.118.1.3)
- Neff & Karney (2009), *JPSP* 97(3):435–450. Study 1: diaries from 146 newlywed couples; under more stress, daily global satisfaction **co-varied more strongly with specific relationship experiences**. Study 2: 82 couples, 7-day diaries at 3 waves over 4 years; **within-person, reactivity rose when stress was above the person's norm**, controlling individual differences. [M–E; Diary + Long; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1037/a0015663](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015663)
- Neff, Nguyen & Williamson (2021; online 2020), *PSPB* 47(11):1565–1579. Daily diary, 121 married couples. **Husbands under greater chronic stress were less accurate about their partner's support needs across diary days, and even when husbands noticed a need, they were less likely to provide support on days they were coping with their own stress.** [M; Diary; WEIRD] — [doi:10.1177/0146167220974490](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220974490)
- Randall & Bodenmann (2009), *Clinical Psychology Review* 29(2):105–115, a review of stress and marital satisfaction (content not retrieved) — [doi:10.1016/j.cpr.2008.10.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2008.10.004)
- Balzarini et al. (2023): COVID stressors went with poorer relationship quality, buffered by PPR, in N = 3,593 from 57 countries and N = 1,125 over 3 months. [M] — [doi:10.1177/19485506221094437](https://doi.org/10.1177/19485506221094437)
- Williamson (2020), *Psychological Science* 31(12):1479–1487. N = 654, measured before the pandemic (Dec 2019) and twice early in it (Mar and Apr 2020). **Satisfaction and causal attributions did not change on average**, and responsibility attributions decreased. Change was not moderated by demographics or negative pandemic repercussions. Small moderation: couples with better coping and less conflict improved, those with worse functioning declined. [M; Long; WEIRD; a counterpoint to simple "stress erodes love" claims] — [doi:10.1177/0956797620972688](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797620972688)
- Karney & Bradbury (2020): dyadic processes adaptive for affluent couples may operate differently in lower-income couples. [E as review] — [doi:10.1111/jomf.12635](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12635)
- McNulty & Karney (2004): expectations help or hurt depending on skills. McNulty (2016), "Highlighting the contextual nature of interpersonal relationships," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* pp. 247–315 (Crossref lists no volume), argues that processes are context-dependent (content not retrieved). — [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.86.5.729](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.86.5.729); [doi:10.1016/bs.aesp.2016.02.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.aesp.2016.02.003)
- Tay & Diener (2011): societal need fulfillment predicts well-being beyond individual fulfillment. Context matters for need satisfaction itself. — [doi:10.1037/a0023779](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023779)

### Inferences
- **Stress is a rival explanation to "translation."** Many apparent translation failures may be *capacity* failures. The same loving partner, under stress, does not notice the need or does not act on it (Neff et al. 2021). H should add a *capacity or resources* stage between "intended" and "expressed," and another between "expressed" and "perceived" (stress also amplifies reactivity to daily events; Neff & Karney 2009). [Inference; M-level base]
- Responsiveness buffering (Balzarini et al.) suggests the received side of care can *offset* external stress. Without experiments, though, it is unclear whether PPR is a cause or a marker of better-functioning couples. [Inference]

### Gaps
- Stress and translation studies outside WEIRD samples, other than Balzarini et al., were not found within this scope.
- Randall & Bodenmann (2009) and McNulty (2016) were cited only bibliographically.

## 8. Stress tests for H

### Takeaway
- **Case A (deep understanding, incompatible goals):** goal congruence and goal conflict predict relationship quality and well-being independently of how well partners understand each other. Interdependence theory treats conflict of interests as a structural feature that information cannot remove, and costly sacrifice has mixed consequences. Translation is not the problem here, and H should keep compatibility separate from understanding, which the data support.
- **Case G (different but successful expression styles):** couples build private shared codes (idioms, shared reality), and spouses express love through different behaviors. Breadth of loving behavior predicts quality better than matching any one "primary" channel. Acceptance-based couple therapy performs about as well as change-based therapy long term. Difference in expression is compatible with health, but similarity in emotions and values is sometimes associated with better quality.
- **Case H (stability without strong romantic love):** in small, selected samples, arranged marriages report satisfaction comparable to love marriages, and love can grow after marriage (retrospective). Larger or probability samples (Chengdu, Nepal) favor self-chosen marriages. Stable but unhappy marriages exist, are predicted by moral and structural commitment, and carry well-being costs. Love's importance for marriage decisions varies by culture. In Western samples, though, love strongly predicts stability.

**Case A: perfect understanding but incompatible goals**

- Counter-evidence from Western samples: **love is among the strongest predictors of nonmarital breakup** (Le et al. 2010), and **abatement of love predicts divorce** (Huston et al. 2001). — [doi:10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x); [doi:10.1037/0022-3514.80.2.237](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.2.237)

### Inferences
- **Case A supports H's separation of compatibility from understanding.** Outcome structure (goal congruence or conflict) predicts well-being independently of empathy or understanding, and interdependence theory says information cannot remove a conflict of interests. It also shows a class of relationship problems where "translation" is *not* the cause. That partly **disconfirms** "many problems come from translation," unless "many" is quantified. [Inference]
- **Case G:** the best data say healthy couples need *enough and varied* care plus a co-constructed code (idioms, shared reality), not identical styles. This fits the "balanced diet" account better than the "matching languages" account. Similarity in emotions and values is still often associated with quality, so "different styles are fine" holds for *expression channels* more than for *values or emotional responding*. [Inference; M]
- **Case H:**
  - Stability without strong romantic love is clearly possible (moral and structural commitment; arranged marriages; stable unhappy marriages).
  - Whether *quality* is comparable is unresolved. Small selected samples say yes; larger probability samples in China and Nepal favor self-choice, and spouse choice is confounded with education and modernity, which the cross-sectional designs cannot remove.
  - In US data, stability without quality carries well-being costs (Hawkins & Booth).
  - Implication for H: "love" and "stability" can come apart, but that is a love-vs-commitment distinction, not a translation phenomenon.

  [Inference; SPEC regarding causality]
- The retrospective "love grows" result (Epstein et al.) is weak evidence: recall bias, tiny N, selection of intact marriages. Hatfield et al. (2008) find *both* love types decline over time in Western marriages. [Inference]

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study found that measures love, need fulfillment and stability prospectively from the start of arranged marriages in a representative sample.
- Levine et al.'s country percentages were not verified.
- Gere & Schimmack's design (cross-sectional?) and country were not verified.
- No study was found in which couples with *verified* high mutual understanding (e.g., high empathic accuracy) differ in goal compatibility, i.e., a direct operationalization of Case A. No peer-reviewed evidence was found on disagreement over having children predicting dissolution (search returned only fertility-intention studies).

## 9. Cross-cutting comparison of theories: evidence-base scorecard and what it implies for falsifying H

### Takeaway
On evidence quality, rank interdependence/investment and attachment highest. Need fulfillment and responsiveness are close behind: their strengths are breadth (multi-country) and prospective or physiological outcomes, and their weakness is reliance on self-report. Ideal-standards and similarity models come next, with modest effects. Love languages rank last. LL "matching" is best treated as a narrow, weakly supported special case of responsiveness or need fulfillment. The most defensible version of H is a perception-centered model in which *received or perceived* care, shaped by attachment, ambiguity, stress and need balance, mediates between intended care and outcomes. That version is only partly tested, and the strong "rather than amount" clause is contradicted by evidence that love, commitment and dependence predict outcomes directly.

- Supporting H's chain (intended → expressed → perceived → received):
  - The same standardized message is appraised differently by insecure recipients when it is ambiguous ([Collins & Feeney 2004](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.87.3.363)).
  - Stress breaks noticing and enacting ([Neff et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220974490)).
  - Biased models of partner preferences shape expressions ([Coy & Rodriguez 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.12655)).
  - Perceived partner behavior predicts satisfaction more than one's own enacted love ([Halamová 2026](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1773641); [Ogolsky & Bowers 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407512463338)).
  - Trust is built from *perceived* acts ([Wieselquist 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.77.5.942)).
  - Intrusive support undermines exploration ([Feeney & Thrush 2010](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0016961)).
  - Compassionate love given vs received differ ([Fehr et al. 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407514533768)).
- Disconfirming or limiting H:
  - The amount of love, commitment and dependence predicts dissolution directly ([Le 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x); [Huston 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.2.237); [Acevedo & Aron 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0014226)).
  - Channel-specific matching adds little ([Flicker 2024](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.12747); [Flicker 2025](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.70078); analog in [Eastwick 2025](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspp0000524)).
  - Change in quality is barely predictable ([Joel 2020](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117)).
  - Communication and satisfaction effects are weak and bidirectional ([Lavner 2016](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12301); [Johnson 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672211016920)).
  - Changing negative communication does not reliably raise satisfaction ([Karney & Bradbury 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12635)).
  - Goal incompatibility harms well-being regardless of understanding ([Gere 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jrp.2011.06.010); [Gere & Schimmack 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-011-9314-2)).

### Inferences
- **A falsifiable reformulation of H** [SPEC]: "Holding constant the *level* of partner-intended and partner-enacted care (partner-reported and observed), variance in the recipient's *perceived* care (from ambiguity, attachment, stress and need imbalance) predicts *change* in the recipient's quality beyond the level of care." This is falsified if:
  1. perceived care adds nothing beyond enacted level in longitudinal dyadic models;
  2. the effect disappears with controls for prior satisfaction (reverse causation);
  3. channel-mismatch terms add less than ~1% in response-surface models.
- The current literature gives partial support for parts of the chain, in experiments limited to ambiguous messages and in diaries for stress. **It gives no support for LL-style channel mismatch as a major cause, and direct evidence that the amount of love matters.**

### Gaps
- No dyadic longitudinal study measures all of H's stages in one design. This is the decisive missing study.
- Comparative evidence on non-WEIRD couples is thin for every framework except need satisfaction (Tay & Diener; Chen et al.), PPR buffering (Balzarini et al.) and preference matching (Eastwick et al.).
