# Misperception asymmetry: when a care-giver sincerely reports "I gave 90/100" and the receiver sincerely reports "I received 40/100"

Reading legend (applies to every bullet). Evidence grades: **[E]** Established; **[M]** Moderate or mixed; **[P]** Preliminary; **[T]** Theoretical proposition; **[SPEC]** my own speculative extrapolation, not a finding. Sample flags: **WEIRD** (US, Canada, Western Europe, Australia, NZ), **non-WEIRD**, or "country not stated in abstract". Where a bullet gives a country such as "US" without saying the abstract states it, the country is inferred from the authors' affiliations and lab and should be checked against the Method section. "Causal ID" = how well the design identifies causation. Unless marked UNVERIFIED, every DOI below was resolved on 2026-11-04 via the OpenAlex or Crossref APIs and its title, authors and year matched. Where a number comes only from press coverage or a secondary source, the bullet says so. All effect sizes are as reported by the original authors. These notes test the hypothesis "love intended ≠ expressed ≠ perceived ≠ received ≠ compatibility; many problems come from translation rather than amount of love" and give disconfirming evidence the same weight as supporting evidence.

## Overall: Is "A believes they provided 90, B experiences 40" a predictable, measurable phenomenon, and does the evidence favour the "translation" hypothesis?

### Takeaway
Sincere giver–receiver discrepancies are a real, measurable family of effects, but three caveats apply. First, typical mean-level gaps are small to moderate, e.g. parent vs adolescent g ≈ .24, couples' positive mean bias r ≈ .09, and agreement on whether a supportive act happened kappa ≈ .39. They are nowhere near a 50-point gap. Second, the direction is not fixed. Givers overvalue costly or unrequested gifts and their own share of joint work, but undervalue warm expressive acts and their own sacrifice costs. Third, much of the strongest causal evidence runs from global sentiment to perception, not the other way. This includes sentiment override, decoding deficits that appear only with one's own spouse, and reciprocal attribution–satisfaction effects. That is a serious rival to the claim that translation failures cause relationship problems. Overall grade for the hypothesis as a causal claim: **[M] moderate or mixed at best**.

### Cited Findings
**Evidence that the gap exists and is systematic**
- [E] A meta-analysis of romantic perception against external benchmarks found substantial tracking accuracy (r = .47, 98 studies) alongside a small positive mean-level bias (r = .09, 48 studies). Signed mean-level bias was *negative* for judgments about the interaction, such as the partner's love and communication. In other words, on average perceivers slightly underestimate a partner's love relative to the benchmark. The two kinds of accuracy were uncorrelated across 38 studies. Country mix not given in the abstract (presumably mostly WEIRD). Causal ID: none (meta-analysis of correlational designs). — [Fletcher & Kerr 2010, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019792)
- [E] Parent vs adolescent reports of parenting: 313 studies, agreement r = .276 [.262, .290]. Parents perceived parenting more positively, g = .242 [.188, .296]. — [Hou et al. 2020, Am Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000463)
- [M] Whether a supportive act occurred: students and their support persons independently reported the supporter's behaviours after an exam. Agreement was only moderate (kappa = .39) and was higher in more intimate dyads and for helpful than harmful behaviours. US. Causal ID: none. — [Coriell & Cohen 1995, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.69.2.289)
- [M] In a couples' daily diary, many support transactions reported by providers were not reported by recipients ("invisible support"). Country not stated in abstract (US lab). — [Bolger, Zuckerman & Kessler 2000, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953)
- [M] In two daily-experience studies of couples (total N = 426), partners' sacrifices were missed about as often as they were detected. About half the time people "saw" a sacrifice the partner did not declare. Missed sacrifices elicited no gratitude and lowered the *sacrificer's* satisfaction. Country not stated in abstract. Causal ID: within-person daily associations only. — [Visserman et al. 2019, SPPS](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599)
- [M] 4,055 parent–adult-child dyads (Netherlands; WEIRD): both generations over-report help given and under-report help received. This is a direct analogue of "I gave more than you say you got." — [Mandemakers & Dykstra 2008, JMF](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2008.00496.x)
- [E] Egocentric over-claiming: group members' self-allocated responsibility routinely sums to more than 100%, starting with married couples rating household activities. Over-claiming grows with group size. — [Ross & Sicoly 1979, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.3.322), as described in [Caruso, Epley & Bazerman 2006](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Caruso-et_al_Undoing_Egocentrism_JPSP2006.pdf); [Schroeder, Caruso & Epley 2016, JEP:Applied](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000080)
- [M] In a small study (29 couples), positive feelings were *inaccurately* perceived while negative ones were accurately decoded. Men read a partner's failure to express love as hostility. This is the closest direct evidence that love specifically is "lost in translation." — [Gaelick, Bodenhausen & Wyer 1985, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.49.5.1246)

**Evidence that the direction depends on the type of care**
- [M] Givers expected pricier gifts to earn more appreciation, but recipients' appreciation was unrelated to price. — [Flynn & Adams 2009, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2008.11.003)
- [M] Givers wrongly assumed unrequested gifts would be appreciated as much as requested ones. — [Gino & Flynn 2011, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2011.03.015)
- [M] For warm, expressive acts the giver's error reverses. Expressers *underestimated* the positive impact of gratitude letters, compliments, random kindness and support messages. — [Kumar & Epley 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618772506); [Zhao & Epley 2021](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000277); [Boothby & Bohns 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220949003); [Kumar & Epley 2023](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001271); [Dungan et al. 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976221082942)
- [M] Sacrificers *underestimated* their own sacrifice costs and overestimated the benefits. Study 1: N = 125 couples; Study 2: preregistered, N = 775. — [Visserman et al. 2020, SPPS](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550619896671)

**Evidence against "translation as the cause"**
- [E] Empathic accuracy correlates only weakly with satisfaction (r = .134; 21 studies, N = 2,739). — [Sened et al. 2017, J Fam Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000320)
- [M] Newlyweds' subjective sense of understanding and being understood predicted relationship well-being; actual knowledge of the partner did not. — [Pollmann & Finkenauer 2009, PSPB](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209342754)
- [M] Across 43 longitudinal couple datasets, partner-reported variables added no predictive variance beyond the actor's own relationship-specific perceptions. Change in relationship quality was largely unpredictable. — [Joel et al. 2020, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117)
- [M] A single global-sentiment factor ("Q") captured most of the common variance across 34 relationship measures (N = 3,439, US). Self-reported "facets," translation-related ones included, may largely reflect global sentiment. — [Kim et al. 2026, PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0342451)
- [M] Low-adjustment spouses decoded their own spouse worse than other spouses did, but decoded strangers no worse. The author suggested communication problems may be a consequence rather than a cause of unhappiness. — [Noller 1981, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.2.272)
- [M] Attributions and satisfaction influence each other in both directions over time. — [Fincham, Harold & Gano-Phillips 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.14.2.267); [Karney & Bradbury 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.295)
- [M] Inaccuracy in a positive direction can help:
  - idealising a partner predicts satisfaction and protects against decline ([Murray, Holmes & Griffin 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.70.1.79); [Murray et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611403155));
  - support the recipient never notices can reduce distress ([Bolger et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953); [Bolger & Amarel 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.458)).
- [E] The classic actor–observer asymmetry is essentially absent in a meta-analysis (d = −0.016 to 0.095), so it cannot carry a translation hypothesis. — [Malle 2006, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.895)

### Inferences
- The four "≠" links each have some empirical support, but of different quality:
  - **Intended → expressed** (encoding): Noller's standard-content work attributes some errors to the sender. Senders also overestimate how readable they are (illusion of transparency; closeness-communication bias). Grade [M].
  - **Expressed → perceived** (detection and decoding): Gaelick 1985; Gable 2003; Visserman 2019; Coriell & Cohen 1995. Grade [M].
  - **Perceived → received benefit**: invisible support shows benefit without perception, a dissociation in the direction the hypothesis does not predict. Grade [M].
  - **Perceived → relationship outcome**: perceptions dominate prediction (Joel 2020). This supports "perceived love matters" but not "the gap matters." Grade [M].
- [SPEC] The illustrative numbers mix two constructs. "I provided 90/100 of the care that was needed" is an adequacy-relative-to-need judgment. Egocentric over-claiming studies measure share of joint work, and recipient forecasts measure momentary affect. A study of the 90-vs-40 pattern must measure both partners on the same construct, time frame and scale.
- [SPEC] Magnitude check: a 50-point gap on a 0–100 scale with SD ≈ 20 is roughly d ≈ 2.5. That is several times larger than the mean-level gaps above (g ≈ .2–.5). If real dyads show gaps this large, the first explanations to rule out are distress-driven sentiment override, different scale anchors, different constructs, and retrospective global reporting. Only then should a "translation" failure be inferred.
- Falsification tests the literature already permits:
  - Do act-level intent–impact gaps predict later satisfaction once prior satisfaction, global sentiment (Q) and scale use are controlled?
  - Do decoding errors appear with the partner but not with strangers (Noller 1981)? If so, they are probably downstream of distress.
  - Does the giver's account add anything beyond the receiver's perception (Joel 2020 suggests little)?

### Gaps
- No study located measures "care intended" (giver) and "care experienced" (receiver) for the *same acts*, on a common 0–100 metric corrected for scale use, with momentary and retrospective layers, in couples or parent–child dyads. No Korean study of this kind was found.
- The most "love-specific" decoding finding (Gaelick et al. 1985) rests on N = 29 couples. No large preregistered replication was found.
- No non-US replication of expresser underestimation (gratitude, compliments, support) was found in two targeted searches.

## Empathic accuracy: how strongly does it relate to satisfaction, when does accuracy hurt, and is feeling understood more important than accurate knowledge?

### Takeaway
Empathic accuracy (EA) relates only weakly to relationship satisfaction. It is r ≈ .13 overall and r ≈ .17 for reading a partner's negative emotions; for positive emotions it is non-significant. EA can hurt when what is accurately perceived is threatening. Several studies suggest subjective understanding, projection and assumed similarity predict relationship outcomes better than objective accuracy does. This weakens any strong claim that accurate "translation" is the main driver of relationship quality. Grade **[E]** for the small association; **[M]** for the boundary conditions.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Sened et al. (2017) meta-analysis.** 21 studies, total N = 2,739, all using the dyadic interaction paradigm. EA–satisfaction r = .134 (p < .05). EA for negative emotions r = .171; for positive emotions r = .068 (n.s.). The link was stronger at moderate relationship length. It was not moderated by gender or procedure, and did not differ between perceiver's and target's satisfaction (actor vs partner effects). Countries not stated in abstract. Causal ID: none. — [Sened et al. 2017, J Fam Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000320)
- [M] **Simpson, Ickes & Blackstone (1995).** Dating couples rated and discussed photos of attractive or less attractive opposite-sex people. Partners who were close, insecure, and viewing highly attractive people showed the *least* EA. The effects were additive and mediated by perceived threat. The authors interpret this as motivated inaccuracy ("the head protects the heart"). N not in abstract (not verified here); US. Causal ID: partly experimental (attractiveness manipulated). — [Simpson et al. 1995, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.69.4.629)
- [M] **Simpson, Oriña & Ickes (2003).** Married couples, problem-resolution discussion followed by video review. When the partner's thoughts were relationship-threatening, greater perceiver EA predicted pre-to-post *declines* in the perceiver's closeness. When thoughts were non-threatening, EA predicted gains. The effect was partly mediated by observer-rated avoidance of the issue. N not in abstract; US. Causal ID: correlational, short-term pre–post. — [Simpson et al. 2003, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.85.5.881)
- [M] **Pollmann & Finkenauer (2009).** Prospective study of 199 newlywed couples. Subjective understanding was independent of actual knowledge. Self-reported and perceived understanding predicted relationship well-being; knowledge did not. Dutch authors; country not stated in abstract (WEIRD). Causal ID: prospective correlational. — [Pollmann & Finkenauer 2009, PSPB](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209342754)
- [M] **Reis, Lemay & Finkenauer (2017) review.** Feeling understood is only modestly related to actually being understood, and feeling understood matters for well-being. — [Reis et al. 2017, SPPC](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12308)
- [M] **Neff & Karney (2005), "To know you is to love you."** Two longitudinal newlywed studies. Most spouses enhanced their partner globally but varied in accuracy about specific qualities. For wives only, specific accuracy was associated with:
  - more supportive behaviour,
  - feelings of control,
  - lower divorce.

  Husbands showed no such associations. The authors concluded that love grounded in specific accuracy is stronger. Sample sizes not verified here (search found none); US. Causal ID: longitudinal correlational. — [Neff & Karney 2005, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.88.3.480)
- [M] **Thomas, Fletcher & Lange (1997).** 74 married couples, on-line EA during problem-solving. EA was higher in more educated and shorter-married couples. *Assumed similarity*, not EA, was related to satisfaction and the positivity of verbal behaviour. Country not stated in abstract (NZ lab). — [Thomas et al. 1997, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.72.4.839)
- [M] **Luo & Snider (2009).** 288 newlywed couples. Accuracy, positivity bias and similarity bias each independently predicted the perceiver's satisfaction. Accuracy and similarity bias, but not positivity bias, also predicted the target's satisfaction. US. — [Luo & Snider 2009, Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02449.x)

#### Instruments
- **Dyadic interaction paradigm / unstructured-interaction EA.** Video review in which each person lists their thoughts and feelings and infers the partner's; coders rate content accuracy. Coder reliability α = .94 in the original stranger-dyad study (38 dyads). Self-reported empathic skill did *not* predict EA. Korean validation: none located. — [Ickes, Stinson, Bissonnette & Garcia 1990, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.59.4.730)
- **On-line EA in marital interaction** (scores for EA, assumed similarity and shared cognitive focus). — [Thomas et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.72.4.839)
- **Subjective understanding and knowledge measures** (self-reported and perceived understanding vs domain knowledge tests). — [Pollmann & Finkenauer 2009](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167209342754)

### Inferences
- An EA–satisfaction r of .13 means EA explains under 2% of the variance in satisfaction. If misreading a partner's inner states were a *major* source of relationship problems, a larger association would be expected. Grade: [M] inference.
- The negative-emotion asymmetry (.17 vs .07) suggests that *missing distress* matters more than *missing affection*. That fits Gaelick 1985, where hostility was decoded accurately and love was not, but those findings sit in tension. [SPEC] Perhaps missed affection does little damage because receivers fill the gap with global sentiment (see sentiment override).
- Simpson's work implies that sometimes *not* translating accurately is protective. A model of the form "more accurate translation = better" is too simple.

### Gaps
- Sened 2017 covered the dyadic interaction paradigm only. No meta-analysis of *care-specific* EA (for example, accuracy about how cared-for the partner feels) was found.
- Neff & Karney's sample sizes and effect sizes could not be verified from accessible text.
- No Korean or East Asian EA studies in couples were located (other researchers cover cultural work).

## Truth-and-bias, positive illusions, tracking accuracy, projection, and Cronbach's critique: how accurate and how biased are partners at the same time?

### Takeaway
Partners are simultaneously quite accurate at tracking *relative* differences (r ≈ .47) and modestly biased at the *mean level* (r ≈ .09 positive overall, negative for love and communication judgments). The two are statistically independent. Assumed similarity (projection) is often a stronger force than accuracy. That means B's sense of how much A cares is partly B projecting B's own caring. Cronbach's 1955 critique shows that naive "agreement" scores confound these components. Grade **[E]** for the coexistence of accuracy and bias; **[M]** for which components predict outcomes.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Fletcher & Kerr (2010) meta-analysis.** Distinguished tracking accuracy (r = .47; 98 studies) from mean-level bias (r = .09; 48 studies). Signed bias was negative for interaction judgments (e.g., love, communication). The two indices were uncorrelated across 38 studies. Gender, relationship length and relationship evaluations moderated bias but not tracking. Heterosexual romantic samples; country mix not given in the abstract, presumably mostly WEIRD. — [Fletcher & Kerr 2010, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0019792)
- [M] **Overall, Fletcher & Kenny (2012).** 57 couples. Every 30 seconds during a video review of a "what I'd change about you" discussion, perceivers judged the partner's regard while partners reported their actual regard.
  - Perceivers *underestimated* the partner's regard on average, yet tracked its changes well.
  - The more negatively biased perceivers tracked more accurately.
  - Insecure women showed more negative bias and better tracking.

  Country not stated in abstract (NZ lab). Causal ID: none. — [Overall et al. 2012, PSPB](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211432764)
- [E] **Kenny & Acitelli (2001).** 238 dating and married couples. Both accuracy and assumed similarity were present. Bias effects were considerably stronger, especially for relationship-linked measures. Little gender difference. — [Kenny & Acitelli 2001, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.3.439)
- [T/E] **West & Kenny (2011), truth and bias (T&B) model.** Judgments are pulled by a truth force and bias forces. Bias comes in two kinds: variable-measured biases such as assumed similarity, and *directional bias* (a pull toward one end of the scale). Moderators change the forces rather than the judgment directly. This is the formal frame for separating "B tracks what A does" from "B's ratings are shifted down or up." — [West & Kenny 2011, Psychol Rev](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022936)
- [E, methodological] **Cronbach (1955).** Showed that "understanding of others" scores, computed as discrepancies between predicted and actual ratings, mix several components: elevation, differential elevation, stereotype accuracy and differential accuracy. They are also contaminated by assumed similarity and response sets. Abstract not available in OpenAlex; component names given from the well-known paper. — [Cronbach 1955, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0044919)
- [M] **Biesanz's Social Accuracy Model (SAM).** Splits accuracy into normative accuracy (knowing the average profile) and distinctive accuracy (knowing what is unique to this person), for both perceivers and targets. This is the modern implementation of Cronbach's components. — [Biesanz 2010, Multivariate Behav Res](https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2010.519262)
- [M] **Lemay, Clark & Feeney (2007), projection of responsiveness.** Two dyadic marriage studies, a daily diary and an experiment. One's *own* responsiveness predicted perceptions of the spouse's responsiveness more strongly than the spouse's self-reported responsiveness did. Projected perceptions predicted satisfaction. Making it feel hard to recall one's own support provision reduced perceived partner responsiveness (experimental). US. Causal ID: experimental for the projection step. — [Lemay et al. 2007, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.5.834)
- [M] **Kenny & DePaulo (1993).** 8 social relations model studies, 569 participants. People infer how others see them mainly from their own self-perceptions, not from feedback. They know how others see them in general better than how a *specific* person sees them. — [Kenny & DePaulo 1993, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.114.1.145)
- [E] **Montoya, Horton & Kirchner (2008) meta-analysis.** 313 studies, 460 effects. Actual similarity r = .47 and perceived similarity r = .39 with attraction overall. Actual similarity was *not* significant in existing relationships; perceived similarity predicted attraction across all designs. — [Montoya et al. 2008, JSPR](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407508096700)
- [M] **Positive illusions.**
  - Impressions of partners mirrored perceivers' self-images and ideals more than partners' self-reports, and idealisation predicted satisfaction ([Murray, Holmes & Griffin 1996](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.70.1.79)).
  - Newlyweds who idealised their partner most showed no satisfaction decline over 3 years ([Murray et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611403155)).
  - Review: bias and accuracy are independent and can coexist, and they serve different goals ([Gagné & Lydon 2004, PSPR](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0804_1)).

#### Instruments
- **Kenny–Acitelli dyadic accuracy/bias paradigm.** Each partner rates self and predicts the partner on the same items; analysed with T&B/APIM-style models. — [Kenny & Acitelli 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.3.439); [West & Kenny 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022936)
- **Continuous tracking paradigm** (30-second segment ratings during video review). — [Overall et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167211432764)
- **SAM** for separating normative from distinctive accuracy. — [Biesanz 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2010.519262)
- Korean validations: none located for these paradigms (gap).

### Inferences
- In T&B terms, "A says 90, B says 40" is a large **directional bias** (mean-level gap) between two reports. It is fully compatible with high **tracking accuracy**: B may correctly register which days A gave more, while placing the whole series lower. Measuring correlations alone would miss the gap; measuring mean differences alone would miss the tracking. Grade: [M] inference.
- Projection evidence (Lemay 2007; Kenny & Acitelli 2001) implies B's "40" may partly reflect B's own low giving or low self-view, not A's transmission. [SPEC] A low-giving or depressed receiver could under-perceive care regardless of how well A "translates."
- Fletcher & Kerr's negative signed bias for "love, communication" judgments is the meta-analytic result most supportive of "love perceived < love given." The effect is small and the benchmark is partner self-report, which is itself biased.

### Gaps
- No T&B analysis specifically of *care provision vs care receipt* was located.
- The magnitude of negative mean-level bias for love judgments specifically, separated from Fletcher & Kerr's pooled estimate, was not extractable from the abstract.

## Attributions in relationships: do interpretations cause dissatisfaction, or vice versa?

### Takeaway
Distressed spouses explain partner behaviour in ways that cast it negatively. Longitudinal data show that attributions predict later satisfaction beyond initial satisfaction. However, the effect is reciprocal, and attributions are not a stable trait. This is the best-supported route by which the *meaning* given to the same act could drive outcomes, but it cannot be cleanly separated from sentiment driving meaning. Grade **[E]** for the association; **[M]** for causal direction (bidirectional).

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Bradbury & Fincham (1990) review.** Dissatisfied spouses make attributions that cast the partner's behaviour in a negative light. Experimental, clinical-outcome and longitudinal data suggest attributions may influence satisfaction. The authors explicitly examined rival hypotheses. — [Bradbury & Fincham 1990, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.107.1.3)
- [M] **Fincham & Bradbury (1993).** 130 couples, 12-month lag. Non-benign attributions for negative partner behaviour predicted lower satisfaction a year later. This held controlling for depression, self-esteem and initial satisfaction, and after excluding people with chronic individual or marital disorder. US. Causal ID: prospective, with controls. — [Fincham & Bradbury 1993, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.3.442)
- [M] **Karney & Bradbury (2000).** 8 waves of newlywed data (N not in abstract; US).
  - No support for an enduring attributional style: attributions changed linearly over time.
  - Within-spouse changes in attributions strongly tracked changes in satisfaction.
  - Initial attributions predicted *changes* in satisfaction more than initial satisfaction predicted changes in attributions.

  Causal ID: growth-curve, temporal precedence. — [Karney & Bradbury 2000, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.295)
- [M] **Fincham, Harold & Gano-Phillips (2000).** Couples married 15–20 months, followed 18 months. Cross-lagged paths were significant in both directions for both spouses: attributions → later satisfaction *and* satisfaction → later attributions. Efficacy expectations mediated. Causal ID: cross-lagged panel, reciprocal. — [Fincham et al. 2000, J Fam Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.14.2.267)
- [M] **Durtschi et al. (2011).** 280 couples, Family Transitions Project (US), first 4 years of marriage. Early responsibility attributions predicted marital quality 4 years later, controlling for initial quality. Warm and hostile behaviour at year 2 mediated the effect. APIM. — [Durtschi et al. 2011, Fam Relat](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2011.00655.x)

#### Instruments
- **Relationship Attribution Measure (RAM).** Brief vignette-based measure of causal and responsibility attributions for hypothetical negative partner behaviours. High internal consistency and test–retest reliability in 3 married-couple studies. Responsibility attributions correlated with reported anger, wives' displayed anger and whining during problem-solving. Korean or East Asian validation: none located in this search (gap). — [Fincham & Bradbury 1992, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.62.3.457)

### Inferences
- Attribution research measures *meaning* given to (mostly hypothetical, negative) partner behaviours, not missed or misread *care*. Stretching it to "B attributes A's caring act to an ulterior motive, so it registers as 40" is plausible. [SPEC] However, the RAM vignettes are negative behaviours, so evidence for attributions about *positive* acts is thinner.
- Reciprocal causation means the translation hypothesis cannot claim attributions as purely upstream. The best wording is "interpretation and satisfaction co-evolve; interpretation has some temporal precedence" (Karney & Bradbury 2000).

### Gaps
- Large-scale non-WEIRD longitudinal tests of attributions → satisfaction were not located in this search.
- Attributions for *positive* partner behaviour (e.g., discounting care as obligation) were not searched in depth.

## Actor–observer asymmetry: how big is it, and what does that imply for a hypothesis built on it?

### Takeaway
Malle's meta-analysis of 173 studies found the classic asymmetry (actors cite situations, observers cite dispositions) to be essentially zero on average (d = −0.016 to 0.095). It held only under narrow conditions, one of which is when actor and observer are intimates. Any hypothesis that "A explains own lapses by circumstance, B explains them by A's character" should **not** rest on the classic actor–observer effect. Grade **[E]** that the classic effect is near zero.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Malle (2006) meta-analysis.** 173 published studies; average effect sizes d = −0.016 to 0.095. The asymmetry appeared only when:
  - the actor was portrayed as highly idiosyncratic,
  - the events explained were hypothetical,
  - actor and observer were intimates,
  - or explanations were free-response coded.

  It held for negative events but *reversed* for positive events, a self-serving valence pattern. Across valence, there was no asymmetry. — [Malle 2006, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.895)
- [M] **Malle, Knobe & Nelson (2007).** Six studies found no evidence for the traditional person/situation hypothesis. They found systematic actor–observer differences of a different kind, in the *modes* of explanation (folk-conceptual theory). These were driven by impression management, general knowledge and co-presence. — [Malle et al. 2007, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.93.4.491)
- [T] **De Los Reyes & Kazdin (2005).** The Attribution Bias Context (ABC) model explains informant discrepancies through informants' attributions, perspectives and the clinical context. It builds on attribution ideas, which Malle's meta-analysis later undercut in their classic person/situation form. Characterisation partly from the framework description; full text not checked here. — [De Los Reyes & Kazdin 2005, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.131.4.483)

#### Instruments
- **Free-response explanation coding** (folk-conceptual coding scheme in Malle et al. 2007). Korean validation: none located.

### Inferences
- The valence pattern (asymmetry for negative events, reversed for positive events) predicts something *self-serving*, not perspectival. Applied to care [SPEC]: A explains their own *failures* to provide care situationally and their *successes* dispositionally ("I'm a caring person"). Observers do the opposite only for negative events. This is a motivated-reasoning account, not a perspective-taking account.
- The intimates moderator is the one condition directly relevant to couples. Its effect size could not be extracted from the abstract.

### Gaps
- The effect size for the intimates subset in Malle 2006 was not verified. If the hypothesis leans on it, check the paper's moderator tables.

## Egocentric overestimation of one's own contributions and sacrifices: magnitudes and replications

### Takeaway
Over-claiming responsibility for joint outcomes is robust across couples, academics, MBA groups and sports, and grows with group size. The best-supported mechanism is that one's own contributions are more available in memory. Over-claiming falls when people "unpack" others' contributions individually, but doing so reduced satisfaction among those who had contributed most. Two findings complicate the giver-overestimation story: sacrificers *underestimate* their own sacrifice costs, and in intergenerational support both sides over-report giving. Grade **[E]** for over-claiming; **[M]** for its application to care adequacy.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Ross & Sicoly (1979).** Married couples estimated their responsibility for household activities such as making breakfast, shopping and important decisions. Summed self-allocations exceeded 100%. Similar results appeared with academics. N = 37 couples per secondary bibliographic records; the exact count of over-100% couples was **not verified** from primary text. Probably Canada (authors' affiliation), not verified; WEIRD either way. — [Ross & Sicoly 1979, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.3.322); description in [Caruso et al. 2006 PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Caruso-et_al_Undoing_Egocentrism_JPSP2006.pdf)
- [M] **Kruger & Gilovich (1999), "naive cynicism."** Four studies: married couples, video gamers, debaters and darts players. People expected *others'* allocations to be motivationally biased (self-serving) but not their own. This held whether or not actual allocations were biased. Teammates were expected to be less biased than opponents. US. — [Kruger & Gilovich 1999, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.5.743)
- [M] **Savitsky, Van Boven, Epley & Wight (2005), unpacking effect.** Across debate teams, MBA groups and academic group projects, people asked to consider collaborators *individually* claimed significantly less credit than controls. Abstract not available via API; summary from Caruso et al. 2006's description. — [Savitsky et al. 2005, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2004.08.008); [Caruso et al. 2006 PDF](https://anderson-review.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Caruso-et_al_Undoing_Egocentrism_JPSP2006.pdf)
- [M] **Caruso, Epley & Bazerman (2006).** Four experiments. After considering others' contributions, members who had contributed *much* became less satisfied and less interested in future collaboration than low contributors, especially in cooperative groups. US. Causal ID: experimental. — [Caruso et al. 2006, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.91.5.857)
- [E] **Schroeder, Caruso & Epley (2016).** Two field studies (academic author teams; MBA study groups) and two experiments. Over-claiming increased with group size and was reduced by explicitly considering others' contributions. US. — [Schroeder et al. 2016, JEP:Applied](https://doi.org/10.1037/xap0000080)
- [M] **Mandemakers & Dykstra (2008).** Netherlands Kinship Panel Study, 4,055 parent–child dyads.
  - Parents and children both over-report help given and under-report help received.
  - Parents were *not* generally positively biased, contrary to the intergenerational stake hypothesis.
  - Positive biases appeared with strong family-obligation norms and in high-quality relationships.
  - More educated respondents reported more accurately.

  Causal ID: none. — [Mandemakers & Dykstra 2008, JMF](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2008.00496.x)
- [M] **Disconfirming for "givers inflate sacrifice": Visserman et al. (2020).** Study 1: 125 couples, lab conversation plus experience sampling. Study 2: preregistered, N = 775, imagined sacrifice by self, partner or a stranger. A consistent own-vs-partner discrepancy was driven mainly by people *underestimating their own sacrifice costs* and overestimating the benefits. The underestimation was associated with feeling better and more satisfied. Country not stated in abstract. — [Visserman et al. 2020, SPPS](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550619896671)
- [P] **Housework reports.** Husbands' and wives' reports of household labour diverge systematically (a "he said, she said" analysis). Direction and size not verified here: the abstract was not retrievable. — [Kamo 2000, Soc Sci Res](https://doi.org/10.1006/ssre.2000.0674)

### Inferences
- The availability mechanism predicts the giver's high self-rating without any bad faith: A remembers A's own acts, and B remembers B's needs and A's lapses. This is a parsimonious, well-supported alternative to "translation failure." The gap can arise even if every act is perfectly perceived when it happens. Grade: [M].
- Caruso 2006 is a warning for interventions [M→SPEC for couples]. Making givers see the other's contributions may lower the satisfaction of the higher contributor. Correcting "translation" errors is not automatically benign.
- [SPEC] Visserman 2020 suggests a domain split. Givers may over-claim *share of work* (availability) while under-claiming the *cost* of sacrifices (a motivated, satisfaction-protecting bias). A "90" that refers to effort and a "90" that refers to cost would behave differently.

### Gaps
- The exact Ross & Sicoly statistics (how many couples exceeded 100%; whether the bias held for negative items such as "causing arguments") were not verified from primary text.
- No direct replication of Ross & Sicoly with couples rating *caregiving adequacy* was found, and no Korean replication was located.

## Giver–recipient asymmetries (gifts, gratitude, compliments, support, kindness): which way do givers err, and does it depend on the type of care?

### Takeaway
Givers err in **both** directions depending on the type of care.
- For **costly, material or surprise gifts**, givers over-weight price and novelty and expect more appreciation than recipients report, so the giver *over*values.
- For **warm or expressive acts** (gratitude, compliments, kindness, reaching out with support), givers *under*estimate the recipient's positive response and overestimate awkwardness.

Proposed mechanisms: givers focus on the moment of exchange, on competence or cost, and on social norms; recipients focus on warmth and on the item's usefulness over time. Evidence is overwhelmingly US. **No non-US replications were located.** Grade **[M]**.

### Cited Findings
- [M] **Flynn & Adams (2009).** Three studies. Givers expected pricier gifts to elicit more appreciation; recipients reported no link between price and appreciation. One study involved engagement rings: about 33 recently engaged individuals per press summaries, sample details unverified. US. Causal ID: mixed survey and experimental. — [Flynn & Adams 2009, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2008.11.003); [Stanford GSB summary](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/research-confirms-its-thought-counts)
- [M] **Gino & Flynn (2011).** Five studies (first: wedding-registry gifts). Recipients appreciated *requested* gifts more and saw them as more thoughtful. Givers assumed requested and unrequested gifts would be equally appreciated, expecting unrequested gifts to signal thoughtfulness. Givers also thought money would be appreciated less than a requested gift; recipients felt the reverse. Details per publisher and press summaries; abstract not available via API. — [Gino & Flynn 2011, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2011.03.015); [Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/give-them-what-they-want-benefits-explicitness-gift-exchange)
- [M] **Zhang & Epley (2012).** Four experiments.
  - A giver's thoughtfulness increased recipients' appreciation only when something triggered the recipient to consider it, e.g., a *bad* gift from a friend.
  - Givers mispredicted when their thoughts would count.
  - Thoughtfulness "counted" for givers themselves by increasing their sense of connection.

  The authors conclude that mental-state inferences are not automatic. — [Zhang & Epley 2012, JEP:General](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0029223)
- [T/M] **Galak, Givi & Williams (2016) framework.** Givers focus on the moment of exchange; recipients focus on the gift's value during ownership. This partly explains many documented asymmetries. — [Galak et al. 2016, Curr Dir Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721416656937)
- [M] **Givi & Galak (2024).** Five experiments supporting a social-norms framework. Asymmetries arise when one option is less descriptively (but not less injunctively) normative, with *discomfort* as the mechanism. Asymmetries were absent where the framework predicted none. — [Givi & Galak 2024, Eur J Mark](https://doi.org/10.1108/ejm-04-2022-0311)
- [M] **Kumar & Epley (2018), gratitude.** Three experiments. Writers of gratitude letters:
  - underestimated recipients' surprise and positive feelings,
  - overestimated recipients' awkwardness.

  Expected awkwardness and mood predicted willingness to express gratitude. US. Causal ID: paired forecasting vs actual reports. — [Kumar & Epley 2018, Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618772506)
- [M] **Zhao & Epley (2021), compliments.** Nine experiments. Expressers underestimated recipients' positivity and overestimated awkwardness. Mechanism: expressers underestimate how competent, and to a lesser extent how warm, their compliments seem. Directing attention to warmth increased willingness to compliment (experimental). — [Zhao & Epley 2021, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspa0000277)
- [M] **Boothby & Bohns (2021), independent lab.** Five studies. Compliment givers underestimated recipients' positive feelings and overestimated their discomfort, even in hindsight. Third-party forecasters were accurate. The giver's own anxiety and competence concerns drove the error. — [Boothby & Bohns 2021, PSPB](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220949003)
- [M] **Kumar & Epley (2023), random kindness.** Field and lab experiments. Givers underestimated recipients' positivity. Recipients' reactions were boosted by the warmth conveyed; givers' expectations were insensitive to warmth. Givers also underestimated downstream "pay-it-forward" behaviour. — [Kumar & Epley 2023, JEP:General](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0001271)
- [M] **Dungan, Munguia Gomez & Epley (2022), reaching out with support.** Expectations predicted willingness to express support (Study 1, N = 100). People sending support to acquaintances (Study 2, N = 120 students) or in person to new acquaintances (Study 3, N = 50 pairs) underestimated how positively recipients responded. Mechanism (Study 4, N = 300): expressers focus on competence, recipients on warmth. — [Dungan et al. 2022, Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976221082942)
- [T] **Epley et al. (2022), "undersociality" review.** Miscalibrated expectations across conversation, appreciation and kindness. Proposed mechanisms: differential construal, uncertain responsiveness and asymmetric learning (people rarely get feedback that would correct the miscalibration). — [Epley et al. 2022, Trends Cogn Sci](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2022.02.007)
- **Non-US replications:** none located in two targeted searches (Japan, Korea, China, Hong Kong, Turkey). — search record only, no source.

#### Instruments
- **Paired forecasting paradigm.** The expresser predicts the recipient's feelings on the same scales the recipient later uses. No standardized, validated instrument exists.
- **Appreciation in Relationships (AIR) scale** for the recipient side (felt appreciation and appreciating). Korean validation: not located. — [Gordon et al. 2012, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0028723)

### Inferences
- For the hypothesis, the *type of care* is a key moderator.
  - Material, costly or "big gesture" care: the giver may sit at 90 while the recipient sits at 40. This fits the illustration.
  - Warm or expressive care: the error reverses (the giver expects 40 when the recipient feels 90). This does *not* produce the illustrative gap. Instead it predicts **under-provision**: givers express less warmth than would be optimal.
  - Grade: [M] for each direction; [SPEC] for generalising from strangers and acquaintances to long-term partners and parents.
- Most studies involve strangers, acquaintances or one-off acts and measure momentary affect. Whether the asymmetries survive inside long-term relationships, where recipients have strong priors (sentiment override), is untested in the sources found.
- The competence-vs-warmth construal gap (Zhao & Epley 2021; Dungan 2022) is a concrete, testable "translation" mechanism: givers judge their care on execution quality, recipients on felt warmth.

### Gaps
- No non-WEIRD replications were found, an important gap given cultural differences in gift and gratitude norms.
- No study of these forecast asymmetries in established romantic or parent–child dyads over repeated acts was located.

## Encoding/decoding paradigms in couples: are deficits specific to the relationship, and are encoding or decoding errors larger?

### Takeaway
Standard-content paradigms show that less satisfied couples communicate affect less accurately. Wives tend to be better encoders, and husbands' sending (Noller) or receiving (Gottman & Porterfield) is the satisfaction-linked weak point. Crucially, the decoding deficits are **specific to the relationship**: unhappy spouses misread their own spouse but read strangers normally. That points to distress causing misreading rather than a general skill deficit causing distress. Spouses also overestimate how well they communicate with each other. Grade **[M]** (small, older, WEIRD samples).

### Cited Findings
- [M] **Kahn (1970).** 21 satisfied and 21 dissatisfied college couples (Locke–Wallace). Satisfaction was related to accuracy of non-verbal communication, measured both behaviourally (Marital Communication Scale, MCS) and by self-report (Primary Communication Inventory). The two measures shared little variance. US presumed. — [Kahn 1970, Fam Process](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1545-5300.1970.00449.x)
- [M] **Noller (1980).** 48 married couples exchanged a standard set of ambiguous messages whose meaning (positive, neutral or negative) depended on non-verbal delivery. Judges classified each error as an encoding or a decoding error.
  - Women were better encoders, especially of positive messages.
  - High-adjustment couples communicated better, mainly because husbands sent clearer messages and made fewer errors.
  - A higher share of errors on wives' messages was attributable to husbands' decoding.

  Likely Australian (the author's Univ. of Queensland thesis); not stated in abstract. — [Noller 1980, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077716) (**correct DOI**; the candidate lead 10.1037/0022-3514.39.6.1135 does not resolve)
- [M] **Noller (1981).** The same couples decoded opposite-sex strangers. Low-adjustment spouses decoded their own spouses worse but did not differ when decoding strangers. The author suggests communication problems may be a *consequence* of unhappiness. — [Noller 1981, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.2.272)
- [M] **Noller & Venardos (1986).** 46 married couples.
  - Low-adjustment spouses were equally confident in correct and incorrect decodings.
  - High-adjustment spouses, especially wives, better predicted whether their spouse would decode a message correctly.
  - Message clarity ratings did not differ by adjustment.
  - Negative messages were rated clearer and predicted more accurately.

  Meta-communication (knowing when you have been understood) tracks adjustment. — [Noller & Venardos 1986, JSPR](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407586031003)
- [M] **Gottman & Porterfield (1981).** 21 couples plus 21 stranger husbands and wives. Satisfaction related to non-verbal competence only for *husbands reading wives*. Male strangers read wives equally well regardless of the wife's satisfaction. The deficit lay in husbands as receivers of their own wives' cues. US. — [Gottman & Porterfield 1981, JMF](https://doi.org/10.2307/351339)
- [M] **Gaelick, Bodenhausen & Wyer (1985).** 29 couples, video discussion, with ratings of the communicator's *intended* feelings and the recipient's *perceived* feelings.
  - Partners tried to reciprocate perceived positive and negative feelings, but only negative feelings were actually reciprocated.
  - Recipients decoded negative feelings accurately and positive feelings inaccurately.
  - Men read absence of love as hostility; women read absence of hostility as love.

  Hostility parameters related to women's satisfaction only. — [Gaelick et al. 1985, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.49.5.1246)
- [M] **Savitsky, Keysar, Epley, Carter & Swanson (2011), closeness-communication bias.** Study 2b: 24 married couples (married 14.4 years on average; Williamstown, MA). Speakers expected their spouse to understand ambiguous phrases better than strangers would. In fact accuracy with spouses and strangers was statistically identical (paired t(11) = 1.08, n.s.). Speakers were more confident they had been understood by their spouse than by strangers (M = 6.27 vs 5.00; paired t(11) = 5.97). Small N; US. Causal ID: within-subject experiment. — [Savitsky et al. 2011, JESP](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2010.09.005); [full text PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/2.-Savitsky-Comm-Bias.pdf)
- [M] **Illusion of transparency.** People overestimate how well others can read their internal states, through insufficient adjustment from their own experience. — [Gilovich, Savitsky & Medvec 1998, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.75.2.332)
- [P — finding UNVERIFIED] **Gottman et al. (1976), "talk table."** Speakers rated the intended impact of each message and listeners rated its actual impact. The commonly cited result is that distressed and non-distressed couples differed in *impact* but not *intent*. That result **could not be verified** from primary text here; only the DOI was verified. — [Gottman, Notarius, Markman, Bank, Yoppi & Rubin 1976, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.34.1.14)

#### Instruments
- **Marital Communication Scale (MCS) / standard-content paradigm.** Scripted ambiguous messages with judge-defined intended meaning; scores encoding vs decoding accuracy. Low overlap with self-report communication measures. — [Kahn 1970](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1545-5300.1970.00449.x); [Noller 1980](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077716)
- **Stranger-benchmark design** (separating sender from receiver deficits). — [Gottman & Porterfield 1981](https://doi.org/10.2307/351339); [Noller 1981](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.2.272)
- **Intent–impact ratings** (talk table; Gaelick-style communicator vs recipient ratings). — [Gottman et al. 1976](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.34.1.14); [Gaelick et al. 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.49.5.1246)
- **Confidence and clarity meta-communication ratings.** — [Noller & Venardos 1986](https://doi.org/10.1177/0265407586031003)
- Korean validations: none located.

### Inferences
- **Encoding vs decoding errors:** neither is uniformly larger. Noller (1980) found husbands' *encoding* most linked to adjustment, while errors on wives' messages were more often the husbands' *decoding*. Gottman & Porterfield located the deficit in husbands' *decoding*. Results depend on sex and on the benchmark used. Grade: [M].
- The stranger-benchmark findings are among the strongest *disconfirming* evidence for a "skill-deficit translation" account. The miscommunication is relationship-specific and plausibly produced by distress, a reverse-causation reading the authors themselves raised. Grade: [M].
- Gaelick 1985 and Savitsky 2011 support a narrower, still important claim. Even when decoding is no worse than with strangers, intimates *overestimate* how well they are understood. That overconfidence would sustain "I gave 90" beliefs. Grade: [M].

### Gaps
- No modern, large, preregistered replication of the standard-content paradigm in couples was found.
- The Gottman 1976 intent–impact result needs primary-text verification.

## Sentiment override: does global relationship sentiment change how identical behaviours are perceived?

### Takeaway
Yes, within limits. Spouses' global sentiment colours their ratings of the partner's specific behaviours relative to objective coders, mainly for positive and low-intensity negative affect, not for high-intensity hostility. Newer psychometric work suggests most self-report relationship measures largely tap one global-sentiment factor. Two implications follow. Perceived care may be downstream of how much one already loves, which is reverse causation for the hypothesis. And self-reported "translation" facets may not be separable from global satisfaction. Grade **[M]**, with a strong measurement implication.

### Cited Findings
- [T] **Weiss (1980)** coined "sentiment override": global sentiment overrides perception of specific partner behaviour. Book chapter (Advances in Family Intervention, Assessment and Theory, Vol. 1); no DOI; citation details UNVERIFIED here.
- [M] **Hawkins, Carrère & Gottman (2002).** 96 newlywed couples. Spouses' ratings of the partner's specific affect were compared with objective coders.
  - Wives' marital bond predicted their ratings of husbands' positive affect and low-intensity negative affect.
  - Positive sentiment override appeared when wives rated husbands' low-intensity negativity.
  - Neither spouse showed override for high-intensity negative affect.

  US. Causal ID: none. — [Hawkins et al. 2002, JMF](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00193.x)
- [M] **Fincham, Garnier, Gano-Phillips & Osborne (1995).** 92 couples. Satisfaction correlated with expectations of partner behaviour held *before* the interaction, and those expectations mediated the satisfaction–affect link. Accessibility of evaluative judgments (response latency) moderated the link for husbands. — [Fincham et al. 1995, J Fam Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.9.1.3)
- [M] **Kim, Joel et al. (2026).** Two US census-matched samples, N = 3,439; exploratory bifactor analyses of items from 34 measures.
  - A single general factor Q (global sentiment) explained the vast majority of common variance.
  - The authors frame this as consistent with sentiment override.
  - Press coverage reports Q captured more than 70% of facet variance and that sexual satisfaction stood partly apart; these figures were not verified in the paper.
  - Authors' caveat: the dominance of Q may be specific to self-report.

  Published April 2026; preprint 2024. — [Kim et al. 2026, PLOS ONE](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0342451); [EurekAlert release](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1121756)
- [M] **Joel et al. (2020).** 43 dyadic longitudinal datasets from 29 labs, machine learning (random forests).
  - Top relationship-specific predictors: perceived partner commitment, appreciation, sexual satisfaction, perceived partner satisfaction and conflict.
  - Relationship-specific variables predicted up to 45% of the variance at baseline and up to 18% at the end of each study.
  - Actor reports predicted 2–4× more variance than partner reports, and partner reports added nothing beyond actor-reported relationship-specific variables.
  - *Change* in quality was largely unpredictable.

  Multi-country but mostly WEIRD. — [Joel et al. 2020, PNAS](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1917036117)
- [P — details UNVERIFIED] **Classic behavioural-observation studies.**
  - Robinson & Price (1980) compared spouses' and observers' records of pleasurable behaviour. The commonly cited result is that distressed spouses under-recorded positive behaviour; it was **not verified**. DOI verified: [Robinson & Price 1980, JCCP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.48.1.117).
  - Jacobson & Moore (1981) studied spouses as observers of daily events. A secondary source reports distressed couples overlook positive aspects of their spouse's behaviour; agreement percentages were **not verified**. — [Jacobson & Moore 1981, JCCP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.49.2.269)
  - A clinical replication of the same design exists: [Elwood & Jacobson 1982](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.50.5.783).

#### Instruments
- **Observer coding against spouse ratings** (Hawkins et al. 2002 operationalised override as the gap between spouse rating and objective coding). The coding system cited is SPAFF (Specific Affect Coding System; chapter source, no DOI verified).
- **Video-recall rating dial** (continuous self-rated affect). 30 couples; ratings discriminated high- vs low-conflict interactions, correlated with satisfaction, were coherent between spouses and related to observer coding. — [Gottman & Levenson 1985, JCCP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.53.2.151)
- **Pre-interaction expectation and accessibility (latency) measures.** — [Fincham et al. 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.9.1.3)
- **Global factor modelling (bifactor) to estimate how much of a facet is global sentiment.** — [Kim et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0342451)
- Korean validations: none located.

### Inferences
- Sentiment override offers a strong rival explanation for "B experiences 40." Low global sentiment in B could pull B's ratings of identical acts downward, especially mild or ambiguous ones. On this view the gap is a symptom of low love or satisfaction, not its cause. A design that cannot rule this out (e.g., by observer-coded benchmarks or experimental standard stimuli) cannot support the translation hypothesis. Grade: [M].
- The "high-intensity negativity is not overridden" boundary implies override operates on *ambiguous* signals. [SPEC] Subtle or ambiguous care (practical help, quiet presence) should be most vulnerable to being under- or over-perceived according to B's global sentiment.
- Joel et al. 2020 cuts both ways.
  - For the hypothesis: perceived partner commitment and appreciation dominate prediction, so "love perceived" matters.
  - Against it: the partner's own report adds nothing. So "love given," as the giver reports it, is not a separately useful predictor. Neither is any gap computed from it, unless gaps are modelled explicitly (e.g., response surfaces).

### Gaps
- The Robinson & Price (1980) and Jacobson & Moore (1981) numbers need primary-text verification.
- No experimental manipulation of global sentiment followed by rating of *identical, standardised care acts* was located.

## Retrospective vs momentary assessments: does memory rewrite how much care was given and received?

### Takeaway
Retrospective judgments of relationships are systematically reconstructed. Partners "remember" improvement that contemporaneous scales do not show. Wives' memories of earlier marriage are negatively biased in ways that sustain current satisfaction. Memories of a partner's responsiveness are biased by the perceiver's desire to bond. A "90 vs 40" obtained from global retrospective questions is therefore not a measurement of what happened day to day. Grade **[M]**.

### Cited Findings
- [M] **Sprecher (1999).** Multi-wave longitudinal study over several years (N not in abstract; US).
  - Partners in intact relationships reported at each wave that love, commitment and satisfaction had *increased*, but contemporaneous scale scores did not generally rise.
  - Perceived change was nevertheless related to actual change and to future stability.
  - Partners who later broke up reported decreases in the months before.

  Causal ID: longitudinal comparison of retrospective vs concurrent scores. — [Sprecher 1999, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.1.46)
- [M] **Karney & Coombs (2000).** 20 years of retrospective and longitudinal data from wives (N not in abstract; US).
  - At 10 years, memories of the past were negatively biased, so the present seemed an improvement.
  - At 20 years, memories remained negatively biased but resembled current perceptions.
  - Concurrent ratings actually declined.
  - Memory bias at Time 2 predicted the course of satisfaction over the next 10 years.

  — [Karney & Coombs 2000, PSPB](https://doi.org/10.1177/01461672002610006)
- [M] **Lemay & Neal (2013), "wishful memory."** Study 1: dyadic daily reports. Study 2: dyadic longitudinal study with behavioural observation.
  - Perceivers' valuing of the partner biased their *memories* of the partner's responsiveness, independently of their own initial encoding, the partner's sentiments and the partner's behaviour.
  - The bias was not explained by satisfaction, attachment, self-esteem or consistency effects.
  - Biased memories reduced emotional reactivity and fed trust.

  US. — [Lemay & Neal 2013, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030422)

#### Instruments
- **Ecological momentary assessment (EMA)** and daily diaries, which reduce recall bias by sampling in real time. — [Shiffman, Stone & Hufford 2008, Annu Rev Clin Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.clinpsy.3.022806.091415)
- **Day Reconstruction Method (DRM).** Structured reconstruction of the previous day; showed close correspondence with experience sampling in 909 employed women (US). — [Kahneman et al. 2004, Science](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1103572)
- **Retrospective-change items vs repeated contemporaneous scales** (Sprecher's design). — [Sprecher 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.1.46)

### Inferences
- [SPEC] A giver's global "I provided 90" and a receiver's global "I received 40" are each reconstructions. For the giver, this can include availability of own acts (egocentric bias). For the receiver, it can include current sentiment projected backward (Karney & Coombs; Lemay & Neal) or salient lapses. Comparing *aggregated daily reports* with *end-of-period global reports* from both partners would show how much of the gap is created by memory.
- Lemay & Neal show that motivated memory can *raise* remembered care when the perceiver values the partner. So the receiver's memory bias should run upward in committed relationships and downward in disengaging ones. This again ties the "gap" to sentiment.

### Gaps
- No study comparing momentary vs retrospective reports of *care provided vs care received* within the same dyads was located.

## Parent–child informant discrepancies and the intergenerational stake: who reports more positive care, by how much, and does it hold outside the US?

### Takeaway
Parents consistently report more favourable parenting than their adolescents do. Agreement is modest (r ≈ .25–.29) and the mean-level gap is small to medium (g ≈ .2–.5, larger for psychological control and in older adolescents). Discordance is *higher* in more individualistic societies such as the US. The intergenerational stake (parents more invested and more positive) has US support, a Korean dyadic conference-abstract match, and a large Dutch study *contrary* to a general parental positive bias, where instead both sides over-report giving. Treating informant discrepancies purely as error is now contested: they can carry real information. Grade **[E]** for parent-favourable discrepancies about parenting; **[M]** for intergenerational stake generality.

### Cited Findings
- [E] **Korelitz & Garber (2016) meta-analysis.** 85 studies, 476 effect sizes, all using the CRPBI (Children's Report of Parental Behavior Inventory).
  - Mean agreement r = .23–.28 for mothers and .23–.29 for fathers.
  - Mother–child agreement by construct: acceptance r = .28, psychological control r = .27, behavioural control r = .23.
  - Directional gaps (child minus parent):
    - children reported less maternal acceptance (g = −.21 [−.34, −.07]) and less paternal acceptance (g = −.25 [−.40, −.09]);
    - children reported more maternal psychological control (g = .54 [.38, .70]);
    - no directional gap for maternal behavioural control, but the absolute discrepancy was g = .43 [.34, .53].
  - The maternal-acceptance gap grew with age: ≤11 y g = −.01; 11–14 y g = −.28; ≥14 y g = −.50. For fathers, ≥14 y g = −.50.
  - Moderators included age, race, clinical status and family intactness.

  Causal ID: none. — [Korelitz & Garber 2016, J Youth Adolesc](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10964-016-0524-0); [PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5222679/)
- [E] **Hou et al. (2020).** Meta-analysis of 313 studies plus a qualitative review of 36 studies linking discordance to outcomes.
  - r = .276 [.262, .290]; parents more positive, g = .242 [.188, .296].
  - Discordance was higher for younger and male adolescents, non-clinical parents, and **more individualistic societies such as the US**.
  - Within US samples it was higher for ethnic-minority, low-SES and non-intact families.

  — [Hou et al. 2020, Am Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000463)
- [E] **Cross-informant agreement on child psychopathology.**
  - Parent–child agreement r = .25 across 119 studies and 269 samples ([Achenbach, McConaughy & Howell 1987](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.101.2.213); r value as reported in [Korelitz & Garber 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5222679/)).
  - 341 studies: mean r = .28 (internalizing .25, externalizing .30) ([De Los Reyes et al. 2015, Psychol Bull](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038498)).
- [T/M] **Discrepancies as information, not error.**
  - The Operations Triad Model argues researchers often cannot justify treating informant discrepancies as measurement error ([De Los Reyes, Thomas, Goodman & Kundey 2013](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-clinpsy-050212-185617)).
  - Review of adolescent–parent discrepancies about family relationships: [De Los Reyes, Ohannessian & Racz 2019](https://doi.org/10.1111/cdep.12306).
- [M] **Laird & De Los Reyes (2013).** 218 US parent–adolescent dyads. Difference scores gave invalid tests. Interaction terms in polynomial regression showed that discrepancies did predict adolescent psychopathology. — [Laird & De Los Reyes 2013, J Abnorm Child Psychol](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10802-012-9659-y)
- [T] **Bengtson & Kuypers (1971).** Original "developmental (generational) stake": parents have greater investment in the relationship than children, and so perceive it more positively. — [Bengtson & Kuypers 1971, Aging Hum Dev](https://doi.org/10.2190/AG.2.4.b)
- [P — UNVERIFIED] **Giarrusso, Stallings & Bengtson (1995)**, a 20-year follow-up chapter on the intergenerational stake. Book chapter; no DOI verified; findings not checked.
- [M] **Birditt et al. (2015).** N = 633 adults aged 40–60. Within-person, people rated ties with their children as more important (and more negative) than ties with their parents. Those with the reverse pattern had poorer well-being. US (not stated in abstract). — [Birditt et al. 2015, JMF](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12203)
- [M] **Contrary evidence: Mandemakers & Dykstra (2008).** Netherlands, 4,055 dyads. No general positive bias in parents' reports; both generations over-report giving and under-report receiving. — [Mandemakers & Dykstra 2008](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2008.00496.x)
- [P] **East Asian evidence.**
  - Korea, non-WEIRD: 105 dyads of very old parents (81–97) and their children (65–72). Parents reported *higher support and lower conflict* than their children, consistent with the stake hypothesis. Own perceived conflict predicted depressive symptoms (parents β = 0.26). Conference abstract, not a full peer-reviewed article. — [Han et al. 2020, Innovation in Aging](https://doi.org/10.1093/geroni/igaa057.1119)
  - Japanese Canadian families: 100 interviewed dyads; most were *congruent* on filial obligation. That is congruence on values, not relationship quality. — [Kobayashi & Funk 2010, Can J Aging](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0714980809990341)
- [M] **Meaning differs by culture.** Korean adolescents' perceptions of parental control correlated *positively* with perceived warmth and low neglect, unlike North American findings. The same behaviour ("control") carried a different meaning. Older study. — [Rohner & Pettengill 1985, Child Dev](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129739)
- [E/M] **Perceived (not intended) parental rejection predicts maladjustment across cultures.** 43 studies worldwide: r = .51 for children and r = .46 for adults. Correlational, shared-method self-report. — [Khaleque & Rohner 2002, JMF](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00054.x)

#### Instruments
- **Children's Report of Parental Behavior Inventory (CRPBI).** The instrument pooled in Korelitz & Garber; acceptance, psychological control and behavioural control. Korean validation not located. — [Schaefer 1965, Child Dev](https://doi.org/10.2307/1126465)
- **Parental Bonding Instrument (PBI).** — [Parker, Tupling & Brown 1979](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8341.1979.tb02487.x). Korean version: not verified in this search.
- **Parental Acceptance–Rejection Questionnaire (PARQ).** Theory and cross-cultural evidence summarised in [Rohner, Khaleque & Cournoyer 2005, Ethos](https://doi.org/10.1525/eth.2005.33.3.299). Used with Korean adolescents in [Rohner & Pettengill 1985](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129739); a formal Korean psychometric validation was not verified.
- **Intergenerational relationship quality** (importance, positive and negative quality). — [Birditt et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12203). Bengtson's Positive Affect Index is a chapter-based instrument; no DOI verified.

### Inferences
- For "care provided vs care experienced," parent–child data are the best-quantified analogue. The parent-favourable gap is reliable, but in typical samples it is **small (g ≈ .2–.25 overall), not 50 points**. It is larger for psychological control and in older adolescents. Grade: [E] for the magnitude range.
- The individualism moderator (Hou 2020) means the gap is *not* culturally universal. [SPEC] If discordance is lower in less individualistic societies, a Korean family sample may show smaller parent–child gaps on standard scales. Reference-group and meaning effects (Heine 2002; Rohner & Pettengill 1985) could mask or create differences.
- Mandemakers & Dykstra suggest that for *support exchanges*, egocentric giving/receiving bias may be a better model than a parental stake. Both parties report "I gave more than you say."

### Gaps
- The number and outcomes of Asian samples in Hou et al. 2020 were not extractable from the abstract.
- No full peer-reviewed test of the intergenerational stake hypothesis in Korea was found (only the conference abstract); other researchers cover Korean family work.
- Giarrusso et al. 1995 could not be verified.

## Measurement: how can researchers separate disagreement about whether an act occurred, disagreement about what it meant, and differences in how each person uses a rating scale?

### Takeaway
The three sources of disagreement need different tools.
- **(a) Occurrence:** act-level dyadic diaries scored with quasi-signal-detection logic (hits, misses, false alarms), plus kappa and observer benchmarks.
- **(b) Meaning:** intent–impact ratings of *matched* acts, standard-stimulus paradigms with stranger benchmarks, and attribution measures.
- **(c) Scale use:** anchoring vignettes, T&B directional-bias terms, response-style models, and response surface or polynomial models instead of difference scores.

Each tool rests on assumptions that can fail, notably the vignette equivalence and response consistency assumptions. Grade **[E]** for the methods' existence and logic; **[M]** for their validity in relationship data, which is rarely tested.

### Cited Findings
**(a) Did the act occur?**
- [M] **Gable, Reis & Downey (2003), quasi-signal detection.** 58 dating couples, daily reports of own behaviours and of the partner's behaviours. The enactor's beliefs and the perceiver's interpretation *combined* to affect daily mood and satisfaction. For negative behaviours, the enactor's perspective had independent effects. US. — [Gable et al. 2003, Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.t01-1-01426)
- [M] **Visserman et al. (2019).** Extended quasi-signal detection to sacrifices (N = 426; see the Overall section). Missed sacrifices produced no gratitude and lowered the sacrificer's satisfaction. — [Visserman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599)
- [M] **Coriell & Cohen (1995).** Kappa = .39 for occurrence of support behaviours; concordance varied with intimacy and fulfilled expectations. — [Coriell & Cohen 1995](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.69.2.289)
- [M] **Invisible support.**
  - Diary: transactions reported by provider but not recipient ([Bolger et al. 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953)).
  - Experiments: 3 experiments, N = 257 women. Invisible support reduced emotional reactivity vs visible support and vs no support; visible support was ineffective or harmful ([Bolger & Amarel 2007, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.458)).
- [P] **Network/support "veridicality"** (principal vs network member agreement). Details not verified. — [Antonucci & Israel 1986, JCCP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006X.54.4.432)

**(b) What did the act mean?**
- [M] **Communicator-intended vs recipient-perceived feeling ratings** of the same messages. — [Gaelick et al. 1985](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.49.5.1246)
- [M] **Standard-content messages with judges and stranger decoders** to localise encoding vs decoding errors. — [Noller 1980](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0077716); [Noller 1981](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.41.2.272); [Gottman & Porterfield 1981](https://doi.org/10.2307/351339)
- [E] **Attribution measures** for meaning given to partner acts. — [Fincham & Bradbury 1992 (RAM)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.62.3.457)
- [M] **Same label, different meaning across cultures** (parental "control" as warmth in Korea). — [Rohner & Pettengill 1985](https://doi.org/10.2307/1129739)

**(c) Is each person using the scale differently?**
- [E] **Anchoring vignettes (King et al. 2004).** Respondents rate hypothetical vignette persons on the same scale as themselves. Because vignette levels are fixed, variation in vignette ratings reveals response-category incomparability, which can be corrected nonparametrically or with a model. The authors showed uncorrected incomparability can drastically mislead. — [King, Murray, Salomon & Tandon 2004, APSR](https://doi.org/10.1017/S000305540400108X)
  - Methods for evaluating and choosing vignettes: [King & Wand 2007, Polit Anal](https://doi.org/10.1093/pan/mpl011).
  - A latent-trait model using vignettes to control any response style: [Bolt, Lu & Kim 2014, Psychol Methods](https://doi.org/10.1037/met0000016).
- [M] **Critique of anchoring vignettes.** Formal tests of the two key assumptions, vignette equivalence and response consistency, **rejected both** for reports of cognitive and physical functioning among older English adults. A weaker test did not reject response consistency for cognition. — [Bago d'Uva et al. 2011, J Hum Resour](https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.46.4.875)
- [E] **Reference-group effect (Heine et al. 2002).**
  - Experts agreed East Asians are more collectivistic, but Likert self-reports failed to show it.
  - Manipulating the reference group enhanced the expected differences.
  - Cultural groups *within* one country differed more than groups across countries.
  - The authors conclude that cross-cultural comparisons of subjective Likert scales are compromised.

  — [Heine et al. 2002, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.6.903)
- [E] **Directional bias as an explicit parameter** in the T&B model. — [West & Kenny 2011](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0022936)
- [E] **Difference scores are problematic.**
  - Edwards reviews the problems and the myths around polynomial regression ([Edwards 2001, Organ Res Methods](https://doi.org/10.1177/109442810143005)).
  - Discrepancies can predict outcomes when modelled with polynomial-regression interactions rather than difference scores ([Laird & De Los Reyes 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10802-012-9659-y)).
  - Dyadic response surface analysis for similarity/discrepancy hypotheses ([Schönbrodt, Humberg & Nestler 2018, Eur J Pers](https://doi.org/10.1002/per.2169)).
- [E] **Cronbach's component critique and its modern SAM implementation** (separating elevation and normative accuracy from distinctive accuracy). — [Cronbach 1955](https://doi.org/10.1037/h0044919); [Biesanz 2010](https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2010.519262)
- [E] **Momentary vs retrospective tools** to separate memory reconstruction from experience. — [Shiffman et al. 2008](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.clinpsy.3.022806.091415); [Kahneman et al. 2004](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1103572)

### Inferences
- [SPEC] A design that could test the "90 vs 40" claim, assembled from the verified tools above (no single published study implements all layers):
  1. **Act ledger:** both partners log each day which caring acts A did (A's list) and which B noticed (B's list). Score hits, misses and false alarms, separately for material or instrumental acts and expressive acts (Gable 2003; Visserman 2019).
  2. **Matched intent–impact:** for every *matched* act, A rates intended care (0–100) and B rates experienced care (0–100). This isolates disagreement about meaning from disagreement about occurrence (Gaelick 1985).
  3. **Scale calibration:** both partners rate a shared set of standardised care vignettes on the same 0–100 scale, used as anchoring vignettes. Test vignette equivalence and response consistency rather than assuming them (King 2004; d'Uva 2011).
  4. **Sentiment control:** measure global sentiment (Q) and, where feasible, collect observer-coded benchmarks of recorded interactions. This tests override: does B's sentiment predict B's impact ratings beyond occurrence and coder ratings? (Hawkins 2002; Kim 2026)
  5. **Projection control:** measure B's own giving. Estimate the T&B truth force (A's report), the assumed-similarity force (B's own giving) and directional bias (West & Kenny 2011; Lemay 2007).
  6. **Memory layer:** collect end-of-period global "how much care did you give or receive" ratings and compare them with aggregated daily data (Sprecher 1999; Karney & Coombs 2000).
  7. **Outcome models:** response surface or polynomial models of A-report × B-report predicting later satisfaction, controlling prior satisfaction. Never use raw difference scores (Edwards 2001; Laird & De Los Reyes 2013; Schönbrodt 2018).
- [SPEC] The translation hypothesis would be **falsified** for a sample if:
  - most of the global 90–40 gap disappears after scale calibration and memory correction; or
  - B's impact ratings are explained by B's sentiment and projection rather than by A's acts; or
  - matched intent–impact gaps do not predict change in satisfaction beyond prior satisfaction.

### Gaps
- Anchoring vignettes have rarely, if ever, been used for relationship-care ratings (none found). Their key assumptions are untested in this domain and have failed in health-functioning data.
- No published study combining quasi-signal detection, intent–impact matching and vignette calibration was found.

## Error management theory as a formal account of biased intention reading: evidence and limits

### Takeaway
Error management theory (EMT) predicts biases in mind-reading where false-positive and false-negative errors had asymmetric costs over evolutionary history, e.g., men's sexual-intent overperception and women's commitment skepticism. Two core findings replicate in several samples, including face-to-face stranger dyads and Germany. However:
- the "overperception" benchmark is contested (women may understate their own intentions);
- error management may operate on *behaviour* rather than *beliefs*;
- the theory addresses mating inferences, not ongoing caregiving.

Using EMT to explain why receivers under-read care is a **theoretical extrapolation**. Grade **[M]** for the core courtship biases; **[T/SPEC]** for caregiving.

### Cited Findings
- [M] **Haselton & Buss (2000).** Study 1 (N = 217) documented women's under-perception of men's commitment. Study 2 (N = 289) replicated it and found a condition in which men's sexual over-perception was corrected. US students. Causal ID: survey and vignette comparisons. — [Haselton & Buss 2000, JPSP](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.1.81)
- [M] **Henningsen & Henningsen (2010).** 111 face-to-face man–woman stranger dyads. Support for EMT, with boundary conditions. Women's perceptions of men's commitment desire were related to the women's *own* sexual interest. There was evidence that people *project* their own interest onto partners. US. — [Henningsen & Henningsen 2010, Hum Commun Res](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2010.01391.x)
- [M] **Critique: Perilloux & Kurzban (2015).** Three studies.
  - Replicated the sex difference.
  - Men kept their ratings when incentivised for accuracy.
  - Women believed other women understate their sexual intentions.

  The authors argue men may be *accurate*, managing errors via biased *behaviour* rather than biased beliefs. — [Perilloux & Kurzban 2015, Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614555727)
- [M] **Reply: Murray, Murphy, von Hippel, Trivers & Haselton (2017).** A preregistered test of competing predictions, concluding (per title) that men do overestimate women's sexual intent. Abstract not retrieved. — [Murray et al. 2017, Psychol Sci](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797616675474)
- [P] **Cyrus, Schwarz & Hassebrauck (2011).** German study framing women's commitment skepticism as a life-history strategy. Title verified; findings not checked here. — [Cyrus et al. 2011, Evol Hum Behav](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2010.07.006)
- [T] **McKay & Efferson (2010).** Theoretical critique of EMT's subtleties. Abstract not retrieved; argument not checked here, verify before citing. — [McKay & Efferson 2010, Evol Hum Behav](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.evolhumbehav.2010.04.005)

#### Instruments
- **Perceived-intent rating paradigms** (vignettes or face-to-face interactions in which perceivers rate the target's sexual or commitment intent, compared with the target's self-report). — [Haselton & Buss 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.1.81); [Henningsen & Henningsen 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1468-2958.2010.01391.x)
- Korean validations: none located.

### Inferences
- [SPEC] An EMT-style account of care could run either way.
  - Receivers might be biased toward *under*-perceiving care when wrongly believing oneself cared for is costly, e.g., in exploitation-prone contexts. This parallels commitment skepticism.
  - Givers might be biased toward *over*-perceiving the adequacy of their care if under-investing was historically costly.

  No study located tests EMT for caregiving, so this is theoretical only.
- The benchmark problem in the Perilloux–Murray exchange applies directly to the care question. If B's "40" is a *strategic understatement*, or A's "90" a strategic overstatement, then "accuracy" against self-report is undefined. That argues for behavioural or observer benchmarks and incentive-compatible reports.
- Henningsen's projection finding again points to assumed similarity as a competing mechanism.

### Gaps
- No direct test of EMT predictions about perceiving care or support in established couples or parent–child dyads was found.
- McKay & Efferson's and Cyrus et al.'s arguments and findings need primary-text verification.
