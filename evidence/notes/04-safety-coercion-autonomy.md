# Safety, coercion, autonomy, boundaries and power: the dark side and boundary conditions of care (stress-test cases B–F)

Reading key (applies throughout):
- **Evidence grades:** **[Established]** = convergent meta-analytic or multi-lab evidence; **[Moderate/mixed]** = several studies or one strong design, or a meta-analysis with important caveats or conflicting results; **[Preliminary]** = single study, small or convenience samples, unreplicated; **[Theoretical]** = theoretical proposition or conceptual review; **[Speculative extrapolation]** = my own inference beyond the data.
- **Causal identification:** *none* (cross-sectional); *weak* (prospective observational); *moderate* (cross-lagged panel, within-person diary, quasi-experimental, or experiments on judgments only); *strong* (randomized trial of the real-world process).
- **WEIRD flag:** marks samples drawn only from Western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic populations (US, Canada, Western Europe, Australia/NZ). Israel is noted separately.
- **Verification:** every DOI cited was resolved in this session (Nov 2026) through OpenAlex or Crossref (title, authors, venue, year, volume/pages) and/or PubMed. Abstracts were read for nearly all items; full texts were generally **not** read, so effect sizes come from abstracts or meta-analytic summaries. Items whose content I could not read are labelled "title-level only". Items I could not resolve are labelled UNVERIFIED. The candidate-DOI verification log is the last section.
- **Measured vs inferred:** for key studies I separate what was measured from what the authors inferred.

## Case C — Love and abuse: how can people feel intensely loved (and loving) while experiencing abuse or coercion, and what does this imply for using perceived love or perceived responsiveness as an indicator of relationship health?

### Takeaway
Attachment, commitment and attributions of "love" routinely coexist with abuse. Several mechanisms drive this: commitment that pushes people to read aggression as benign, dependence on the partner and lack of alternatives, intermittent abuse, prior vulnerability, and cultural scripts that code jealousy and control as love. Perceived love or responsiveness is therefore not a valid indicator of relationship safety; in abusive relationships it is partly *produced by* the commitment, dependence and abuse dynamics themselves. Disconfirming caveats: the classic traumatic-bonding evidence is small, dated and mechanistically under-tested; staying is at least as well explained by structural dependence as by love; and Johnson's typology is better supported as a severity and control continuum than as discrete types.

### Cited Findings

#### C1. Traumatic bonding (attachment produced by intermittency and power imbalance)
- **[Preliminary as a mechanism; the phenomenon of post-separation attachment is Moderate]** Dutton, D. G., & Painter, S. (1993). Emotional attachments in abusive relationships: A test of traumatic bonding theory. *Violence and Victims, 8*(2), 105–120. Design: short-term prospective interviews; N = 75 women who had recently left abusive relationships (Canada; WEIRD). *Measured:* attachment to the ex-partner, abuse severity, intermittency of abuse, power differential. *Result:* relationship variables (total abuse, intermittency, power differential) accounted for 55% of the variance in attachment at Time 2; intermittency and changes in the power balance were the strongest predictors. *Inferred by authors:* a traumatic-bonding mechanism, which was not directly tested (no comparison group). The follow-up interval is given as about 6 months in secondary sources; I did not verify it. Causal ID: weak. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1891/0886-6708.8.2.105); [NCJRS abstract](https://www.ojp.gov/ncjrs/virtual-library/abstracts/emotional-attachments-abusive-relationships-test-traumatic-bonding)
- **[Preliminary]** Shaughnessy, E. V., Simons, R. M., Simons, J. S., & Freeman, H. (2023). Risk factors for traumatic bonding and associations with PTSD symptoms: A moderated mediation. *Child Abuse & Neglect, 144*, 106390. Cross-sectional path model; N = 354 "high-risk" participants currently in abusive relationships (country not stated in abstract). Childhood maltreatment and attachment insecurity predicted traumatic bonding **over and above romantic love**, age and gender. Attachment insecurity amplified the maltreatment→bonding link, and bonding correlated with PTSD symptoms. Causal ID: none. Implication: bonding partly reflects pre-existing vulnerability, not only relationship dynamics. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chiabu.2023.106390)
- **[Theoretical; critique of the construct]** Bailey, R., Dugard, J., Smith, S. F., & Porges, S. W. (2023). Appeasement: replacing Stockholm syndrome as a definition of a survival strategy. *European Journal of Psychotraumatology, 14*(1), 2161038. The authors state that "scarce empirical research" supports the Stockholm-syndrome or traumatic-bonding "positive bond" claim. They propose "appeasement" (calming a dangerous perpetrator to survive) as the better account of apparent emotional connection. Implication for the paper: affectionate displays toward an abuser may be **safety behaviours**, i.e., neither love "given" nor love "received" in the ordinary sense. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2022.2161038)
- **[Preliminary; qualitative; title-level only]** Casassa, Knight & Mengo (2022), a scoping review of trauma bonding in sex trafficking in *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse* — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838020985542). Casassa, Ploss & Karandikar (2024), service-provider interviews titled "'He loves me hard and then he abuses me hard'" in *Violence Against Women* — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/10778012231158104). The title itself encodes the alternation of intense affection and abuse; I verified titles and venues but did not review the content.

#### C2. Why people stay: commitment, investment and structural dependence (alternative explanation to "love")
- **[Moderate]** Rusbult, C. E., & Martz, J. M. (1995). Remaining in an abusive relationship: An investment model analysis of nonvoluntary dependence. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 21*(6), 558–571. Design: intake interviews at a shelter for battered women, with follow-up on whether women returned to their partners (US; WEIRD; N commonly cited as 100, not verified). *Measured:* commitment, quality of economic alternatives, investments (e.g., being married), dissatisfaction (abuse severity), stay/leave behaviour. *Result:* commitment was higher with poorer economic alternatives, greater investments and **less** dissatisfaction (less severe abuse). Commitment distinguished women who returned to partners immediately after leaving the shelter. *Inferred:* commitment mediates the effects of alternatives, investments and satisfaction ("causal modeling analyses suggested"). Causal ID: weak. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167295216002)
- **[Established for relationships in general]** Le, B., & Agnew, C. R. (2003). Commitment and its theorized determinants: A meta-analysis of the Investment Model. *Personal Relationships, 10*(1), 37–57. 52 studies, 60 samples, N = 11,582. Satisfaction, alternatives and investments together explain nearly two-thirds of the variance in commitment, and commitment predicts breakup. Associations varied minimally by demographic factors such as ethnicity (sample composition not reviewed). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1475-6811.00035)
- **[Moderate]** Edwards, K. M., Gidycz, C. A., & Murphy, M. J. (2011). College women's stay/leave decisions in abusive dating relationships: A prospective analysis of an expanded investment model. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence, 26*(7), 1446–1462. Prospective over 10 weeks; N = 323 US college women (WEIRD). Investment-model variables plus childhood abuse, distress, avoidance coping and self-esteem predicted leaving (good path-model fit). Causal ID: weak. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260510369131)
- **[Moderate; narrative review]** Anderson, D. K., & Saunders, D. G. (2003). Leaving an abusive partner: An empirical review of predictors, the process of leaving, and psychological well-being. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse, 4*(2), 163–191. Leaving is predicted by both material and psychological factors. It is a **process** with several cognitive shifts before leaving permanently, and some women who have just left have greater psychological difficulty than those still in the relationship. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838002250769)
- **[Moderate; narrative review]** Bornstein, R. F. (2006). The complex relationship between dependency and domestic violence: Converging psychological factors and social forces. *American Psychologist, 61*(6), 595–606. Economic dependency in women and emotional dependency in men independently contribute to abuse risk. High emotional dependency in the abused partner may reduce the likelihood of ending the relationship. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.61.6.595)
- **[Title-level only; results not retrieved]** Strube & Barbour (1983), *The decision to leave an abusive relationship: Economic dependence and psychological commitment*, *J. Marriage & Family, 45*(4), 785–793 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/351791). Rhatigan & Axsom (2006), *Using the investment model to understand battered women's commitment to abusive relationships*, *J. Family Violence, 21*(2), 153–162 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10896-005-9013-z). Kalmuss & Straus (1982), *Wife's marital dependency and wife abuse*, *J. Marriage & Family, 44*(2), 277 (first page) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/351538).
- **[Theoretical / qualitative meta-synthesis]** Kearney, M. H. (2001). Enduring love: A grounded formal theory of women's experience of domestic violence. *Research in Nursing & Health, 24*(4), 270–282. 13 qualitative reports; 282 ethnically and geographically diverse women aged 16–67. The core process is "enduring love". Women first **discount early violence for the sake of romantic commitment** ("This is what I wanted"). They then endure increasingly unpredictable violence through careful monitoring of the partner and stifling of the self ("The more I do, the worse I am"), later redefine the situation as unacceptable, and finally leave. This happens in cultural contexts that "normalized relationship violence while promoting idealized romance." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/nur.1029)

#### C3. Commitment biases how the receiver perceives aggression (the perception stage is motivated)
- **[Preliminary → Moderate]** Arriaga, X. B. (2002). Joking violence among highly committed individuals. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence, 17*(6), 591–610. N = 54 people whose partner had used physical violence during conflict (US; WEIRD). *Measured:* the same violent acts rated in a "conflict" context and in a "joking around" context, plus commitment. *Result:* only highly committed people who had experienced substantial violence reported severely violent acts as mere "joking around". *Inferred:* cognitive-consistency-driven reinterpretation. Causal ID: none. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260502017006001)
- **[Moderate — the strongest causal evidence in Case C]** Arriaga, X. B., Capezza, N. M., & Daly, C. (2016; online 2015). Personal standards for judging aggression by a relationship partner: How much aggression is too much? *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 110*(1), 36–54. Studies 1a/1b (n = 689): experiencing aggression in the current relationship and greater commitment predicted greater tolerance of common partner aggression. Study 2 (longitudinal; n = 52 never-aggressed at baseline): **once aggression occurred, people adopted more tolerant standards, but only if highly committed.** Study 3 (experiment; n = 73 with current partner aggression): people were more tolerant of acts imagined from their partner than of the same acts by a stranger, again only if highly committed. US (WEIRD). Causal ID: moderate (longitudinal plus experimental manipulation of target relevance). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000035)
- **[Preliminary → Moderate]** Arriaga, X. B., Capezza, N. M., Goodfriend, W., Rayl, E. S., & Sands, K. J. (2013). Individual well-being and relationship maintenance at odds: The unexpected perils of maintaining a relationship with an aggressive partner. *Social Psychological and Personality Science, 4*(6), 676–684. Longitudinal; n = 171 with current partner aggression (US; WEIRD). Committed individuals **overestimated** how unhappy a breakup would make them. Those with high partner aggression became **happier** without the partner than they had forecast, and forecasting unhappiness predicted staying. Authors: bias occurs "not only in forecasting future happiness but also in misreading how badly one feels now." Causal ID: weak. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550613480822)
- **[Preliminary]** Gordon, K. C., Burton, S., & Porter, L. (2004). Predicting the intentions of women in domestic violence shelters to return to partners: Does forgiveness play a role? *Journal of Family Psychology, 18*(2), 331–338. N = 121 women in urban and rural US shelters (WEIRD). Forgiveness of the partner predicted intention to return **over and above** violence severity, attributions and investment. *Measured:* intention, not actual return. Causal ID: none. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.18.2.331)

#### C4. Cultural scripts that read jealousy, violence or control as love
- **[Moderate for judgments; experimental vignettes]** Puente, S., & Cohen, D. (2003). Jealousy and the meaning (or nonmeaning) of violence. *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 29*(4), 449–460. Three studies with North American participants (WEIRD). Jealousy was believed to signal both insecurity and love (S1). Equating jealousy with love led to tacit acceptance of jealousy-related violence (S2), extending to emotional and sexual abuse (S3). **Men who hit or abused their wives over jealousy were judged to romantically love their wives as much as men who did not abuse.** Violence in non-jealousy arguments was judged quite negatively, but much of that negativity disappeared in the jealousy case. *Measured:* third-party judgments, not victims' perceptions or behaviour. Causal ID: moderate for judgments only. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167202250912)
- **[Moderate]** Vandello, J. A., & Cohen, D. (2003). Male honor and female fidelity: Implicit cultural scripts that perpetuate domestic violence. *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 84*(5), 997–1010. Study 1: Brazil (honour culture) vs US vignettes. Study 2: southern Anglo, Latino and northern Anglo participants witnessing a staged aggression against a female confederate. Female infidelity damages a man's reputation, especially in honour cultures; violence partially restores it; and women are expected to stay loyal despite jealousy-related violence. Partly non-WEIRD (Brazil). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.5.997)
- **[Moderate]** Vandello, J. A., Cohen, D., Grandon, R., & Franiuk, R. (2009; online 2008). Stand by your man: Indirect prescriptions for honorable violence and feminine loyalty in Canada, Chile, and the United States. *Journal of Cross-Cultural Psychology, 40*(1), 81–104. Honour-culture participants (Latinos and southern Anglos; Chileans) were relatively more favourable to a woman who **stays** in an abusive relationship. Chileans rated a husband more positively than Canadians did when the violent conflict was jealousy-related, but not when it was about money. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022108326194)
- **[Moderate; observational systematic review]** Pichon, M., Treves-Kagan, S., Stern, E., Kyegombe, N., Stöckl, H., et al. (2020). A mixed-methods systematic review: Infidelity, romantic jealousy and intimate partner violence against women. *International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 17*(16), 5682. 51 papers from 28 countries (2009–2019). Real or suspected infidelity and romantic jealousy were consistently associated with IPV. Pathways support theories of patriarchal culture, threatened masculinities and poor emotion regulation, "but not evolutionary theories." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/ijerph17165682)
- **[Preliminary; measure development]** Lara, L., & Gómez-Urrutia, V. (2021). Development and validation of the Romantic Love Myths Questionnaire. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence, 36*(21–22), NP12342–NP12359. Chile; N = 1,538 students aged 14–24; 10 items in two factors, **"idealization" and "love-abuse"**, correlated with dating violence. Males endorsed love-abuse more; adolescents scored higher than emerging adults. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260519892958)
- **[Title-level only]** Papp, L. J., Liss, M., Erchull, M. J., Godfrey, H., & Waaland-Kreutzer, L. (2017; online 2016). The dark side of heterosexual romance: Endorsement of romantic beliefs relates to intimate partner violence. *Sex Roles, 76*(1–2), 99–109. Abstract not retrieved. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-016-0668-0)

#### C5. Coercive control and Johnson's typology
- **[Theoretical; highly influential]** Stark, E. (2007). *Coercive control: How men entrap women in personal life.* Oxford University Press. Argues that coercive control (isolation, intimidation and control, possibly with no physical violence) is the more prevalent and devastating form of domestic abuse, and is invisible to incident-focused interventions. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/oso/9780195154276.001.0001)
- **[Moderate]** Johnson, M. P. (1995). Patriarchal terrorism and common couple violence: Two forms of violence against women. *Journal of Marriage and the Family, 57*(2), 283–294 (end page from standard citation). Reconciles gender-symmetric survey data with shelter data by distinguishing occasional "common couple violence" from systematic male "patriarchal terrorism". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/353683). Johnson, M. P., & Leone, J. M. (2005). The differential effects of intimate terrorism and situational couple violence: Findings from the National Violence Against Women Survey. *Journal of Family Issues, 26*(3), 322–349. US national survey (WEIRD). Victims of intimate terrorism were attacked more often, with violence less likely to stop, and had more injury, PTSD symptoms, painkiller use and missed work. They had left more often. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0192513X04270345)
- **[Moderate]** Graham-Kevan, N., & Archer, J. (2003). Intimate terrorism and common couple violence: A test of Johnson's predictions in four British samples. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence, 18*(11), 1247–1270. UK samples: shelter women and partners (86), students (208), treatment programme (8), prisoners and partners (192). Cluster analysis gave "broad support". — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0886260503256656)
- **[Moderate; nationally representative]** Myhill, A. (2015). Measuring coercive control: What can we learn from national population surveys? *Violence Against Women, 21*(3), 355–375. Crime Survey for England & Wales. Situational violence was "fairly symmetrical" by gender, while coercive controlling abuse was "highly gendered, with women overwhelmingly the victims." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077801214568032)
- **[Moderate; non-WEIRD replication]** Tiwari, A., Chan, K. L., Cheung, D. S., Fong, D. Y., Yan, E. C., et al. (2015). The differential effects of intimate terrorism and situational couple violence on mental health outcomes among abused Chinese women: A mixed-method study. *BMC Public Health, 15*, 314. Hong Kong; N = 613 women with past-year IPV: 35.1% classified as intimate terrorism, 52.9% as situational couple violence. IT victims had more injury, medical use, depression and PTSD (all p < .001). Cross-sectional. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-015-1649-x)
- **[Moderate; methods]** Hardesty, J. L., Crossman, K. A., Haselschwerdt, M. L., Raffaelli, M., Ogolsky, B. G., & Johnson, M. P. (2015). Toward a standard approach to operationalizing coercive control and classifying violence types. *Journal of Marriage and Family, 77*(4), 833–843. N = 190 divorcing mothers (US). Measuring coercive control by **frequency** works better than counting tactics. The authors propose a cutoff on the PMWI Dominance–Isolation subscale, and the resulting types relate to fear, perceived threat and post-separation violence. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jomf.12201)
- **[Moderate; disconfirming of discrete types]** Love, H. A., Spencer, C. M., May, S. A., Mendez, M., & Stith, S. M. (2020). Perpetrator risk markers for intimate terrorism and situational couple violence: A meta-analysis. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse, 21*(5), 922–931. 149 studies, 216 effect sizes. IT-type markers (control, power, jealousy, patriarchal beliefs) were stronger in clinical than community samples. The authors conclude the typology "may be conceptualized as representing a **continuum** of violence." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1524838018801331)
- **[Moderate; measurement instability]** Eckstein, J. J. (2017). Intimate terrorism and situational couple violence: Classification variability across five methods to distinguish Johnson's violent relationship types. *Violence and Victims, 32*(6), 955–976. N = 840 (community and agency sampling). Five classification methods (two hierarchical-clustering variants, vignette choice, cutoff scoring, expert coding) produced variable groupings. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1891/0886-6708.VV-D-16-00022)
- **[Preliminary]** Conroy, N. E., & Crowley, C. G. (2022). Extending Johnson's typology: Additional manifestations of dating violence and coercive control. *Journal of Interpersonal Violence, 37*(15–16), NP13315–NP13341. N = 398 US young adults aged 18–27 (WEIRD). 35% of reported relationships were abusive, and **11% involved nonviolent coercive control**. Victims of IT and of nonviolent coercive control were more fearful than SCV victims, while SCV victims did not differ in fear from respondents in non-abusive relationships. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/08862605211005149)
- **[Established that the construct is inconsistently defined]** Hamberger, L. K., Larsen, S. E., & Lehrner, A. (2017). Coercive control in intimate partner violence. *Aggression and Violent Behavior, 37*, 1–11. Review notes "widespread inconsistency" in how coercive control is defined and measured. A Monash University research brief reports that the review identified **22 different definitions**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.avb.2017.08.003); [Monash brief](https://bridges.monash.edu/articles/online_resource/Criminalisation_of_Coercive_Control_-_Research_Brief/13017743)
- **[Moderate]** Lohmann, S., Cowlishaw, S., Ney, L., O'Donnell, M., & Felmingham, K. (2024). The trauma and mental health impacts of coercive control: A systematic review and meta-analysis. *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse, 25*(1), 630–647. 68 studies identified, 45 meta-analysed (observational). Coercive control was associated with PTSD at r = .32 [.28, .37] and depression at r = .27 [.22, .31]. These are **comparable to** broader psychological IPV (PTSD r = .34 [.25, .42]; depression r = .33 [.26, .40]). So in these correlates, coercive control is not shown to be uniquely more harmful than broadly measured psychological abuse. Causal ID: none. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/15248380231162972)
- **[Title-level only]** Dutton, M. A., & Goodman, L. A. (2005). Coercion in intimate partner violence: Toward a new conceptualization. *Sex Roles, 52*(11–12), 743–756. Abstract not retrieved. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11199-005-4196-6)

#### C6. Global prevalence context (WHO)
- **[Established]** Sardinha, L., Maheu-Giroux, M., Stöckl, H., Meyer, S. R., & García-Moreno, C. (2022). Global, regional, and national prevalence estimates of physical or sexual, or both, intimate partner violence against women in 2018. *The Lancet, 399*(10327), 803–813. 366 studies, about 2 million women, 161 countries, Bayesian multilevel model. Lifetime physical and/or sexual IPV was 27% (UI 23–31%) among ever-partnered women aged 15–49, and past-year IPV 13% (10–16%). Already 24% of 15–19-year-olds were affected. Estimates are **act-based physical/sexual violence only** (no psychological abuse or coercive control). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736%2821%2902664-7)
- **[Established]** WHO (2025). *Violence against women prevalence estimates, 2023* (released 19–20 Nov 2025; 168 countries/areas). Lifetime physical and/or sexual IPV was 25.8% among ever-partnered women aged 15–49 and 24.7% among those aged 15+. Lifetime IPV and/or non-partner sexual violence was **31.6% (~840 million women)**. WHO-linked coverage reports past-year IPV of about **316 million women (11%)**, with Oceania (excluding Australia/NZ) at 38% and an average decline of only ~0.2% per year. WHO notes true prevalence is "likely to be much higher" because of stigma. — [WHO fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/violence-against-women); [WHO report page](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240116962); [coverage of past-year figures](https://healthpolicy-watch.news/840-million-women-have-faced-violence-during-their-lifetime-who-estimates/)

#### C7. Measurement instruments (Case C), with cross-cultural/Korean notes
- **Revised Conflict Tactics Scales (CTS2):** Straus, M. A., Hamby, S. L., Boney-McCoy, S., & Sugarman, D. B. (1996). *Journal of Family Issues, 17*(3), 283–316. Scales: psychological aggression, physical assault, sexual coercion, injury, negotiation; reliability .79–.95 with "preliminary evidence of construct validity." It is act-based and context-free: it does not capture motive, control pattern or fear (the measurement point behind Johnson 1995 and Myhill 2015). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/019251396017003001). **Korean:** a CTS2 study of Korean couples by Sohn Jeong Young (husbands' tactics and wife abuse) reported subscale reliabilities of .64–.94 and total .90, with preliminary construct validity; sample size not in the abstract — [KISS record](https://kiss.kstudy.com/Detail/Ar?key=219167). A child-report adaptation tested on N = 288 Korean university students reported α .83–.98 (Baek, 1998, *J. Korean Home Economics Association*) — [KoreaScience](https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO199811919396955.pub?lang=en).
- **Checklist of Controlling Behaviors (CCB):** Lehmann, P., Simmons, C. A., & Pillai, V. K. (2012). *Violence Against Women, 18*(8), 913–933. 84 items in 10 subscales: physical, sexual, male privilege, isolation, minimizing/denying, blaming, intimidation, threats, emotional, economic. Validated only with N = 2,135 US shelter residents (selected, WEIRD). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077801212456522)
- **Psychological Maltreatment of Women Inventory (PMWI):** Tolman, R. M. (1989). *Violence and Victims, 4*(3), 159–177. 58 items with dominance–isolation and emotional–verbal factors; 407 men and 207 women at DV-programme intake. Partners' agreement was low, though dominance–isolation scores correlated within couples. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1891/0886-6708.4.3.159). Validation study: Tolman (1999), *Violence and Victims, 14*(1), 25–37 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1891/0886-6708.14.1.25). A classification cutoff on the Dominance–Isolation subscale is given in Hardesty et al. (2015).
- **Composite Abuse Scale (CAS) and CAS(R)-SF:** Hegarty, Sheehan & Schonfeld (1999), *Journal of Family Violence, 14*(4), 399–415 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1022834215681). Ford-Gilboe, M., et al. (2016), *BMJ Open, 6*(12), e012824: 15 items across physical, sexual and psychological domains; α = .942; pooled N = 6,278 Canadian women; correlates moderately with depression, PTSD and coercive control — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2016-012824).
- **Economic abuse:** Scale of Economic Abuse (Adams, Sullivan, Bybee & Greeson, 2008, *Violence Against Women, 14*(5), 563–588; N = 103 survivors) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077801208315529). SEA-12 short form (Postmus, Plummer & Stylianou, 2016, *VAW, 22*(6), 692–703; N = 120) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/1077801215610012).
- **Newer coercive-control instruments (2023–2026; Preliminary):** Legal Abuse Scale (Gutowski & Goodman, 2023, *J. Family Violence*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10896-022-00408-3); French validation of the Coercive Control Screen with clinical thresholds (2026, *Eur. J. Psychotraumatology*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/20008066.2026.2694186); Immigration-Related Coercive Control Scale (2026) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10903-026-02007-6). Titles and venues verified via PubMed; psychometrics not reviewed.
- **Systematic evaluation of IPV measures:** Alexander, E. F., Backes, B. L., & Johnson, M. D. (2022). *Trauma, Violence, & Abuse, 23*(5), 1549–1567. 222 studies on 87 measures, rated against COSMIN-revised criteria. Trauma Bonding Scale for Adults: validated in US young adults and in Kenya (N = 538 aged 18–29; Chenneville, Reid, Wasilewski & Adeli, 2024, *JIV, 39*(9–10), 2148–2164) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/08862605231215015).
- **Romantic love myths:** RLMQ (Chile; Lara & Gómez-Urrutia, 2021, above); brief Scale of Myths of Romantic Love (Peru; 2025, *J. Marital & Family Therapy*) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jmft.12741).
- **Korean validations of coercive-control, trauma-bonding, CCB, PMWI or romantic-myth scales:** none found (see Gaps).

### Inferences
- **Perceived love lacks discriminant validity for abuse** (well grounded). Third parties attribute as much love to jealous abusers as to non-abusers (Puente & Cohen 2003). Victims' commitment shifts their standards toward tolerance once aggression begins (Arriaga et al. 2016) and reframes violence as play (Arriaga 2002). People in aggressive relationships also misread their own current and future affect (Arriaga et al. 2013). Perceived love and commitment are thus biased upward in exactly the relationships where safety is worst. A model that treats "love perceived" or "responsiveness perceived" as the endpoint will score some abusive relationships as high-functioning.
- **The perception stage is endogenous, not just noisy.** Perceived love is partly produced by the abuse dynamic (intermittency and power imbalance; Dutton & Painter 1993) and by dependence (poor alternatives raise commitment; Rusbult & Martz 1995). Reverse causation and confounding are therefore built in. "Love perceived" cannot serve as an independent outcome when safety is in question.
- **"Translation" can run the wrong way.** In Case C the typical error is *over*-attribution of love (aggression read as jealousy-as-love, joking or devotion), not under-reception of care. This is a different failure mode from the hypothesis' "care lost in translation". It is better described as **capture** of the perception channel by commitment, dependence and cultural scripts. [Inference]
- **Competing explanation to the hypothesis.** Staying and attachment are well predicted by structural dependence (alternatives, investments, economic dependency) and individual vulnerability (childhood maltreatment, attachment insecurity), with no "translation" step needed (Rusbult & Martz 1995; Bornstein 2006; Shaughnessy et al. 2023). Abuse is therefore a **boundary condition** that the translation hypothesis should explicitly exclude or model separately. Framing abuse as a translation failure risks implying shared responsibility for the harm.
- **Safety needs its own measurement**, independent of love and responsiveness scales: act-based violence (CTS2/CAS), pattern-based control (PMWI dominance–isolation, CCB, SEA-12), and **fear/threat**. Fear distinguished intimate terrorism and nonviolent coercive control from situational violence (Conroy & Crowley 2022; Hardesty et al. 2015). [Inference for modelling]
- **Evidence-quality caveat.** Mechanistic claims (traumatic bonding, motivated reinterpretation) rest on small, mostly US/Canadian samples with cross-sectional or short prospective designs. Arriaga et al. (2016) is the main exception, with longitudinal plus experimental evidence. Prevalence (WHO) is established, but WHO figures exclude psychological abuse and coercive control, so they understate the coercion relevant to the paper.

### Gaps
- **No study found that measured perceived partner responsiveness (e.g., the PPR scale) inside abusive or coercive relationships**, i.e., whether victims rate abusive partners as responsive, or how PPR changes as coercion escalates. A PubMed search (perceived partner responsiveness × aggression/violence/abuse) returned only two unrelated records. This is the most important empirical gap for the paper.
- Rusbult & Martz's N and Dutton & Painter's follow-up interval could not be verified from full text. Strube & Barbour (1983), Rhatigan & Axsom (2006), Kalmuss & Straus (1982), Papp et al. (2017) and Dutton & Goodman (2005) are cited at title level only.
- Hamberger et al. (2017): I recall the review proposing defining facets of coercive control that include the target's *negative perception* of the controlling behaviour, but I could **not** verify this from the abstract (UNVERIFIED). If correct, any definition that requires the target to perceive control negatively would miss control the target construes as love — exactly the Case C problem. Check the full text.
- No Korean validations found for coercive-control measures, trauma-bonding scales, the CCB, the PMWI or romantic-myth scales. The Korean CTS2 validation's sample details were unavailable.
- Global prevalence of coercive control or psychological IPV is not covered by WHO estimates. Gender-asymmetry claims depend on the measure (Myhill 2015).
- Johnson-type classification is unstable across methods (Eckstein 2017), and whether any instrument validly separates the types remains unresolved (Alexander et al. 2022 aim; result not retrieved).

## Case B — Manipulative responsiveness: can the perception of responsiveness and emotional understanding be produced, or used, without benevolent motives?

### Takeaway
Probably yes for warmth and liking signals, and plausibly for responsiveness. Self-interested actors are liked at first sight, and the most exploitative facets of narcissism are the most attractive at zero acquaintance. Ingratiation reliably raises liking. Emotion-regulation knowledge amplifies Machiavellians' interpersonal deviance. Psychopathic traits predict *more accurate* detection of others' vulnerability. A subgroup of people combine dark traits with high empathy ("dark empaths"). However, there is **no direct evidence** that perceived partner responsiveness itself is manufactured in established intimate relationships. "Love bombing" has essentially no peer-reviewed evidence base. And the meta-analytic picture of dark-triad empathy is mixed: narcissism leaves cognitive empathy intact, but psychopathy shows cognitive-empathy deficits too.

### Inferences
- **Answer to the key question.** Perceptions of warmth, charm and liking can demonstrably be produced by self-interested actors (Back et al. 2010; Paulhus 1998; Gordon 1996). The skills that underlie responsiveness (emotion knowledge, cognitive empathy, vulnerability detection) are motive-neutral amplifiers (Côté et al. 2011; Wheeler et al. 2009). The specific claim that *perceived partner responsiveness* in committed relationships can be manufactured without benevolent motives is therefore plausible but **not directly tested** [Preliminary / Speculative extrapolation].
- **Time course matters.** Manufactured positivity is strongest at first acquaintance and decays (Paulhus 1998; Leckelt et al. 2015). A single early measurement of perceived responsiveness is the most exploitable point; repeated, behaviour-anchored assessment is more robust. [Speculative extrapolation to romantic relationships]
- **Responsiveness is a means, not a motive.** The same capacities predict prosocial or exploitative outcomes depending on the actor's goals. A care model therefore needs **actor-motive variables** (e.g., communal vs self-oriented goals) that are independent of the receiver's perceived responsiveness. Kanat-Maymon et al. (2016) show that even sincere affection, when made contingent, functions as control.
- **Receivers are not passive.** Lemay & Clark (2008) show that perceivers discount regard they think is non-genuine, and Gordon (1996) shows that transparency weakens ingratiation. Capture is probabilistic and depends on the receiver's inferences about motive, which are themselves shaped by insecurity and dependence. This is consistent with, not contrary to, a "translation" framing, but it adds a *motive-inference* stage.
- **Popular constructs outrun evidence.** "Love bombing" and the "narcissistic abuser" are culturally salient, but the evidence is weak (one student-journal study) or small (narcissism–IPV r = .15, null for physical IPV). The paper should not lean on these constructs.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal or experimental study found that tracks perceived partner responsiveness in relationships with manipulative or coercive partners.
- No validated, peer-reviewed love-bombing measure, and no prospective data linking early "love bombing" to later abuse or coercive control.
- The dark-empath replication is unpublished, and cross-cultural (including Korean) data on dark empathy and emotional manipulation are lacking.
- Effect sizes for Côté et al. (2011) and Austin et al. (2007) were not retrieved; Wai & Tiliopoulos' abstract was read only through secondary summaries.

## Case D — Parental love plus autonomy violation: how can sincere parental (or romantic) love coexist with violations of autonomy, and does cultural normativeness remove, reduce or not affect the harm?

### Takeaway
Psychological control is intrusive, guilt-inducing, love-withdrawing control of the child's inner life. It is one of the most robustly replicated harmful parenting dimensions. Its association with youth ill-being (r ≈ .20–.27) **generalizes across world regions, national individualism and cultural hierarchy**, according to 2025–2026 meta-analyses with >100,000 children, including East Asian and Korean samples. Parental conditional regard is love given contingently, and it "works" for compliance at an affective cost. On cultural normativeness the answer is **"reduces, sometimes; does not remove"**: moderation, where found, runs mainly through the *child's own appraisal* (perceived normativeness, legitimacy, mattering vs intrusion). Disconfirming caveats:
- effects are modest and heterogeneous;
- they are mostly cross-sectional and youth-reported, so shared-method variance is likely;
- they are bidirectional, and child effects sometimes dominate;
- some intrusive involvement (helicopter parenting) co-occurs with closeness, and even benefits, in Korean and US samples.

Internalizing counterpart. *Marriage & Family Review, 53*(7), 613–640. 1,015 studies; psychological control was associated with internalizing, bidirectionally — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1080/01494929.2016.1247761).
- **[Moderate]** Soenens, B., Park, S.-Y., Vansteenkiste, M., & Mouratidis, A. (2012; online 2011). #### D3. Reverse causation and the size of parent effects (disconfirming / qualifying)

#### D4. Conditional regard: love made contingent
- **[Moderate]** Assor, A., Roth, G., & Deci, E. L. (2004; online 2003). The emotional costs of parents' conditional regard: A self-determination theory analysis. *Journal of Personality, 72*(1), 47–88. Israel.
  - Study 1 (three generations): mothers' reports of their own parents' conditional regard predicted the mothers' poorer well-being and more controlling attitudes, and their daughters' perceptions of conditional regard (intergenerational transmission).
  - Study 2 (college students, 4 domains): perceived conditional regard related to introjection, behavioural enactment, self-esteem fluctuation, perceived disapproval and **resentment**; introjection mediated conditional regard → enactment.
  - Authors: conditional regard "can promote enactment of the desired behaviors but does so with significant affective costs."
  - Causal ID: none (retrospective reports). — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0022-3506.2004.00256.x)
- **[Moderate]** Roth, G., Assor, A., Niemiec, C. P., Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2009). Israeli 9th graders (n = 169; n = 156); multiple reporters.
  - Conditional negative regard → resentment → emotional dysregulation and academic disengagement.
  - **Conditional positive regard** → internal compulsion → suppressive regulation and grade-focused engagement.
  - Autonomy support → sense of choice → integrated regulation and interest-focused engagement.

- **[Moderate → Established (meta of correlational studies)]** Haines, J. E., & Schutte, N. S. (2023). Parental conditional regard: A meta-analysis. *Journal of Adolescence, 95*(2), 195–223. 31 samples. Conditional regard correlated with introjected regulation r = .33, contingent self-esteem r = .29, depressive symptoms r = .22, and relatedness r = −.24. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/jad.12111)
- Romantic extension: Kanat-Maymon et al. (2016), Case B4. **Korean validation of a conditional-regard scale: none found.**

#### D5. Overparenting / helicopter parenting: intrusive involvement that coexists with closeness
- **[Moderate]** Padilla-Walker, L. M., & Nelson, L. J. (2012). Black hawk down? N = 438 US undergraduates (WEIRD) plus at least one parent. Helicopter parenting loaded on a factor separate from behavioural and psychological control. - **[Moderate]** Segrin, C., Woszidlo, A., Givertz, M., Bauer, A., & Murphy, M. T. (2012). Overparenting was associated with lower-quality communication (and, indirectly, lower family satisfaction) and with **entitlement**; it was unrelated to adaptive traits. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3729.2011.00689.x)
- **[Preliminary]** LeMoyne, T., & Buchanan, T. (2011). Does "hovering" matter? Helicopter parenting and its effect on well-being. *Sociological Spectrum, 31*(4), 399–418. N = 317 US students. - **[Moderate]** Two meta-analyses of overparenting:
  - Zhang, Q., & Ji, W. (2023). *Development and Psychopathology* (online), 1–16. 52 articles. Overparenting correlated with depression r = .15 (k = 133), anxiety r = .14 (k = 101) and internalizing r = .19 (k = 58). Effects were "largely equal across SES groups, cultures," age, gender and design. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1017/S095457942300055X)
  - Hu, N., et al. (2025). *Behavioral Sciences, 15*(9), 1235. 44 studies, N = 21,607. Anxiety r = .16, depression r = .20. Culture, parent gender and developmental stage explained heterogeneity. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/bs15091235)
- **[Preliminary; Korean samples — mixed and partly benign]**
  - Lee, J., & Kang, S. (2018). *Journal of Child and Family Studies, 27*(11), 3672–3686. N = 562 Korean emerging adults aged 19–34. Helicopter parenting was **directly** associated with more depressive symptoms and indirectly with worse adjustment via pressure from parental career expectations. But it was also **indirectly associated with better adjustment via parent–child affection**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-018-1193-2)
  - Hwang, W., Jung, E., Kim, S., & Hadi, N. (2023). *Frontiers in Psychology, 14*, 1097348. US (433 mother-child, 401 father-child groups) and South Korea (207 and 195).   - Kwon, K.-A., Yoo, G., & Bingham, G. E. (2016). Helicopter parenting in emerging adulthood: Support or barrier for Korean college students' psychological adjustment? *JCFS, 25*(1), 136–145 (title-level only) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10826-015-0195-6).

#### D6. Legitimacy of parental authority by domain (social-domain theory) and children's appraisals
- **[Established framework]** Smetana, J. G., & Asquith, P. (1994). Adolescents' and parents' conceptions of parental authority and personal autonomy. *Child Development, 65*(4), 1147–1162 (end page from standard citation). N = 68 sixth, eighth and tenth graders plus parents (US). Both agreed that parents hold legitimate authority over **moral and conventional** issues. Parents treated multifaceted, friendship, prudential and **personal** issues as more subject to their authority than adolescents did, and personal-jurisdiction reasoning increased with age. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1131311)
- **[Moderate]** Smetana, J. G., & Daddis, C. (2002). Domain-specific antecedents of parental psychological control and monitoring: The role of parenting beliefs and practices. *Child Development, 73*(2), 563–580. 93 middle-class African American early adolescents and mothers, 2-year longitudinal. Adolescents who rated parents as more restrictive over **personal** issues, *and who believed parents had less legitimate authority there*, rated mothers higher in psychological control. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00424)
- **[Moderate; non-WEIRD replication]** Hasebe, Y., Nucci, L., & Nucci, M. S. (2004). Parental control of the personal domain and adolescent symptoms of psychopathology: A cross-national study in the United States and Japan. *Child Development, 75*(3), 815–828. US N = 170, **Japan N = 125**. In both countries, perceived parental control over **personal** issues correlated with internalizing symptoms; control over conventional or prudential issues did not. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2004.00708.x)
- **[Preliminary; experimental vignettes]** Kakihara, F., & Tilton-Weaver, L. (2009). Adolescents' interpretations of parental control: Differentiated by domain and types of control. *Child Development, 80*(6), 1722–1738. N = 67 adolescents (country not stated in abstract). Type, level and domain of control were manipulated. **At high levels, adolescents did not differentiate behavioural from psychological control: both were read as signalling less "mattering" to parents and more intrusiveness.** High control over personal-domain issues was read most negatively. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-8624.2009.01364.x)
- **[Moderate; cross-cultural judgments]** Helwig, C. C., To, S., Wang, Q., Liu, C., & Yang, S. (2014). #### D7. Chao's (1994) "training" argument and how later evidence evaluated it
- **[Theoretical proposition with descriptive support]** Chao, R. K. (1994). Beyond parental control and authoritarian parenting style: Understanding Chinese parenting through the cultural notion of training. *Child Development, 65*(4), 1111–1119 (end page from standard citation). Immigrant Chinese vs European-American mothers of preschoolers. *Measured:* mothers' self-reported parenting-style and control scores, plus "training" (*chiao shun / guan*) items. Chinese mothers scored higher on training after controlling for education and standard measures. *Inferred (not measured):* that training explains Chinese children's school success, and that authoritative/authoritarian categories are ethnocentric. No child outcomes were measured. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2307/1131308)
- **[Moderate; partial support for cultural moderation]** Chao, R. K. (2001). Extending research on the consequences of parenting style for Chinese Americans and European Americans. *Child Development, 72*(6), 1832–1843. More than 500 adolescents (148 first-generation and 176 second-generation Chinese; 208 European American). The positive effects of authoritative parenting and closeness on school performance held for European Americans, partly for second-generation Chinese, and **not for first-generation Chinese**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-8624.00381)
- **[Moderate; disconfirming the strong "training is beneficial" reading]** Kim, S. Y., Wang, Y., Orozco-Lapray, D., Shen, Y., & Murtuza, M. (2013; online 2012). Does "tiger parenting" exist? Parenting profiles of Chinese Americans and adolescent developmental outcomes. *Asian American Journal of Psychology, 4*(1), 7–18. 444 Chinese American families, three waves over 8 years. Four profiles emerged: supportive (most common, best outcomes), easygoing, tiger, harsh. **Compared with supportive parenting, tiger parenting was associated with lower GPA and educational attainment, less sense of family obligation, more academic pressure, more depressive symptoms and greater alienation.** Causal ID: weak. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0030612)
- **[Established (meta)]** Pinquart, M., & Kauser, R. (2018; online 2017). Do the associations of parenting styles with behavior problems and academic achievement vary by culture?   - Authoritarian parenting was associated with at least one negative outcome **in all regions**.
  - The authoritative–achievement association was weaker in Asian minorities in Western countries.
  - The authoritarian–achievement association was less negative in Hispanic families.
  - Associations of authoritarian parenting with outcomes were **weaker in countries with higher individualism** (as reported; this runs opposite to a simple "harm is lower where the practice is normative" prediction).
  - Authors: authoritarian parenting is "to some extent, tolerable in a few cultural contexts." — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/cdp0000149)
- **[Moderate; supports "meaning matters"]** Rudy, D., & Grusec, J. E. (2006). Authoritarian parenting in individualist and collectivist groups: Associations with maternal emotion and cognition and children's self-esteem. *Journal of Family Psychology, 20*(1), 68–78. Canada: individualist (Western European) vs collectivist (Egyptian, Iranian, Indian, Pakistani background) families with children aged 7–12. Collectivist mothers endorsed authoritarian parenting more but did not feel or think more negatively about their children, and their children were not lower in self-esteem. Authoritarianism was associated with maternal negative emotion and cognition **only in the individualist group**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.20.1.68)

- **[Moderate]** Lansford, J. E., Chang, L., Dodge, K. A., Malone, P. S., Oburu, P., Palmérus, K., et al. (2005). - **[Moderate → Established; no moderation by group]** Gershoff, E. T., Lansford, J. E., Sexton, H. R., Davis-Kean, P., & Sameroff, A. J. (2012). - **[Established]** Gershoff, E. T., & Grogan-Kaylor, A. (2016). ### Inferences
- **"Sincere love plus autonomy violation" is the modal case, not an exception.** Psychological control is linked to parental separation anxiety and perfectionism (Soenens et al. 2010). Mothers across four cultures frame control as care (Cho et al. 2021). Conditional regard *is* affection, deployed contingently (Assor et al. 2004; Roth et al. 2009). Intention to care therefore does not protect against harm. Care intent and autonomy respect behave as **separate dimensions**: in the largest meta-analysis, autonomy support and psychological control have distinct associations with outcomes even when each is controlled for the other (Bradshaw et al. 2025).
- **Cultural normativeness: reduces (sometimes), does not remove.** The evidence:
  - physical discipline is still associated with adverse outcomes regardless of normativeness (Lansford et al. 2005);
  - there is no ethnic moderation in US national data (Gershoff et al. 2012);
  - the strongest moderation runs through the *child's own* perceived normativeness (Gershoff et al. 2010; Liu & Wang 2018);
  - near-universal associations hold for psychological control across regions and individualism (Bradshaw 2025; Salaam & Kyere 2025; Ryan 2026).

  Children in China and Canada alike judge love withdrawal harmful (Helwig et al. 2014).
- **Where the translation hypothesis gains most support:** Case D is where appraisal measurably matters. Examples: domain legitimacy (Smetana & Daddis 2002; Hasebe 2004); "mattering" vs "intrusion" readings (Kakihara & Tilton-Weaver 2009); perceived normativeness (Liu & Wang 2018); and the different emotional meaning of authoritarianism (Rudy & Grusec 2006). But appraisal **attenuates, it does not reverse**, and the strongest effects come from youth-reported measures. That pattern fits either "perception is the mechanism" or "shared-method inflation"; the data cannot separate the two. [Inference]
- **Trade-offs that a single care score would hide.** In South Korean youth, achievement-oriented control co-occurs with higher grades *and* more depressive and antisocial symptoms (Zhang et al. 2023). Helicopter parenting co-occurs with affection *and* depressive symptoms (Lee & Kang 2018). Conditional regard yields enactment *and* resentment (Assor et al. 2004). Netting these into one score would treat compliance or achievement as "care success" and miss the autonomy cost. [Inference]
- **Chao's training thesis, evaluated.** It holds as a descriptive and interpretive claim: practices and meanings differ, and the benefits of authoritative parenting are weaker for first-generation Chinese Americans. It fails as a claim that controlling or harsh "training" is benign or advantageous for Chinese-heritage youth (Kim et al. 2013; Pinquart & Kauser 2018; Wang et al. 2007). Grolnick & Pomerantz's (2009) control-vs-structure distinction offers a reconciliation: what is culturally valued may be *structure*, while *pressure and intrusion* remain harmful across cultures. [Inference, Moderate]
- **Bidirectionality limits causal claims.** Child effects can dominate (Lansford et al. 2018), and controlled longitudinal effects of physical punishment are small (Ferguson 2013). Parent "care delivery" is partly a *response* to child behaviour, so models should allow feedback loops from receiver to giver.

### Gaps
- Few experimental tests (RCTs reducing psychological control or conditional regard with child outcomes) were located. Causal magnitudes are uncertain given bidirectionality.
- No Korean validations found for conditional-regard or parentification scales. The Korean-language PCDS, DAPC and K-POS-2 validation papers were seen only via database records.
- Kwon et al. (2016), the Korean helicopter-parenting study, and Deater-Deckard et al. (1996) were not read beyond title. Barber et al.'s (2005) country-level effects were not retrieved.
- Pinquart & Kauser's finding that authoritarian associations are *weaker* in more individualist countries needs checking in full text before use, because it runs opposite to the normativeness prediction.

## Case E — Good intentions, harmful outcomes: when do benevolent processes (forgiveness, positive construal, kindness, accommodation, protection) cause harm?

### Takeaway
Longitudinal and some experimental evidence shows that ostensibly positive processes help in healthy contexts but maintain or worsen problems in troubled or unsafe ones. These processes are forgiveness, benevolent attributions, avoiding negative behaviour, accommodating a loved one's anxiety or compulsions, and protective buffering or overprotection of an ill partner. The strongest causal anchor is a randomized non-inferiority trial: teaching parents to *reduce* accommodation of a child's anxiety was as effective as child CBT. Caveats: McNulty's contextual findings come largely from one research programme with US newlywed samples of modest size, and the forgiveness–aggression link is correlational, so its direction is ambiguous.

### Cited Findings

No Korean validations checked.

### Inferences
- **Care has context-dependent sign.** The same caring act (forgiving, construing benevolently, accommodating, protecting) is beneficial or harmful depending on problem severity, the partner's safety signals, or the receiver's distress. In model terms, assigning a fixed positive weight to "care expressed" is mis-specified. The weight would need to be a function of context, including safety. [Inference]
- **Perceived success ≠ actual benefit.** In Overall et al. (2009), the approach that *felt* successful produced no change, while the approach that felt unsuccessful worked. Perceived care or success is not a valid endpoint even in non-abusive couples. [Inference]
- **Case E links to Case C.** In unsafe relationships, forgiveness is associated with continued aggression (McNulty 2011) and intentions to return (Gordon et al. 2004). It erodes self-respect when the offender gives no safety signal (Luchies et al. 2010). The receiver's "love-preserving" responses can therefore lock in harm. Correlational designs limit this conclusion, except for Luchies's experiments.
- **The accommodation literature contains the cleanest causal evidence that well-intended care can sustain a problem** (SPACE RCT). Even here, baseline accommodation did not predict treatment response (Hermida-Barros 2024), and the association is much smaller with child-reported measures. The "enabling" story is real but should not be over-generalized.

### Gaps
- Independent replications of McNulty's contextual effects outside US newlywed samples, and in Korean or other non-WEIRD samples, were not found.
- No experiment isolates accommodation reduction from the full SPACE package.
- "Enabling" and codependency in addiction contexts: no robust meta-analytic evidence was located in this search, and construct validity is contested. Not reviewed.
- The book commonly cited for this theme, Grolnick (2003), *The Psychology of Parental Control: How Well-Meant Parenting Backfires* (Erlbaum), was **not verified** in this session (UNVERIFIED; no DOI checked).

## Case F — Unsustainable needs: when can one person's needs be unsustainable for the other, and is a child responsible for providing equivalent care to a parent?

### Takeaway
Communal care is reliably linked to *relationship* benefits for both partners. It is linked to the giver's *personal* well-being only when it is mitigated by self-concern. Unmitigated, avoidance-motivated, suppressive or chronically burdensome caregiving carries costs **for the giver** that dyad-level satisfaction can mask. Unmitigated communion can even co-develop with stable relationship satisfaction. On children: parentification predicts adult psychopathology only weakly (r = .14). The harm appears tied to **developmental inappropriateness and unfairness**, not to helping per se. Moderate, fair, culturally normative family assistance is often neutral or beneficial. This supports an "asymmetric communal" principle (children are not responsible for equivalent care) on normative and empirical grounds, but not a blanket claim that child caregiving is harmful.

### Inferences
- **"Unsustainable needs" shows up mainly as giver-side cost**, not as direct measures of a mismatch between need and capacity. The evidence: unmitigated communion → self-neglect and distress; avoidance-motivated or suppressed sacrifice → lower well-being; strained caregiving → depression and mortality risk; excessive reassurance seeking → partner rejection (r = .14), which is the most direct "need exceeds supply" signal. [Inference]
- **Dyad-level indices hide asymmetric costs.** Unmitigated communion helps relationship well-being but not the giver's personal well-being (Le et al. 2018), and it even slows declines in relationship satisfaction (Horne et al. 2020). A care model scored on "relationship satisfaction" or "love received" would rate a self-neglecting giver's relationship as *healthy*. Giver well-being has to be measured separately. [Inference]
- **The asymmetry principle (a child is not responsible for equivalent care to a parent)** is supported by:
  - (a) communal theory's asymmetric-communal norm [Theoretical];
  - (b) parentification's small but reliable link to later psychopathology (Hooper 2011);
  - (c) evidence that harm tracks **unfairness and developmental inappropriateness**, not helping as such (Kuperminc et al. 2009; Telzer & Fuligni 2009; Dariotis et al. 2023).

  The Korean repayment-expectation findings (Choe et al. 2026) suggest that reciprocity demands on children can operate *through* psychological control, which connects Case F to Case D. [Preliminary]
- **Disconfirming nuance:** caregiving is not uniformly harmful. Population studies show lower mortality among caregivers overall (Roth et al. 2015), and daily family assistance is often beneficial. A constraint such as "no asymmetric load" would be too blunt. The evidence favours thresholds on **strain, fairness and developmental appropriateness**. [Inference]

- No validated dyadic measure of "need load vs care capacity" in couples was found. Parental burnout literature was not reviewed in depth.
- Clark & Mills (1979) sample details and the 2012 chapter content (the asymmetric communal norm) were not retrieved — verify before quoting.
- Korean validations of unmitigated-communion, communal-strength and parentification measures were not found, and a Korean Zarit Burden Interview was not verified.

## Power and dependence: how does power asymmetry shape perception, responsiveness, and who adapts to whom?

### Takeaway
Interdependence and exchange theory define power through **dependence**: B's dependence on A is A's power over B, and the less involved or less dependent partner holds more control. Empirically, power asymmetry shapes several things:
- **who adapts**: lower-power partners sacrifice more;
- **whose perspective is taken**: power reduces perspective-taking and empathic accuracy in self-focused people, although meta-analytically power relates weakly to *greater* interpersonal accuracy, so the field is contested;
- **how care is perceived**: higher relationship power weakens the path from gratitude to perceived partner responsiveness;
- **aggression**: men with low relationship power, or hostile-sexist *biased perceptions* of low power, respond more aggressively.

*Experienced* power predicts relationship quality better than *positional* power. Economic and emotional dependence is a core mechanism of staying in abusive relationships (Case C2).

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Power sets the direction of adaptation.** Lower-power partners sacrifice more (Righetti et al. 2015). Dependent partners shift their standards (Emerson's "cost reduction"; Arriaga et al. 2016, which shows this for committed partners). So in asymmetric relationships the low-power partner does more of the "translating". A model of care transmission needs **power and dependence as structural parameters** acting on both expression and perception; they cannot be absorbed into "amount of love." [Inference]
- **The perception stage is power-dependent.** Perceived responsiveness itself varies with power (Jin et al. 2024). Perceptions of power can be systematically biased (Cross et al. 2019). Power's effect on empathic accuracy depends on the holder's self- vs other-focus (Gordon & Chen 2013). [Inference, Preliminary–Moderate]
- **Balance per se may matter less than experienced power and satisfaction with it** (Körner & Schütz 2021; Drigotas et al. 1999), at least in non-abusive WEIRD couples. A "power imbalance" constraint should target *coercive* use of power (control, threat, dependence-exploitation) rather than any asymmetry. Asymmetry is normal in parent–child relationships and common in couples. [Inference]

### Gaps
- No Korean validation of the RPI was found, and power studies are overwhelmingly WEIRD or Israeli (US, NZ, Germany). The sample country of Jin et al. (2024) is unclear.
- Hall et al.'s (2015) published effect sizes were not retrieved, so the power–accuracy link remains contested.
- Few couple studies link power asymmetry directly to the *accuracy* of perceived responsiveness. Gordon & Chen (2013) is the closest.
- Thibaut & Kelley (1959) and the Simpson et al. (2015) chapter content were not reviewed.

## Synthesis: can the perception stage of care "transmission" be captured, biased or exploited, what does that mean for models treating perceived love as the endpoint, and should safety, autonomy, boundaries and power be independent constraints rather than weighted variables inside a "care" score?

### Takeaway
Evidence from independent literatures shows the perception stage is **endogenous and capturable**:
- commitment and dependence bias appraisals of aggression;
- cultural scripts re-code jealousy and control as love;
- self-interested actors manufacture early warmth;
- intermittency and prior vulnerability produce attachment;
- power shapes who perceives what.

Perceived love or responsiveness therefore cannot be the endpoint or sole criterion of a care model. It is at best a **biased sensor**.

For **safety** (physical/sexual violence, threats, coercive control) the evidence supports **non-compensatory** treatment. Harm persists regardless of perceived love and cultural normativeness, and it shows up on dimensions (fear, PTSD, injury) orthogonal to love ratings. For **autonomy** and **care asymmetry** the evidence supports separate, non-netted dimensions: psychological control predicts ill-being even after controlling for autonomy support, and giver costs are invisible in dyadic satisfaction. But these effects are graded, modest and context-moderated, which favours thresholds or separate dimensions rather than absolute vetoes. Whether to call these "constraints" is a modelling and normative decision that the data inform but do not settle.

### Inferences
- **Modelling implication 1 (well grounded):** perceived love or responsiveness is the output of a perception function whose inputs include the actor's behaviour *and* the receiver's commitment, dependence, cultural scripts, insecurity, and the partners' relative power. It is a **mediator and a biased sensor, not the criterion**. Any model that optimizes or validates against "love perceived" or "love received" will be gamed by exactly the processes in Cases B and C.
- **Modelling implication 2: safety as a gate [Speculative extrapolation; normative choice].** Safety needs independent sensors: act-based (CTS2/CAS-R-SF), pattern-based (PMWI dominance–isolation, CCB, SEA-12), and fear or threat. Any positive safety indicator should override a high "care" score, rather than trade off against it. The Case C evidence shows that love and commitment measures move *in the wrong direction* precisely when safety is lowest, so a weighted sum would partly cancel the danger signal.
- **Modelling implication 3: autonomy and asymmetry as separate, un-netted dimensions [Inference; Moderate support].** Psychological control and conditional regard should be measured separately from warmth and support, with domain legitimacy (Smetana) as a moderator. Giver well-being, fairness and developmental appropriateness should also be measured separately. Thresholds (e.g., clinically meaningful strain, or control over the personal domain) fit the graded evidence better than absolute vetoes.
- **Modelling implication 4: power as a structural moderator of the whole chain [Inference].** Power and dependence condition expression (who sacrifices), perception (who reinterprets; power-biased perceptions) and exit (alternatives). It belongs in the model's structure, not as one more weighted term.
- **What this means for the hypothesis.**
  - It is *supported* in that intended, expressed, perceived and received care demonstrably diverge.
  - It is **challenged** as a general account of "many relationship problems." In coercion and abuse, the core problem is neither translation nor amount of love but **control, power and safety**. The receiver's perception is often *over*-generous (capture), not under-generous. Staying is well explained by structural dependence without any translation step.
  - In parenting (Case D), harm from psychological control generalizes across cultures and appraisals and is only attenuated by them.
  - Translation-type mechanisms (legitimacy appraisal, perceived normativeness, motive attribution, context-dependent valence of benevolent acts) are best supported in Cases D and E, and even there they **moderate rather than create** harm.
  - The most falsification-relevant prediction: if translation were primary, interventions that change *appraisals* would reduce harm as much as interventions that change *behaviour*. The SPACE trial (changing parent behaviour) and the universality of psychological-control effects point the other way. No head-to-head test was found (see Gaps). [Inference]
- **Framing risk.** Treating abuse as a "translation failure" can symmetrize responsibility and pathologize victims' perceptions. The paper should treat safety, coercion and power as boundary conditions *outside* the translation model. [Inference; ethical consideration]

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares compensatory and non-compensatory functional forms, e.g., testing whether high warmth offsets psychological control across the full outcome range, beyond isolated interaction tests. Meta-analyses report unique associations, not interaction structure.
- No within-relationship study was found that simultaneously measures intended care, expressed behaviour, perceived responsiveness and harm (safety, autonomy) in abusive or controlling relationships, which is the design needed to test the "translation" model in Cases B and C.
- No head-to-head trial was found comparing appraisal-focused vs behaviour-focused interventions for controlling parenting or partner coercion.
- Cross-cultural evidence for Cases B, C and F is thin. Korean evidence exists mainly for Case D (psychological control, helicopter parenting, reciprocity demands).
