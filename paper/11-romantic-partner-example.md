# 11. Worked romantic-partner example

Consider a hypothetical couple. Partner A shows care through problem-solving and practical acts, and Partner B, under stress at work, wants to be heard. Unlike the parent–child case, reciprocity is a legitimate norm here, and both partners are givers and receivers on the same day.

**Phase 1: a support mismatch.** B describes a bad week, and A offers solutions. In observed marital interactions, emotional disclosures met with emotional support predicted perceived sensitivity, while mismatched support predicted lower satisfaction through lower perceived sensitivity ([Cutrona et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-3200.21.4.754)) [Moderate/mixed]. A's advice may also be *visible* support that signals B's inefficacy ([Bolger & Amarel 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.458)). A meanwhile quietly takes over chores, which is invisible support B never registers. On the substantive route this helps B's functioning; on the symbolic route it earns nothing ([Girme et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/fam0000453)). In the model:
- channel mismatch appears as $`S < N`$ for emotional support and $`S > N`$ for informational support, the type 3 misalignment;
- unnoticed chores appear as misses in detection, type 1;
- A's unthanked effort lowers A's own satisfaction ([Visserman et al. 2019](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550618757599)).

**Phase 2: stress and drift.** B's stress keeps rising. A, also stressed, notices B's needs less accurately and acts on them less ([Neff, Nguyen & Williamson 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220974490)), a type 4 capacity limit. Under stress, global satisfaction becomes more reactive to daily events ([Neff & Karney 2009](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015663)). B begins attributing A's advice to impatience rather than concern, and non-benign attributions predict lower satisfaction a year later ([Fincham & Bradbury 1993](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.64.3.442)). Demand and withdrawal may appear ([Schrodt, Witt & Shimkowski 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/03637751.2013.813632)). As B's sentiment drops, ambiguous acts by A are read more negatively ([Hawkins, Carrère & Gottman 2002](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1741-3737.2002.00193.x)). This is the point where translation failure and declining sentiment become hard to tell apart.

**Phase 3: three possible futures.** The model distinguishes them by their signatures:
- **Repairable translation.** Both still score high on love components and commitment, and the mismatch is specific to channel and timing. Asking explicitly helps: recipients prefer requested gifts to givers' guesses ([Gino & Flynn 2011](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2011.03.015)). Responsive support, visible or not, is beneficial ([Maisel & Gable 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2009.02388.x)). Gratitude and other-praise restart the loop ([Algoe, Kurtz & Hilaire 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1948550616651681)). The evidence that skills training *specifically* is the remedy is weak ([Rogge et al. 2013](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0034209)). Awareness, shared meaning and stress reduction may matter as much.
- **Disillusionment.** Love components and perceived responsiveness decline together, with no stable channel-specific pattern. This is the trajectory of couples headed for divorce ([Huston et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.2.237)). Better translation would not reverse it, because it is an amount deficit, type 0.
- **Incompatibility.** A has accepted a job in another city, and B's career cannot move. Both understand each other perfectly, and the feasible set $`\mathcal{F}`$ may be empty, type 5. Goal conflict harms quality and well-being whatever the level of understanding ([Gere & Schimmack 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-011-9314-2)).

The romantic case differs from the parental one in three ways. Reciprocity is a fair standard. The *weaker* partner's commitment carries divorce risk ([Schoebi, Karney & Bradbury 2012](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0026290)). And power shapes who adapts: lower-power partners sacrifice more ([Righetti et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167215579054)), so the less powerful partner tends to do more of the "translating."
