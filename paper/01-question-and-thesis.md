# 1. Executive research question and thesis

## The question has a trivial answer and a hard one

The research question asks whether a scientifically informed and partly mathematical framework can explain three things without pretending that love can be objectively measured: how care moves between people, where it becomes misaligned, and how misalignment reshapes relationships over time. Taken literally, the hypothesis "love intended ≠ love expressed ≠ love perceived ≠ love received ≠ relationship compatibility" cannot be false. No two psychological measurements correlate perfectly, so every "≠" is guaranteed by measurement error and by the general background correlation among psychological variables (the "crud factor") ([Meehl 1990](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327965pli0102_1)) [Established]. A hypothesis that cannot lose tells us nothing. The methodological literature's remedy is to rewrite verbal claims as quantitative claims about named parameters, each with a pre-registered smallest effect of interest ([Scheel et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691620966795); [Lakens, Scheel & Isager 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/2515245918770963)) [Established].

The hard version is therefore four questions. How much is lost at each link, and is the loss random or systematic? Under what conditions are the losses large? Do the losses predict relationship outcomes *beyond* the amount of love and care? And are they causes of relationship trouble or symptoms of it?

## The revised thesis

Care moves between two people through a channel that loses and distorts information in **both directions**. Each person is simultaneously a sender and a receiver, and what arrives is shaped at least as much by the receiver's own state as by the sender's signal. The losses are real, measurable and sometimes consequential, but they are usually **smaller than the effects of the amount of love and commitment and the quality of core responsiveness**, meaning attentive understanding and the absence of coldness, hostility and indifference. Translation failures matter most when:
- care is ambiguous or invisible (practical provision, sacrifice, quiet presence);
- it is expressed in "peripheral" currencies whose meaning varies across cultures, generations and families (money, sacrifice, behavioural control, verbal declarations, explicit versus implicit support);
- its kind or timing does not fit the need, especially under stress;
- the receiver's needs change faster than the giver's model of them.

These are the conditions under which "translation" earns its place as an explanation [Our hypothesis, built on Moderate/mixed evidence]. Three classes of problem are not translation problems at all, and a translation frame obscures them if applied:
1. **Incompatibility**: no shared pattern of life can meet both people's essential needs.
2. **Unsustainable demand**: meeting one person's needs would seriously harm the other.
3. **Unsafety and coercion**: violence or control, where the receiver's perception tends to be *over*-generous and the core problem is power and safety, not misunderstanding ([Arriaga, Capezza & Daly 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000035); [Puente & Cohen 2003](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167202250912)) [Moderate/mixed].

## What is original here, and what is not

The empirical findings in Sections 3–6 come from the published literature and are tagged by strength. The following propositions are this paper's own and are **untested** [Our hypothesis]. Each is introduced where it is used.
1. **A two-path decomposition of care.** A substantive path delivers benefit whether or not it is perceived. A symbolic path is the receiver's inference about how much the giver values them.
2. **A six-way typology of misalignment.** Undetected care, misconstrued care, mis-targeted care, capacity-limited care, incompatibility, and unsafe or coercive patterns. It sits alongside a seventh, non-translation cause: too little love or commitment.
3. **A visibility asymmetry.** The costs of some care, such as long working hours, are invisible while its by-products, such as absence, are highly visible. Combined with how people infer how much another values them, this predicts systematic underestimation of the giver's regard.
4. **A developmental-lag model.** A parent's model of a child's needs updates at a rate set by contact and disclosure. Lag grows when needs change quickly.
5. **Care-type-dependent giver error.** Givers overvalue costly or material provision and undervalue brief warm expression.
6. **Safety and compatibility as constraints rather than weighted variables.** Safety is treated as a strict precondition evaluated before any care score, and incompatibility as an empty set of feasible joint arrangements.
7. **A filial-piety marker for failure of the symbolic path.** When provision is not read as regard, gratitude-based (reciprocal) filial piety should weaken while duty-based (authoritarian) filial piety stays intact.
