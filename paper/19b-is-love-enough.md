# Closing question: is love enough?

The question is not well defined until "enough" is given an object. The evidence answers each version differently.

**Enough to be cared for?** No. Care requires capacity, visibility and fit. A loving parent constrained by long hours, or a loving partner too stressed to act fully on a noticed need, delivers less care ([Neff, Nguyen & Williamson 2021](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167220974490)). Conversely, people can be well cared for by those who do not love them, and they may benefit from care they do not register as support, though the evidence for this is mixed ([Bolger, Zuckerman & Kessler 2000](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.79.6.953); [Shrout et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780195380170.003.0007)).

**Enough to feel loved?** Not reliably, and the reason is more surprising than mistranslation. Feeling loved depends substantially on the receiver's own responsiveness, which people project onto their partners, although the partner's self-reported care also registers, especially for concrete acts ([Lemay, Clark & Feeney 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.5.834)). A person can feel loved where care is thin, and feel unloved where it is ample.

**Enough for a healthy relationship?** No. Love coexists with coercion, control and violence. In those relationships a committed person's own standards for what is tolerable can drift: once a partner's aggression begins, the highly committed judge it more leniently ([Arriaga, Capezza & Daly 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000035)). The receiver's judgment is a sensor that can be captured. Sincere parental love coexists with psychological control, which is linked to ill-being across cultures ([Bradshaw et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001389)).

**Enough to sustain commitment?** Closer to yes. Love, commitment and dependence are among the strongest predictors of whether non-marital romantic relationships last, and fading love distinguishes couples headed for divorce ([Le et al. 2010](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01285.x); [Huston et al. 2001](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.80.2.237)). But commitment can also persist without love, at a cost ([Hawkins & Booth 2005](https://doi.org/10.1353/sof.2005.0103)).

**Enough to build a life together, and to stay compatible over time?** No. Compatibility is a property of two lives, not of one heart. Perfect understanding cannot create a shared future that no arrangement can deliver ([Gere & Schimmack 2013](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10902-011-9314-2)).

The author's candidate conclusion was that love is an internal experience whose relational consequences depend on how it becomes behaviour, how that behaviour is interpreted, and whether it supports the needs, autonomy and safety of the people involved. That is close to what the evidence permits, but it needs three corrections:
- **It understates love.** The amount of love and commitment is not a background constant behind the translation process. One's own love and commitment are among the strongest predictors of whether romantic relationships last, and the receiver's own responsiveness partly *shapes* the interpretation the formulation treats as separate.
- **It is one-directional.** Both people are senders and receivers at once. A giver whose care goes unseen may be harmed too.
- **It places safety and compatibility inside the translation chain**, when they are constraints outside it. No translation can make an unsafe relationship safe or an infeasible life feasible.

The strongest conclusion the evidence supports is this:

**Love, as a durable motivation to promote another's welfare together with felt attachment, is among the strongest predictors of whether romantic relationships survive, and it is plausibly close to necessary for the relationships people most want. It is never sufficient. What it achieves depends on whether it can be enacted, whether its acts are seen and read as regard, whether those acts fit what the other needs now, and whether the two lives can fit together at all. All of this holds only within limits that love cannot license: safety, autonomy, and, for children, freedom from any debt.**

Love can be lost in translation. It can also be weakened at its source, distorted by the reader's own state, or confronted with problems that are not about translation at all. Which of these happens more often is unknown, because no study has yet measured those causes against translation head-to-head.
