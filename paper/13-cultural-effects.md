# 13. Cultural effects

## The pattern across studies: same rules, different levels and signals

Across the cross-cultural studies reviewed, the recurring pattern is **similar associations with different levels**. Psychological control predicts depressive symptoms similarly in Belgium and South Korea ([Soenens et al. 2012](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.adolescence.2011.05.001)) and across world regions ([Bradshaw et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0001389)). Perceived emotional support predicts lower distress in four countries alike ([Chentsova Dutton, Choi & Choi 2020](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2020.00353)). Perceived rejection predicts problems in nine countries ([Putnick et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/jcpp.12366)) [Established to Moderate]. Korean distinctiveness is better located in *levels* and *structural conditions*, such as hours, education spending, old-age poverty and rapid normative change, than in different rules linking care to well-being.

A refined version of the hypothesis is consistent with the cultural evidence [Our hypothesis, extending an inference in the family literature]:
- **Peripheral, culturally variable signals of care** (money, sacrifice, behavioural control, verbal declarations, explicit versus implicit support) are where translation varies.
- **Core acceptance cues** (attentive responsiveness and the absence of coldness, hostility and indifference) are largely shared and drive most outcome variance.

## Stereotypes fail, so averages must never describe individuals

Cultural stereotypes about care are unreliable. Using culture-specific items and response-set adjustment, Japanese respondents valued *directly verbalizing* feelings to mother and father more than Americans did, the opposite of the stereotype ([Seki, Matsumoto & Imahori 2002](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022102033003006)). A meta-analysis found European Americans were *not* less collectivistic than Japanese or Koreans ([Oyserman, Coon & Kemmelmeier 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.128.1.3)). Of 35 Japan–US comparisons, only five supported the textbook view ([Takano & Osaka 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/ajsp.12322)). Countries agree more than they disagree on value priorities ([Fischer & Schwartz 2011](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022022110381429)) [Established to Moderate].

Likert self-reports are distorted by **reference-group effects**, because people rate themselves relative to those around them ([Heine et al. 2002](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.82.6.903)). Cross-cultural mean comparisons therefore need measurement invariance, or a defensible alternative such as alignment ([Asparouhov & Muthén 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2014.919210); [Robitzsch & Lüdtke 2023](https://doi.org/10.1080/10705511.2023.2191292)). Most studies cited here did not report it [Established as a methodological requirement].

The research base is also structurally skewed toward WEIRD samples ([Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan 2010](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X0999152X)). Variation *within* Korea is large and documented: by class, with private-education spending of 662,000 versus 192,000 won per month ([Newsis, 12 Mar 2026](https://www.newsis.com/view/NISX20260312_0003545284)); by firm size, with about 68% of fathers taking parental leave working in large firms ([NewsTomato, 2025](https://www.newstomato.com/ReadNews.aspx?no=1285189)); by cohort; and by earner type. Variation by child gender, daughters versus sons, is almost undocumented.

The brief's caution is therefore well founded. A Korean average cannot describe a Korean father, and the same "control" may be structure in one family and intrusion in another. One proposal reconciles the cultural debates. The term "control" would be reserved for pressure and intrusion, and "**structure**" would describe guidance ([Grolnick & Pomerantz 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1750-8606.2009.00099.x)) [Theoretical]. On this reading, what cultures value differently is often structure, while pressure and intrusion appear harmful across cultures. A new Korean instrument separates culturally variable *intrusiveness* from *emotional manipulation* ([Choe et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1177/10731911261440140)) [Preliminary].
