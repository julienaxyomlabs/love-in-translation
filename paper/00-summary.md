# Can Love Be Lost in Translation?

*A critical evidence review and formal framework for how care moves between people, where it becomes misaligned, and how misalignment changes relationships over time*

The strongest available evidence supports the first half of the hypothesis and revises the second. Intended, expressed, perceived and received care can be separated empirically, and the gaps between them can be measured. Partners agree only moderately on whether a supportive act even happened (**κ ≈ .39**). **About half of romantic sacrifices go unnoticed**, and about half of the sacrifices people "see" were not reported by the partner. Received and perceived support share only **about 12% of their variance** (r ≈ .35). But the claim that problems arise "often from the translation of love rather than its amount" does not survive the best-powered data. **Love, commitment and perceived partner commitment are among the strongest predictors of whether relationships last.** Channel-specific "matching", the love-language idea, adds little beyond the overall level and breadth of care. Much of what a person perceives is produced by the perceiver's own love, mood and attachment rather than decoded from the partner's behaviour, so "translation" and "amount" are partly the same variable seen from two sides. The defensible framework treats care as passing through a lossy, two-way channel shaped by the perceiver. Care travels by two routes. On the *substantive* route, needs are met whether or not anyone notices. On the *symbolic* route, the receiver infers how much they are valued. Translation failures are large under specific conditions:
- care that is ambiguous or invisible;
- culturally and generationally variable "currencies" of care, such as money and sacrifice;
- the wrong kind or timing of support;
- capacity limited by stress;
- needs that change faster than the giver's model of them.

The framework can be written mathematically without measuring love itself. It requires need and supply measured on the same scale, response-surface and truth-and-bias models, and randomized designs within dyads. Cosine similarities, importance-weighted scores and single relationship indices will not do. Three things fall outside any translation model and must be modelled as separate constraints: incompatible life goals, unsustainable demands on a giver, and safety, coercion and autonomy. Where safety is at stake, perceived love is a biased and sometimes *captured* sensor. Love is rarely the whole problem and never the whole solution. It matters a great deal, but it does not by itself make care received, make it fit, or make a relationship safe.

## Evidence tags

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[Established]** | Meta-analytic or multiply replicated finding with a consistent direction |
| **[Moderate/mixed]** | Several studies or one strong design, with notable inconsistency, small samples or single-laboratory dependence |
| **[Preliminary]** | Single study, small or convenience sample, conference abstract, preprint, qualitative work, or a finding verified only through a secondary source |
| **[Theoretical]** | A proposition from an existing theory or review that has not itself been directly tested |
| **[Our hypothesis]** | An original, speculative proposition of this paper. It is untested and should be read as a candidate for testing, not a finding |
| **[Assumption]** | A mathematical or measurement assumption adopted to build the formal model |

Unless a passage says otherwise, findings are associations, not demonstrated causes. Most samples come from Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic (WEIRD) societies, and this is flagged where it matters. Years are print years. Cultural and national averages describe populations, never particular families or individuals. Nothing in this paper describes the specific father and daughter the author observed in Korea.
