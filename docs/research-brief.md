# Research brief

The brief that defined this project. The paper refers to it as "the brief". The hypothesis, the motivating example and the final question keep their original wording; the methodological sections are condensed.

## Role

Act as an interdisciplinary research partner combining relationship psychology, social and cultural psychology, family and developmental psychology, behavioral science, mathematical modeling, dynamical systems, philosophy of science and research methodology.

Investigate a personal hypothesis as deeply and rigorously as possible. Do not attempt to validate the intuition; try to falsify it. Do not optimize for an elegant equation, a popular explanation or an emotionally satisfying conclusion. Follow the strongest available evidence, even if it contradicts the original theory.

## 1. The original hypothesis

> Love intended ≠ Love expressed ≠ Love perceived ≠ Love received ≠ Relationship compatibility.

Two people can genuinely love each other while experiencing the relationship in completely different ways. This may happen because intention, expression, perception, expectations, personal needs, upbringing, culture, attachment, history and compatibility are not perfectly aligned. The problem may not be the amount of love someone feels or gives, but the translation of love from one person's internal experience into another person's experienced reality. This is only a hypothesis.

**Central research question.** Can we construct a scientifically informed mathematical framework explaining how care moves between people, where it becomes misaligned, and how that misalignment changes relationships over time — without pretending that love itself can be objectively measured?

## 2. The motivating example

A father grew up believing that financial provision is one of the highest expressions of love. He works, sacrifices time, provides security and ensures that his daughter has material opportunities. He sincerely believes: *"I gave you everything."* His daughter places greater importance on emotional support, empathy, quality time, encouragement or being understood. She sincerely believes: *"You were never there."*

Both statements could be psychologically sincere. Possible explanations include differences in needs, differences in beliefs about love, different interpretations of the same behaviors, cultural and generational expectations, limited communication, misperception of intentions, inadequate responsiveness, conflicting responsibilities and historical relationship experiences.

Versions of this situation were observed in Korea. Do not generalize those observations to Korean families: treat Korea as a case study requiring empirical evidence and comparison with other cultures. Distinguish parental responsibilities from romantic reciprocity; a child is not responsible for providing equivalent care to a parent.

## 3. Literature review before modeling

- **Interpersonal processes:** perceived partner responsiveness, intimacy process models, self-disclosure, emotional validation, perceived understanding, communication, attribution and interpretation, empathic accuracy, support-seeking and support-giving, invisible support, support matching, sacrifice and perceived sacrifice, conflict repair.
- **Individual differences:** attachment, personality, emotional regulation, upbringing, expectations, relationship histories, current needs, attribution biases, perception biases, psychological well-being.
- **Relationship outcomes:** satisfaction, need fulfillment, compatibility, commitment, trust, stability, shared values, adaptation, emotional and physical safety, autonomy and boundaries.
- **Family and culture:** parent–child relationships, intergenerational support, financial provision, instrumental and emotional support, filial obligation, cultural definitions of care, changing family norms, cross-cultural research.
- **Love languages:** critically examine the evidence for and against the Five Love Languages. Do not base the project on popular love-language categories. Investigate whether responsiveness, need fulfillment, empathy and interpretation offer stronger explanatory constructs — without assuming they do.

**Evidence standards.** Prioritize peer-reviewed studies, meta-analyses, systematic reviews, longitudinal studies, validated measures and foundational theories. Distinguish established findings, moderate or mixed evidence, preliminary findings, theoretical propositions and our own speculative hypotheses. Cite a verifiable source with a link or DOI for every substantial empirical claim; never invent citations. Distinguish what studies measured from what their authors inferred, and examine sample sizes, design, cultural limitations and causal identification.

## 4. Challenge the fundamental assumptions

Is love definable as one construct? Are intentions observable or only inferable? Is expressed love always recognizable? Can someone perceive love without receiving appropriate care, or receive meaningful care without perceiving loving intentions? Is need fulfillment equivalent to being loved? Does feeling understood imply compatibility? Can high responsiveness coexist with manipulation? Can stability exist without love, or love without healthy functioning? Identify exactly which concepts must remain separate; do not collapse them into an overall relationship score.

## 5. Dynamic transmission model

Start from the candidate process

`INTERNAL STATE → INTENTION → BEHAVIOR → OBSERVABLE SIGNAL → PERCEPTION → INTERPRETATION → NEED RELEVANCE → EXPERIENCED RESPONSIVENESS → RELATIONSHIP OUTCOME → FEEDBACK`

and test whether every stage is justified, which are described by established theories, which are redundant or unobservable, and where misalignment arises. Consider alternative causal orders, and model two people influencing each other over time rather than a permanent giver and receiver.

## 6. Mathematical framework

Explore vectors of expressed behavior and perceived needs, time-varying weights, weighted similarity, dot products, cosine similarity, mismatch functions, asymmetric interaction matrices, Bayesian updating, latent variables, state-space models, dynamical systems, feedback loops, thresholds, multiplicative bottlenecks, actor–partner interdependence and network models. Address why cosine similarity may mislead when magnitude matters, whether care dimensions are independent, whether more care is always better, how unwanted care becomes intrusive, unmet versus incompatible needs, asymmetry, context-dependent interpretation, the role of trust, safety as a constraint, explanatory versus predictive equations, and identifiability. For every component, state the construct, its measurement, why the operation fits, its assumptions, its empirical support and what remains speculative. Never invent numerical coefficients; reject unjustified mathematics.

## 7. Asymmetry and misperception

Person A may sincerely believe they provided 90/100 of the care needed while Person B sincerely reports 40/100 (illustrative numbers). Investigate actor–observer discrepancies, perceived versus enacted support, overestimated sacrifice, inaccurate beliefs about needs, perceived responsiveness, empathic accuracy, misread intentions, and retrospective versus momentary reports. How can these discrepancies be measured, and can disagreement about facts be separated from disagreement about meaning?

## 8. Time and adaptation

Needs, expectations and trust evolve; strategies adapt; communication shapes future behavior; experience shapes future perception; mismatches accumulate; repair reverses some damage but not necessarily all. Use Bayesian updating and dynamical systems only where justified, and say whether change happens within individuals, between them, or through their interaction.

## 9. Culture and the Korean example

Examine how culture, economic conditions and generational expectations shape what care should look like, what people do, which signals read as caring, what people feel entitled to expect, how unmet needs are communicated and how family roles distribute responsibility. Investigate Korean family research, compare with other countries, and avoid stereotypes and ecological fallacies.

## 10. Stress tests

Break the model with: (A) perfect understanding, incompatible lives; (B) manipulative responsiveness; (C) love and abuse; (D) parental love and violated autonomy; (E) good intentions, harmful outcomes; (F) unsustainable needs; (G) different but successful expressions; (H) stability without strong romantic love. Treat safety, coercion, autonomy, boundaries and power as independently important.

## 11. Empirical validation

Consider diaries, experience sampling, longitudinal couple and parent–child studies, observation, validated scales, actor–partner models, cross-lagged designs and their limits, multilevel models, measurement invariance and ethical experiments. Propose at least one feasible study with hypotheses and rivals, population, measures, methods, confounders, ethics and falsification criteria. Distinguish correlation, prediction and causation.

## 12. Deliverable

A substantial research paper with twenty sections — from the executive question and thesis to a bibliography with DOIs — including a model diagram, an evidence-quality table, a construct-to-measurement table, a notation glossary, a comparison of alternative formulations and a visible separation of established science from original theorizing. Do not manufacture precision or present a conceptual equation as a validated law.

## 13. Final question

Is love enough — and is that even a well-defined question? Enough for what: being cared for, feeling loved, a healthy relationship, sustained commitment, building a life together, staying compatible over time? Give the strongest explanation the evidence permits, not the preferred conclusion.
